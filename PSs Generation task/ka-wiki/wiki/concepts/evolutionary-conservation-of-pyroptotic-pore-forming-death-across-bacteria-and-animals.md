# Evolutionary conservation of pyroptotic pore-forming death across bacteria and animals

Pyroptotic pore-forming death is presented in the source as a regulated cell-death mechanism shared between animals and bacteria through the gasdermin protein family. In mammals, gasdermins form membrane pores that release inflammatory cytokines and induce lytic cell death, whereas the reported bacterial gasdermins likewise execute cell death through pore formation after proteolytic activation. The study concludes that pyroptosis is an ancient form of regulated cell death shared between bacteria and animals. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:20-29, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:62-71]

## Overview

In mammals, [[gasdermins]] are activated when caspases or granzymes cleave an inter-domain linker, freeing a lipophilic N-terminal domain from an inhibitory C-terminal domain. The liberated N-terminal domain oligomerizes into membrane pores that are important for innate immunity. The source identifies this as the established mechanism for mammalian pyroptotic death. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:62-69]

The study reports that bacteria encode homologs of mammalian gasdermins. Sequence analysis identified 50 bacterial gasdermins that form a clade distinct from eukaryotic homologs, and structural analysis showed that bacterial gasdermins share the overall architecture of the mammalian gasdermin N-terminal pore-forming domain. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:72-82]

## Conserved structural logic

Crystal structures of bacterial gasdermins from *Bradyrhizobium tropiciagri* and *Vitiosangium* showed notable homology to the mammalian gasdermin N-terminal domain, including a conserved twisted central anti-parallel beta sheet and similar placement of surrounding helices and strands. These structures indicate conservation of the core pore-forming architecture despite divergence between bacterial and eukaryotic lineages. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:75-82]

At the same time, the source describes a difference in how the inactive state is maintained. Bacterial gasdermins lack the large alpha-helical inhibitory C-terminal domain found in mammalian gasdermins. Instead, they use a short C-terminal peptide that wraps around the beta-sheet core and stabilizes the inactive conformation. Truncation of this peptide caused arrested cell growth, supporting its autoinhibitory role. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:83-94, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:199-206]

The source also identifies a conserved lipid-related feature in bacterial gasdermins. In the *Bradyrhizobium* structure, a palmitoyl thioester attached to an N-terminal cysteine occupies a hydrophobic tunnel, and this cysteine is conserved across most bacterial gasdermins and some fungal homologs. Palmitoylation contributed to stability of the inactive state and is proposed as a conserved mechanism for regulating gasdermin pores. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:95-106, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:279-280]

## Conserved activation by proteolysis

The source reports that most bacterial gasdermins are encoded next to genes with predicted protease domains, most commonly caspase-like peptidases. Specifically, 43 of 50 bacterial gasdermins were genomically associated with one or more protease genes, including peptidase C14 and CHAT family proteases. This genomic organization parallels the proteolytic control of gasdermins in animals. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:119-128]

Experimental work on a [[Runella]] system showed that bacterial gasdermin toxicity required its associated caspase-like protease. Mutation of predicted protease active-site residues abolished toxicity, the gasdermin and protease were toxic only when expressed together, and purified components showed specific cleavage of the gasdermin. The cleavage site was mapped to a loop immediately preceding the C-terminal peptide, and mutations in key cleavage-site residues eliminated cleavage and toxicity. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:139-171, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:207-217]

The authors explicitly interpret these findings as showing that, like mammalian homologs, bacterial gasdermins are cell-death effectors activated by proteolytic cleavage. In both systems, cleavage releases inhibition of the pore-forming portion of the protein. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:217-217, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:270-277]

## Conserved pore formation and membrane disruption

Once activated, bacterial gasdermins behaved as membrane-disrupting pore formers. In cells, N-terminal GFP-tagged bacterial gasdermin formed membrane-associated puncta when co-expressed with its protease, and transmission electron microscopy showed disrupted membrane integrity. In vitro, cleaved bacterial gasdermins permeabilized liposomes and rapidly released their contents, while mutations in the protease active site or gasdermin cleavage site abolished liposome permeabilization. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:218-239]

Electron microscopy further showed that bacterial gasdermins assemble into ring-like membrane pores. For the *Runella* gasdermin, pores observed in liposomes had inner diameters of about 200 to 300 angstroms by cryo-EM and about 240 to 330 angstroms within liposomes, somewhat larger than the 135 to 215 angstrom mammalian gasdermin pores cited in the paper. A metagenomic *Bacteroidetes* gasdermin formed smaller pores, indicating heterogeneity in bacterial pore architecture. Cryo-electron tomography confirmed that these pores span membranes and disrupt membrane integrity. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:240-269]

## Functional context in bacteria and animals

In animals, the source frames gasdermin pore formation as important for [[innate immunity]], inflammatory cytokine release, host defense against pathogens, and cancer-related defense. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:20-22, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:68-69]

In bacteria, gasdermin systems were linked to anti-phage defense. The study found that a four-gene operon from *Lysobacter enzymogenes* conferred defense against coliphages T4, T5, and T6, and deletion of the bacterial gasdermin abolished protection. More broadly, some bacterial gasdermin-protease systems caused cellular toxicity, loss of membrane integrity, and cessation of division when expressed in *E. coli*, consistent with a cell-death-based defense strategy. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:129-138, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:139-157]

The associated bacterial proteases were frequently fused to NACHT or repeat domains that are described as being involved in pathogen recognition and [[inflammasome]] function in human innate immunity. The authors therefore propose a broader parallel in which foreign pathogen recognition may trigger gasdermin cleavage in bacteria, similar to inflammasome-linked initiation of gasdermin activation in mammals. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:125-128, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:270-275]

## Significance

The central conclusion of the study is that the pore-forming logic of [[pyroptosis]] predates the divergence of bacteria and animals. The shared features highlighted by the source include homologous pore-forming gasdermin domains, proteolytic activation, release of autoinhibition, assembly of large membrane pores, and membrane-rupturing cell death. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:23-29, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:270-285]
