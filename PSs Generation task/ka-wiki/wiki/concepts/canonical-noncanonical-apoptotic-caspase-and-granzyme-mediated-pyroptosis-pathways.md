# Canonical, noncanonical, apoptotic-caspase, and granzyme-mediated pyroptosis pathways

[[Pyroptosis]] is an inflammatory form of programmed cell death that depends on gasdermin-family pore formation and is closely linked to inflammasome signaling. In the reviewed framework, four main pyroptosis routes are distinguished: the canonical caspase-1-dependent pathway, the noncanonical caspase-4/5/11-dependent pathway, apoptotic-caspase-mediated pathways involving caspase-3 or caspase-8, and granzyme-mediated pathways driven by cytotoxic lymphocytes. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:223-250]

These pathways converge on cleavage of [[Gasdermin D|gasdermins]] or related gasdermin-family proteins, generating N-terminal fragments that oligomerize in the plasma membrane and form pores. The downstream result is cell swelling, membrane rupture, and release of inflammatory mediators such as [[IL-1beta]], [[IL-18]], and [[HMGB1]]. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:225-231, pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:419-444]

## Shared framework

Pyroptosis is typically initiated when pattern-recognition receptors detect [[PAMPs]] or [[DAMPs]], although some pathways can also be triggered more directly by inflammatory substances, chemotherapeutic drugs, [[TNF-alpha]], or granzymes from immune effector cells. The source specifically notes that lipopolysaccharide can drive a noncanonical pathway, chemotherapeutic drugs can activate caspase-3-dependent pyroptosis, TNF-alpha can activate caspase-8-dependent pyroptosis in tumor cells, and granzyme enters target cells through perforin after release from [[cytotoxic T lymphocytes]] and [[natural killer cells]]. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:201-222]

At the execution stage, the cleaved gasdermin N-terminus translocates to the plasma membrane, binds membrane lipids, oligomerizes, and forms pores. These pores are large enough to allow passage of IL-1beta, IL-18, and other cellular proteins, and they are associated with ionic flux, mitochondrial depolarization, lysosome leakage, cell swelling, and eventual membrane rupture. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:419-444]

## Canonical pyroptosis pathway

The canonical pathway is the caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis route. It requires activation of canonical [[inflammasomes]], which are multimolecular complexes composed of pro-caspase-1, [[ASC]], and pattern-recognition receptors. The review identifies major receptor components including [[AIM2]], [[NLRP1]], [[NLRP3]], [[NLRC4]], and [[Pyrin]]. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:252-264]

This pathway begins when PRRs detect DAMPs or PAMPs and oligomerize after a conformational change. Some CARD-containing PRRs, such as NLRC4 and NLRP1B, can recruit pro-caspase-1 directly through CARD-CARD interactions, while PRRs lacking CARD first recruit ASC, which bridges PYD-containing sensors such as NLRP3, AIM2, and pyrin to the CARD domain of pro-caspase-1. Inflammasome assembly brings pro-caspase-1 into proximity and promotes its activation by autoproteolysis. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:265-291]

Activated caspase-1 processes pro-IL-18 and pro-IL-1 into mature cytokines and cleaves GSDMD to generate the pore-forming N-terminal fragment. The resulting pores drive salt and water influx, cell swelling, and release of mature IL-1beta, IL-18, and HMGB1. The review also notes that caspase-1 can cleave GSDMB at position 236 in a canonical pyroptotic route, generating N-terminal fragments that oligomerize and form membrane pores. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:292-305]

## Noncanonical pyroptosis pathway

The noncanonical inflammasome pathway is centered on caspase-4, caspase-5, or caspase-11 rather than caspase-1. In this route, activation is triggered directly by inflammatory substances such as intracellular [[LPS]] and does not require a conventional inflammasome complex for the initial sensing step. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:306-323]

The review states that interferon and LPS induce interferon-inducible guanylate-binding proteins through interferon receptor and TLR4/MD-2 signaling, and that these GBPs can promote vacuolar rupture, allowing LPS to access the cytoplasm. Human caspase-4 and caspase-5, and mouse caspase-11, bind intracellular LPS and lipid A through their CARD domains. Their activation then provokes GSDMD cleavage and pyroptosis. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:314-323]

Unlike the canonical pathway, the noncanonical route does not directly mature pro-IL-18 and pro-IL-1beta. Instead, it can indirectly promote IL-18 and IL-1beta production by activating [[NLRP3]] and caspase-1 secondarily. The source describes several linked events, including pannexin-1 cleavage, ATP release, P2X7 activation, potassium efflux, and NLRP3/caspase-1 activation. It also notes that caspase-4-mediated potassium efflux can sufficiently activate NLRP3 and accelerate IL-1beta generation. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:324-335]

The review further describes a role for GSDMB in this pathway: GSDMB can directly interact with the CARD domain of caspase-4, progressively increase caspase-4 activity, promote GSDMD cleavage, and associate with increased LDH release. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:335-340]

## Apoptotic-caspase-mediated pyroptosis pathways

In addition to inflammatory caspases, pyroptosis can be driven by apoptotic caspases, especially caspase-3 and caspase-8. The review presents these routes as newer additions to the pyroptosis framework that expand it beyond the traditional inflammasome-centered model. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:243-250, pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:341-365]

In the caspase-3 pathway, active caspase-3 cleaves [[GSDME]]/DFNA5 to generate a pore-forming GSDME N-terminus that punctures the plasma membrane and induces pyroptosis. The source notes that chemotherapeutic drugs including doxorubicin, cisplatin, and actinomycin D can activate caspase-3 and trigger pyroptosis through GSDME cleavage. Although this route does not require an inflammasome for initiation, GSDME N-termini can enable NLRP3 inflammasome activation, which further promotes the conventional pathway and increases IL-1beta and IL-18. GSDME-mediated pyroptosis is also associated with release of HMGB1 and LDH. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:343-357]

The review states that caspase-3-mediated pyroptosis and apoptosis share the same upstream signaling mechanism. In this description, chemotherapy promotes BAX/BAK translocation to the outer mitochondrial membrane, causing mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and cytochrome c release, which then activates caspase-9 and caspase-3. Whether the outcome shifts toward pyroptosis or apoptosis depends mainly on GSDME expression level: high GSDME favors pyroptosis after caspase-3 cleavage, whereas low GSDME favors apoptosis. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:361-385]

Caspase-8 can also redirect cell death toward pyroptosis. The review states that TNF-alpha activates caspase-8 in tumor cells, and that alpha-ketoglutarate promotes expression of death receptor DR6, which activates caspase-8. In this setting, pyroptosis occurs when caspase-8 cleaves [[GSDMC]] at D365 and releases its N-terminal domain. The source also notes that active caspase-8 cleaves both GSDMD and GSDME in Yersinia-infected murine macrophages, leading to pyroptosis, and that caspase-8 cleavage of GSDMC can force cancer cells into pyroptosis instead of apoptosis. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:386-397]

## Granzyme-mediated pyroptosis pathways

Granzyme-mediated pyroptosis is linked to cytotoxic lymphocyte immunity. Granzymes released by CTLs and NK cells enter target cells through perforin and were previously understood mainly as inducers of apoptosis, but the review describes them as direct inducers of pyroptosis as well. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:398-405]

In the GZMA route, granzyme A from CTLs and NK cells cleaves [[GSDMB]], generating membrane holes that trigger pyroptosis. The review states that [[IFN-gamma]] enhances this process by increasing GSDMB expression, and that GSDMB expression is required for GZMA-mediated pyroptosis. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:405-413]

In the GZMB route, granzyme B generated by NK cells induces pyroptosis in tumor target cells by cleaving GSDME at the same site targeted by caspase-3. The review also states that GZMB activates and cleaves caspase-3, reinforcing this pyroptotic mechanism. The source presents granzyme-mediated pyroptosis as a pathway with relevance for antitumor treatment and immunity. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:413-418]

## Comparison of the four pathway classes

Across these pathway classes, the main differences lie in the upstream activators and initiator proteases. Canonical pyroptosis depends on inflammasome assembly and caspase-1; noncanonical pyroptosis depends on direct sensing of intracellular LPS by caspase-4/5/11; apoptotic-caspase-mediated pyroptosis uses caspase-3 or caspase-8; and granzyme-mediated pyroptosis is driven by immune-cell-derived GZMA or GZMB delivered through perforin. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:236-250, pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:252-340, pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:341-418]

They also differ in gasdermin usage and cytokine processing. Canonical and noncanonical pathways prominently involve GSDMD, while apoptotic-caspase pathways prominently involve GSDME or GSDMC, and granzyme-mediated pathways involve GSDMB or GSDME. Canonical caspase-1 directly matures IL-1beta and IL-18, whereas the noncanonical pathway promotes those cytokines indirectly through secondary NLRP3 and caspase-1 activation. ^[pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:292-305, pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:324-340, pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:343-357, pyroptosis-and-inflammasomes-in-cancer-and-inflammation.md:386-418]
