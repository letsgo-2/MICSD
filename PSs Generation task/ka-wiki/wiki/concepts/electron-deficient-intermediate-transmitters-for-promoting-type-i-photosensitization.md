# Electron-deficient intermediate transmitters for promoting type-I photosensitization

Electron-deficient intermediate transmitters are a direct-strategy design element for [[type-I photosensitizer|type-I photosensitizers]]. In the reviewed framework for type-I [[photodynamic therapy]], they are used to enhance the [[electron transfer]] pathway that produces superoxide radical (`O2·−`) and downstream hydroxyl radical (`OH·`). The review places this approach alongside forming an electron-rich microenvironment and introducing electron-transfer-capable primitives as one of the three main direct construction methods for type-I photosensitizers. ^[the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:26-30, the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:388-423]

## Role in the direct strategy

In the direct strategy, the key objective is to strengthen the electron-transfer process rather than suppress [[type-II photosensitization|type-II]] [[excitation energy transfer]]. One of the two main electron-transfer pathways described in the review is that the excited triplet state of a photosensitizer transfers electrons to an electron-deficient substance, which then transfers electrons to molecular oxygen. In this pathway, the electron-deficient substance functions as an intermediary between the photosensitizer and `O2`, helping promote formation of `O2·−`. ^[the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:390-415]

The review states that photosensitizers tend to transfer electrons toward electron-deficient substances, so introducing electron-deficient structural intermediates can either convert a conventional type-II photosensitizer into a type-I system or increase the proportion of type-I reactive oxygen species. It divides this design logic into two categories: an electron-acceptor strategy and a molecular cationization strategy. ^[the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:542-559]

## Electron-acceptor strategy

In the electron-acceptor strategy, the electron acceptor serves as the electron-deficient intermediate transmitter. According to the review, this can shorten the distance between donor and acceptor and improve electron-transfer efficiency. In the proposed sequence, the photosensitizer transfers electrons to an acceptor with a suitable potential, and the acceptor then passes electrons to `O2`, resulting in efficient `O2·−` generation. ^[the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:548-556]

One example summarized in the review is a supramolecular system formed by co-assembling a conventional type-II photosensitizer (`D`) with three different electron acceptors (`A1`, `A2`, `A3`). These assemblies (`DA1`, `DA2`, `DA3`) all increased the fluorescence of the superoxide probe DHE, indicating effective `O2·−` production. Cyclic-voltammetry-derived potentials for `D` and `A1`, together with a calculated Gibbs free energy of `-22.2 kJ mol−1`, were used to show that intermolecular electron transfer in the `D/A1` pair was thermodynamically feasible. ^[the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:560-585]

A related example used a type-II [[BODIPY]] photosensitizer (`D`) and peralkyne diimide (`A`) as the electron-deficient intermediate transmitter to build a type-I supramolecular system. The review reports hydroxyl-radical signals by ESR and gives cyclic-voltammetry data showing an oxidation potential of `+0.702 V` for `D`, a reduction potential of `-0.997 V` for `A`, and a calculated photoinduced electron-transfer `ΔG` of `-26.05 kJ mol−1`, again supporting thermodynamic feasibility. It further describes a mechanism in which excited `D*` rapidly transfers electrons to nearby `A` to form radical ion pairs, after which `D+·` can oxidize water to generate `OH·`. ^[the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:586-602]

The review also describes an acceptor-triggered photogenerated electron-transfer system built by co-assembling PDI with `4F`. In that case, adding the electron-deficient PDI increased type-I reactive oxygen species production, with reported enhancement of `OH·` and `O2·−` generation by 3.5-fold and 2.5-fold, respectively. The proposed mechanism is that excited `4F` transfers electrons to oxygen to generate `O2·−`, while PDI donates electrons to the excited state of `4F`; the resulting cationic radical then captures electrons from surrounding water, oxidizing water to release `OH·`. ^[the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:615-635]

## Molecular cationization strategy

The second route is molecular cationization. The review states that cationization can enhance the electron-accepting capacity of the cationic portion, promote [[intersystem crossing]], and increase electron separation and transfer. It also notes that cationization can provide missing electron gaps and improve electron-transport capacity without requiring a separately introduced matching molecule. ^[the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:556-559, the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:636-640]

As evidence, the review cites cationized derivatives formed from `TBZPy` and `CTBZPy`. Compared with the non-cationized molecules, `TBZPyI` and `CTBZPyI` improved overall ROS generation and increased the proportion of `OH·`. Their reported `ΔES-T` values dropped from `0.801` to `0.215 eV` and from `0.719` to `0.204 eV`, respectively, which the authors interpret as showing that cationization strongly promotes intersystem crossing, generation of triplet states, and radical formation through electron transfer. ^[the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:641-671]

A similar trend is described for `DTPAN`, `DTPAPy`, and their cationized `PF6` salts. The review reports the ROS-generation order `DTPANPF6 > DTPAPyPF6 > DTPAPy > DTPAN ≈ control`, indicating that cationization substantially increased ROS production. It further states that cationization changed the pyridine segment from an electron donor into an electron acceptor, concentrated the LUMO in the pyridinium region, improved HOMO-LUMO separation, and reduced the `ΔES-T` gap of `DTPAPyPF6` relative to `DTPAPy`, supporting better intersystem-crossing capability. ^[the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:672-719]

## Design rationale and limitations

The rationale for electron-deficient intermediate transmitters is that they create a favorable relay for electron flow from the excited photosensitizer to oxygen, shifting the photochemistry toward type-I ROS generation. In the review’s overall summary, this design class is one of the core direct-strategy methods for increasing electron-transfer efficiency in type-I photosensitizers. ^[the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:842-849, the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:878-888]

The review also emphasizes tradeoffs. Introducing a separate electron-deficient intermediate transmitter requires a high degree of matching between the two molecular components. More broadly, electron-deficient intermediates may increase photosensitizer reactivity in ways that could promote undesirable reactions with non-target biomolecules, potentially reducing treatment accuracy or causing side effects. The authors therefore argue that more refined molecular design is needed to improve efficacy while minimizing these liabilities. ^[the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:636-640, the-current-advances-in-design-strategy-indirect-strategy-and-direct-strategy-for-typei-photosensitizers.md:863-870]
