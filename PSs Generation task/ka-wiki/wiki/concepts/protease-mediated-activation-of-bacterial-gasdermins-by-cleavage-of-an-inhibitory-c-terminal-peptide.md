# Protease-mediated activation of bacterial gasdermins by cleavage of an inhibitory C-terminal peptide

Protease-mediated activation of bacterial gasdermins refers to a mechanism in which [[bacterial gasdermins|bacterial gasdermin (bGSDM)]] proteins are kept inactive by a short inhibitory C-terminal peptide and are activated when an associated protease cleaves just before that peptide, allowing the inhibitory segment to be removed. The study describes this as an ancient gasdermin activation strategy that parallels proteolytic activation of mammalian gasdermins while using a short C-terminal peptide instead of a large C-terminal domain. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:24-29, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:83-94, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:157-171, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:270-279]

## Overview

Most identified bGSDMs in the study were encoded next to one or more protease-domain genes, and in most cases those neighboring enzymes were caspase-like peptidases, including peptidase C14 and CHAT family proteases. The associated proteases were often fused to repeat or NACHT domains that are frequently involved in pathogen recognition and inflammasome function in human innate immunity, placing bGSDM activation in a broader defense-system context. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:119-129, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:550-556]

The work focused especially on a [[Runella]] bGSDM-protease system. In vivo, toxicity required co-expression of the bGSDM with its associated protease, and mutation of the predicted protease active-site residues H796 and C840 eliminated toxicity. In a separate [[Lysobacter enzymogenes]] system, mutation of a second trypsin-like protease also abolished anti-phage defense, supporting a direct requirement for protease activity in bGSDM function. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:139-156]

## Autoinhibition by the C-terminal peptide

Structural analysis showed that bacterial gasdermins lack the large alpha-helical C-terminal domain used to autoinhibit mammalian gasdermins. Instead, bGSDMs adopt a similar inactive conformation using a short C-terminal peptide that wraps around the twisted beta-sheet core and terminates across the strand equivalent to mammalian beta2, thereby stabilizing the inactivated state. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:83-94, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:503-516]

The high-resolution [[Bradyrhizobium]] bGSDM structure explains how this peptide restrains the core. Residues F245 and F247 lie along the surface between positions equivalent to the mammalian beta9 strand and alpha1 helix, additional contacts involve N21 and the peptide backbone, F253 latches over the palmitoyl modification at C3, and the peptide terminates below the strand equivalent to beta2 with supporting hydrogen bonds from R27 and N29. Truncating the C-terminal peptide in Runella, Bradyrhizobium, or [[Vitiosangium]] bGSDM constructs caused arrested cell growth, confirming that the peptide is required for autoinhibition. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:172-206, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:624-629]

## Proteolytic cleavage mechanism

Using purified components, the authors reconstituted cleavage of Runella bGSDM by its associated protease. Co-incubation produced a specific lower-molecular-weight bGSDM species, and cleavage depended on the protease catalytic residues but not on bGSDM palmitoylation. Mass spectrometry mapped the cleavage site to after the P1 residue L247. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:157-165, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:615-624]

A 2.9 angstrom structure of Runella bGSDM showed that cleavage occurs in a loop immediately preceding the C-terminal peptide. Crystal packing additionally suggested that the peptide can dissociate from the bGSDM surface after cleavage, supporting a model in which proteolysis removes the inhibitory segment and releases autoinhibition. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:165-171, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:619-624]

Mutagenesis defined sequence requirements for activation. In vitro, cleavage required the L247 P1 position and was inhibited by mutations affecting the P1-prime glycine as well as the P4, P3, P2, and P3-prime residues. In vivo, mutations disrupting the P1 and P1-prime positions eliminated toxicity. The Runella protease also could not activate divergent bGSDMs engineered to contain the Runella cleavage loop, indicating that recognition depends on additional contacts beyond the cleavage motif itself. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:207-217]

## Consequences of activation

After activation, bGSDMs associated with membranes and disrupted membrane integrity. When GFP was fused to the N-terminus of Runella bGSDM, co-expression with the Runella protease caused the protein to coalesce into membrane-associated puncta and induced cellular toxicity. Electron microscopy of cells expressing the active system showed clear membrane disruption. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:218-227, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:642-646]

In vitro, cleaved Runella bGSDM permeabilized liposomes and rapidly released internal contents, whereas protease active-site mutations or bGSDM cleavage-site mutations abolished liposome permeabilization. These results show that proteolysis is essential for activation of pore-forming activity. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:228-239, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:647-656]

Electron microscopy and cryo-electron tomography further showed that activated bGSDMs form membrane-spanning pores. Runella bGSDM formed ring-like pores with an inner diameter of about 200 to 300 angstroms in detergent-solubilized complexes and about 240 to 330 angstroms within liposomes, while a metagenomic Bacteroidetes bGSDM formed smaller pores, indicating heterogeneity in pore architecture across bacterial gasdermins. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:240-269]

## Functional significance

The study links these activation mechanisms to defense and cell death. A four-gene operon from Lysobacter enzymogenes provided robust defense against coliphages T4, T5, and T6, and deleting the bGSDM abolished protection. More generally, expression of some bGSDM-protease systems in E. coli caused strong toxicity, and time-lapse microscopy of the Runella system showed cessation of cell division together with loss of membrane integrity. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:129-156, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:557-577]

The authors conclude that, in both bacterial and mammalian systems, proteolytic cleavage after the lipophilic N-terminal domain releases gasdermin inhibition and enables pore formation. In bacteria, this inhibition is imposed by a notably short C-terminal peptide rather than a large C-terminal domain, and the findings support the idea that pyroptosis-like cell death is an ancient, conserved strategy across life. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:270-285]
