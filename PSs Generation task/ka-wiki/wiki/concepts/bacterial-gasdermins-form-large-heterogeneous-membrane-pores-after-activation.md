# Bacterial gasdermins form large heterogeneous membrane pores after activation

[[Bacterial gasdermins|Bacterial gasdermins (bGSDMs)]] are pore-forming cell-death effectors identified in bacteria that, once activated, assemble into membrane-spanning structures that disrupt membrane integrity. In the source study, activated bGSDMs were linked to bacterial cell death, liposome permeabilization, and anti-phage defense, placing pore formation at the center of their effector function. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:23-29, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:139-148, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:218-239]

## Activation and release of autoinhibition

bGSDMs are produced in an inactive conformation despite lacking the large autoinhibitory C-terminal domain found in mammalian gasdermins. Instead, bacterial proteins use a short C-terminal peptide that wraps around the core fold and stabilizes the inactive state. In the studied systems, activation occurred through site-specific cleavage by associated [[caspase-like proteases]], which removed or released this inhibitory peptide and enabled pore-forming activity. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:83-94, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:119-128, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:157-171, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:207-217]

The paper’s Runella system illustrates this mechanism directly. Co-incubation of purified Runella bGSDM with its cognate protease generated a lower-molecular-weight cleavage product, and mass spectrometry mapped the cleavage site to after residue L247 in a loop immediately preceding the C-terminal peptide. Mutations in the protease catalytic residues or in key cleavage-site positions blocked cleavage and eliminated toxicity or membrane permeabilization, showing that proteolysis is required for activation. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:149-165, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:207-217, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:231-234]

## Membrane association and pore formation

After co-expression with the Runella protease in E. coli, N-terminally GFP-tagged Runella bGSDM formed membrane-associated puncta and induced cellular toxicity. Transmission electron microscopy of cells expressing the active system showed disrupted membrane integrity, supporting the conclusion that activated bGSDMs attack membranes in cells. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:218-227]

The same membrane-disrupting activity was reconstituted in vitro. Cleaved Runella bGSDMs permeabilized liposomes and caused rapid release of encapsulated contents, whereas protease active-site mutations or bGSDM cleavage-site mutations abolished liposome leakage. These results tie pore-forming activity directly to proteolytic activation. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:228-239]

## Large and heterogeneous pores

Electron microscopy showed that activated Runella bGSDM formed pores within liposomes and also appeared in fragmented mesh-like arrays. Cryo-EM and two-dimensional classification of detergent-solubilized complexes identified a ring-like pore with an overall width of about 50 A and an inner diameter ranging from 200 to 300 A. Measurements of pores formed within liposomes gave a similar but slightly larger range of about 240 to 330 A. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:240-250, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:642-663]

The source also reports pore-size variation across bacterial gasdermins. A bGSDM reconstituted from a metagenomic Bacteroidetes scaffold formed smaller pores of about 130 to 190 A in liposomes, indicating that pore architecture differs among bGSDMs. The authors therefore describe bacterial gasdermin pores as heterogeneous rather than uniform. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:251-266]

Cryo-electron tomography of pore-liposome assemblies confirmed that bGSDM pores span the liposomal membrane and disrupt its integrity. In the article’s summary, activation was described as inducing assembly of large and heterogeneous pores that compromise membranes, which matches the structural and functional experiments. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:27-29, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:266-269]

## Relationship to mammalian gasdermins

The study compares bacterial pores with those formed by mammalian [[gasdermins]]. Runella bGSDM pores in liposomes were reported as modestly larger than mammalian gasdermin pores, which the paper cites as roughly 135 to 215 A. More broadly, the authors argue that bacterial and mammalian gasdermins share a common logic in which proteolytic cleavage releases inhibition and permits oligomerization into membrane pores. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:240-250, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:270-285]

## Functional context

In this study, pore formation was part of a broader defense and cell-death program. Some bGSDM-protease systems caused strong toxicity when expressed in E. coli, and a Lysobacter bGSDM-containing operon conferred defense against coliphages T4, T5, and T6, with protection lost when the bGSDM gene was deleted. The authors interpret these findings as evidence that bGSDM-mediated membrane disruption functions in anti-phage defense and regulated bacterial cell death. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:129-148, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:270-285]
