# N-terminal palmitoylation stabilizes inactive bacterial gasdermins and supports membrane disruption

Bacterial gasdermins ([[Gasdermins|bacterial gasdermins]]) are pore-forming cell-death effectors identified in bacterial anti-phage defense systems. Structural analysis showed that, although they lack the large autoinhibitory C-terminal domain found in mammalian gasdermins, they can still adopt an inactive conformation. In these proteins, a short C-terminal peptide helps stabilize the inactive state, and the study identified an additional stabilizing feature: an N-terminal lipid modification. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:72-80, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:83-95]

In the *Bradyrhizobium* bGSDM structure, the authors observed density extending from cysteine 3 into a hydrophobic tunnel in the protein. At 1.5 A resolution, this density was assigned as a 16-carbon palmitoyl thioester. Mass spectrometry confirmed bGSDM palmitoylation, and the corresponding cysteine was reported to be conserved across most bacterial gasdermins and some fungal homologs. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:95-103]

The palmitoyl group is buried in a hydrophobic cavity that is capped by residues from the inhibitory C-terminal peptide, linking lipid modification to the inactive architecture of the protein. The authors state that palmitoylation contributes to stability of the inactive-state protein, and their structural modeling suggests that activation requires substantial reorganization of residues lining this hydrophobic tunnel. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:97-106]

Additional structural analysis explains how the inhibitory peptide and palmitoyl group work together. In *Bradyrhizobium* bGSDM, residue F253 latches over the palmitoyl modification, and similar hydrophobic contacts were observed in *Runella* and *Vitiosangium* structures. Truncation of the C-terminal peptide in bGSDMs from *Runella*, *Bradyrhizobium*, or *Vitiosangium* caused arrested cell growth, confirming that the peptide is required to maintain autoinhibition. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:172-206]

The study also tested the functional role of palmitoylation during membrane damage. In the *Runella* system, strong cellular toxicity required bGSDM palmitoylation, and cells expressing the active system ceased dividing and lost membrane integrity. After activation by a dedicated caspase-like protease, bGSDM formed membrane-associated puncta, disrupted membrane integrity in cells, and permeabilized liposomes in vitro. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:139-148, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:218-233]

However, palmitoylation was not absolutely required for membrane permeabilization. Mutation of the palmitoylated cysteine in *Runella* reduced, but did not abolish, liposome leakage and membrane-associated puncta formation. Likewise, mutation of the putative palmitoylation site in the *Lysobacter* bGSDM system did not abolish anti-phage defense. The authors therefore conclude that lipid modification supports membrane permeabilization rather than serving as an absolute requirement for it. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:234-239]

More broadly, the paper proposes that widespread palmitoylation of bacterial gasdermins points to cysteine modification as a conserved mechanism for regulating [[gasdermin pores]]. In this model, palmitoylation helps stabilize the inactive state before proteolytic activation, then supports the membrane-disrupting activity of the cleaved pore-forming protein. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:270-280]
