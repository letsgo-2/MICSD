# Bacterial gasdermins as phage-defense cell death effectors

[[Bacterial gasdermins]] (bGSDMs) are gasdermin-family proteins encoded in bacteria that function as anti-phage defense effectors and can execute lytic cell death. The source reports that bacterial homologs of mammalian gasdermins were identified in anti-phage defense islands, that some bGSDM systems protect against phage infection, and that activated bGSDMs disrupt membrane integrity through pore formation. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:23-29, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:72-75, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:129-138, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:218-239]

## Overview

The study identified 50 bacterial gasdermins by sequence analysis and found that they form a clade distinct from eukaryotic homologs. Structural analysis of bGSDMs from *Bradyrhizobium tropiciagri* and *Vitiosangium* sp. showed a shared overall architecture homologous to the mammalian gasdermin N-terminal pore-forming domain, including a conserved central beta-sheet core and peripheral helices and strands. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:72-82]

Unlike mammalian gasdermins, which are kept inactive by a large C-terminal domain, the analyzed bGSDMs lack that large CTD and instead contain a short C-terminal peptide that stabilizes the inactive state. The structures also revealed a conserved N-terminal cysteine bearing a palmitoyl thioester in a hydrophobic tunnel, and the source states that this palmitoylation contributes to stability of the inactive protein. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:83-106]

## Genomic association with proteases

Most bGSDMs in the study were genomically linked to one or more predicted protease genes: 43 of 50 were adjacent to protease-domain genes. In most cases these were [[caspase-like proteases]], including peptidase C14 and CHAT-family proteases, and the associated proteases were often fused to repeat or [[NACHT]] domains that are noted in the source as being frequently involved in pathogen recognition and [[inflammasome]] function in human innate immunity. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:119-129]

This genomic organization suggested that bGSDMs might function in defense, especially because bGSDM genes were sometimes located near known immune defense systems. The authors therefore tested bGSDM systems for anti-phage activity. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:129-130]

## Anti-phage defense activity

Among the systems tested in *Escherichia coli*, a four-gene operon from *Lysobacter enzymogenes* provided robust defense against coliphages T4, T5, and T6. Deletion of the bGSDM gene from this operon abolished protection, indicating that the gasdermin was essential for the defense phenotype in that system. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:131-138]

The source also reports that mutating the active site of the second trypsin-like protease in the *Lysobacter* bGSDM system abolished anti-phage defense. This links protease function to activation of the defense pathway, not just the presence of the bGSDM gene itself. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:153-156]

## Cell death effector function

Expression of some bGSDM-protease systems in *E. coli* caused strong cellular toxicity even without phage infection. In the *Runella* system, cells stopped dividing and lost membrane integrity during time-lapse microscopy with propidium iodide, consistent with membrane damage and cell death after bGSDM activation. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:139-148]

For the *Runella* system, toxicity required both the bGSDM and its associated protease. Mutating the predicted caspase-like protease active-site residues H796 and C840 eliminated toxicity, and expression of either component alone was insufficient, supporting a model in which the protease activates bGSDM as a cell death effector. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:149-153]

## Proteolytic activation mechanism

Using purified components, the authors reconstituted cleavage of the *Runella* bGSDM by its associated protease in vitro. Cleavage generated a lower-molecular-weight bGSDM species, depended on the protease catalytic residues, and occurred after residue L247 in a loop immediately preceding the C-terminal peptide. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:157-170]

Structural and mutational analyses support the idea that the short C-terminal peptide maintains autoinhibition until proteolytic activation occurs. Truncation of the C-terminal peptide in *Runella*, *Bradyrhizobium*, or *Vitiosangium* bGSDMs caused arrested cell growth, and mutations disrupting the protease cleavage motif blocked cleavage in vitro and toxicity in vivo. The authors conclude that, like mammalian gasdermins, bGSDMs are cell death effectors activated by proteolytic cleavage. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:172-217]

## Membrane targeting and pore formation

When GFP-tagged *Runella* bGSDM was co-expressed with its protease, it formed membrane-associated puncta in *E. coli* and induced toxicity. Transmission electron microscopy of cells expressing the active system showed disruption of membrane integrity. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:218-227]

In vitro, cleaved *Runella* bGSDM permeabilized liposomes and caused rapid release of internal contents, while protease active-site and bGSDM cleavage-site mutations abolished liposome permeabilization. These results show that proteolytic activation is required for bGSDM membrane-permeabilizing activity. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:228-239]

Electron microscopy showed that activated bGSDMs form ring-like pores in membranes. For *Runella* bGSDM, pores observed in liposomes had inner diameters of about 200 to 330 angstroms, while a metagenomic *Bacteroidetes* bGSDM formed smaller pores of about 130 to 190 angstroms, indicating pore-size heterogeneity among bGSDMs. Cryo-electron tomography confirmed that these pores span the liposomal membrane and disrupt membrane integrity. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:240-269]

## Role of palmitoylation

The source identifies a palmitoyl thioester linked to a conserved N-terminal cysteine in bGSDMs and states that this modification helps stabilize the inactive state. In functional assays, blocking palmitoylation reduced but did not eliminate liposome leakage or membrane-associated puncta formation in the *Runella* system, and mutation of the putative palmitoylation site in the *Lysobacter* bGSDM was not sufficient to abolish anti-phage defense. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:95-106, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:234-239]

These findings indicate that lipid modification supports bGSDM function but is not strictly required for membrane permeabilization or defense in the tested systems. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:234-239]

## Interpretation

The authors present bGSDMs as bacterial pore-forming effectors that can be activated by dedicated proteases to trigger membrane disruption and cell death, with at least some systems functioning in anti-phage defense. They further argue that the parallels between bacterial and mammalian gasdermins support pyroptosis as an ancient form of regulated cell death shared across bacteria and animals. ^[bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:23-29, bacterial-gasdermins-reveal-an-ancient-mechanism-of-cell-death.md:270-285]
