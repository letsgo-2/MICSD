and Jong Seung Kim*

ABSTRACT: Pyroptosis, a newly characterized form of immunogenic cell
death, is attracting increasing attention as a promising approach to cancer
immunotherapy. However, biocompatible strategies to activate pyroptosis
remain rare. Here, we show that a photocatalytic superoxide radical (O2−•)
generator, NI-TA, triggers pyroptosis in cancer cells. NI-TA was designed to
take advantage of an intramolecular triplet-ground state splitting energy
modulation approach. Detailed studies revealed that the pyroptosis triggered
by NI-TA under conditions of photoexcitation proceeds through a caspase-3/
gasdermin E (GSDME) pathway rather than via canonical processes involving
caspase-1/gasdermin-D (GSDMD). NI-TA was found to function via a
partial-O2-recycling mode of action and to trigger cell pyroptosis and provide
for effective cancer cell ablation even under conditions of hypoxia (≤2% O2).
In the case of T47D 3D multicellular spheroids, good antitumor efficiency and
stemness inhibition are achieved. This work highlights how photocatalytic chemistry may be leveraged to develop effective
pyroptosis-inducing agents.
■ INTRODUCTION
Pyroptosis, an immunogenic form of programmed cell death, is
marked by pore formation in membranes, cellular swelling and
lysis, and the release of proinflammatory cytokines. 1−3 Since its
first definition in 2001, 4 pyroptosis has attracted increasing
attention in biology and medicine, particularly in cancer
immune therapy because, relative to conventional cell
apoptosis and necrosis, it is more prone to initiate
inflammatory response via the triggering of intracellular
proinflammatory factors (e.g., IL-1β, IL-18).5−7 To date,
efforts to induce pyroptosis have mainly focused on chemo-
therapic drugs, such as doxorubicin and topotecan (Scheme
1A); 8 however, such strategies often suffer from drug resistance
and can be limited by the severe side effects these agents can
engender in normal tissues. 9 Therefore, there remains an
incentive to explore new approaches to triggering pyroptosis in
the context of tumor therapy.
In recent years, photon-mediated chemical and biomedical
engineering techniques (e.g., optogenetics,10,11 photocaging, 12
photoacoustic tomography,13 etc.), along with phototheranos-
tics (e.g., photopharmacology,14,15 photothermal therapy,16,17
and photodynamic therapy 18,19 ) have led to major advances in
therapy and diagnosis. This progress, in conjunction with its
noninvasive nature and the spatiotemporal control it can
provide,20,21 has led to the consideration that light could
provide a promising means of inducing pyroptosis in living
systems. In a seminal 2021 report from Bin Liu’s group, a
membrane-anchored singlet oxygen ( 1
O 2) generator was used
to induce pyroptosis in various cancer cell lines (Scheme
1A). 22 Unfortunately, many solid tumor microenvironments
are characterized by hypoxia (pO 2 < 5 mm Hg), 23,24 which
imparts resistance to O 2-dependent therapies such as 1
O 2-
based photodynamic therapy (PDT)25−27 and sonodynamic
therapy (SDT). 28,29 We thus sought to develop a low O 2-
dependent light-triggered system that could be used to activate
pyroptosis. We report here a photocatalytic superoxide radical
(O2−•) generation strategy that permits pyroptosis activation
in cancer cells even under conditions of hypoxia (2% O2 ). As
detailed below, it relies on photon-initiated O2−• generation,
superoxide dismutase (SOD)-mediated disproportionation,
and the Haber−Weiss/Fenton reaction to form hydroxyl
radicals (OH•). 30 This combination permits a partial-O 2-
recycling mode of action (MoA), thus allowing oxidative stress
to be triggered in cells at low O2 tension (Scheme 1B).31−33
Importantly, under conditions of photoirradiation, our O 2−•
generator, construct NI-TA, successfully inhibited the
proliferation of hypoxic T47D (human breast cancer cell

■ RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Engineering a Specific O2−• Generator. Recently, light-
triggered O2−• generation has garnered attention in the context
of so-called hypoxia compatible PDT. This is because the
dismutation of O2−• leads to partial O 2 recycling in cells, thus
providing a key factor needed for PDT. However, current
approaches to preparing O 2−• generators, such as the
benzophenothiazine series discovered by Peng et al. (Scheme
S1),31−33 although promising are largely empirically based.
Moreover, compounds that provide for exclusive O 2−•
generation remain limited in number. 35
After considering accepted photocatalytic mechanisms for
O2−• generation (i.e., Type-I photochemistry), we speculated
that if electron transfer from an excited triplet PS* to ground
state triplet oxygen ( 3
O2 ) was fast compared to the competitive
energy transfer process (to form 1
O 2, Scheme 1B), it would be
possible to achieve effective O2−• production.32 Moreover, we
postulated that modulating the triplet (T 1)-ground state (S0 )
splitting energy gap (ΔES0‑T1), such that it is lower than the
3
O2 /1
O 2 band gap, would be an effective means of achieving
this goal since the 1 O2-producing energy transfer pathway
would be necessarily blocked. In fact, we found that when an
electron-donating group (triphenylamine, TPA) was linked via
a through-conjugated dialkyl fluorene spacer (B) to an electron
acceptor (e.g., the 1,8-naphthalimide derivative (NI), Figure
1A), a D−B−A motif (i.e., NI-TA, Figure 1B) with a relatively
low T 1 state energy (ΔES0−T1 = 1.115 eV) could be produced.
Of note is that this ΔES0−T1 value is considerably smaller than
the 3
O 2/1 O2 gap (1.610 eV). This energetics, coupled with fast
intersystem crossing following photoirradiation, makes NI-TA
an effective O 2−• photogenerator. As controls, NI-DPA, linking
an electron donor, and NI-DF, containing a π bridge, were
prepared (Figure 1B). Detailed synthetic routes are provided
in the Supporting Information and shown schematically in
Scheme S2. All new compounds were characterized by 1
H
NMR and 13 C NMR spectroscopies, HR-Mass spectrometry,
and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Fig-
ures S1−S24).
Spectroscopic Characterization. The optical properties
of NI, NI-DF, NI-DPA, and NI-TA were first analyzed by
UV−vis and fluorescence spectroscopies. The corresponding
photophysical properties are summarized in Table 1. These
four compounds all exhibit stable absorption spectra in a
variety of solvents of different polarities (cf. Figure S35). In the
particular case of NI-TA, in addition to the absorption band at
355 nm, a shoulder with a maximum at around 425 nm is seen
that is ascribed to charge transfer between the donor and
acceptor subunits (Figure 2A and Table 1).36 Compared to its
simpler analogues, NI, NI-DPA, and NI-DF, the full D−B−A
system, NI-TA, exhibited a maximal fluorescence emission
band at 535 nm characterized by a 180 nm Stokes shift (Figure
2B). Such a large Stokes shift is considered attractive in the
context of bioluminescence imaging. 37
It has been previously reported that effective intramolecular
charge transfer (ICT) within suitably designed optical probes
favors triplet state formation, a conversion that is critical for
photosensitization reactions.38 Therefore, the excited-state
optical features of NI-TA and the control compounds were
analyzed in a range of organic solvents of differing polarity
(Figure S36). In accord with the Lippert−Mataga equation,
the slope of the Stokes shift (va − vf ) was plotted against the
corresponding orientation polarizability (Δf).39 As shown in
Figure 2C, relative to NI (3.0 ± 0.9 × 10 3 cm−1
), NI-DF (9.1
± 1.3 × 10 3 cm−1
), and NI-DPA (10.8 ± 1.1 × 103 cm−1
), an
ostensibly larger slope was observed in the case of NI-TA (11.5
± 0.7 × 10 3 cm−1 ). Although not a proof, this finding provides
support for the expectation that NI-TA would have the largest
dipole moment among the four compounds initially considered
in this study. As such, the D−B−A system, NI-TA, was
expected to display the greatest ICT effect following
photoexcitation.
The propeller-like TPA moiety has been widely exploited as
a “building block” to construct AIE architectures.40 We were
thus curious whether the incorporation of a TPA subunit into
NI-TA would produce a viable AIE system. If so, it might
provide a way to overcome the ACQ limitations of current
O 2−• photogenerators. To test this proposition, a typical AIE
assay was performed by recording the spectroscopic changes of
NI-TA in a mixed water/methanol solvent system containing
different aqueous fractions (fw). As can be seen from an
inspection of Figure 2D, in pure methanol (fw: 0%), negligible
fluorescence was observed for NI-TA (ΦF < 0.01).
This lack of emission is attributed to the excited-state
nonradiative decay caused by intramolecular motions (e.g.,
bond rotations) within the TPA core (Figure S37A).40 As the
fw increased, the intensity of the NI-TA fluorescence emission
centered at 567 nm likewise increased. For instance, a 96.8-fold
increase in intensity was seen at fw = 90%, as would be
expected for an AIE system (Figure S37B). By contrast, no
such AIE-ascribable responses were detected in the control
compound NI-DF lacking a TPA moiety (Figure S38).
Considering that other polarity-induced structural changes
could contribute to the observed AIE phenomenon, 42 NI-TA
and NI-DF were analyzed using density functional theory
(DFT). The calculations revealed that the dihedral angles
between the NI moiety and the fluorene bridge were similar in
NI-DF (57.24°) and NI-TA (56.63°) (Figure S39). We thus
conclude that it is the presence of the TPA subunit, rather than
differences in molecular geometries, that accounts for the
disparate AIE properties of NI-DF and NI-TA.

evaluated the ability of NI-TA to act as an effective and
selective O 2−• generator. Initial assessments were made using
dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR123) as an O 2−• fluorescent

To test whether NI-TA was a specific O 2−• generator, as
opposed to a system that would produce, for example, other
reactive oxygen species upon photoirradiation, an effort was
made to detect singlet oxygen (1 O2) and the hydroxyl radical
(OH•). Hydroxyphenyl fluorescein (HPF) and 9,10-
anthracenediylbis(methylene)-dimalonic acid (ABDA) were
employed as OH• and 1
O2 indicators, respectively. 44,45 Upon
photoirradiation (white light, 35 mW/cm2 ), the absorption
peaks of ABDA at 378 nm (Figures 2G and S45) and the
fluorescence emission of HPF at 516 nm were barely perturbed
(Figures 2H and S46). EPR spectroscopy likewise revealed an
absence of TEMP−1 O 2 (Figure S47) or DMPO−OH•
(quartet) adducts (Figure 2I). Considered in concert, these
findings are taken as evidence that little, if any, 1 O2 or OH• is
generated by NI-TA under conditions of photoirradiation.
This selectivity is rationalized in terms of NI-TA undergoing a
Type-I electron transfer to generate O2−• to the near exclusion
of deactivation pathways that would produce other reactive
oxygen species (ROS). The overall process can be briefly
described as follows: upon photoirradiation, NI-TA absorbs
photons to produce an excited singlet state (1
[NI-TA*]).
Following intersystem crossing (ISC), the corresponding
triplet state (3
[NI-TA*]) is formed. Electron transfer to
molecular oxygen then produces O2−• (Figure S48).
NI-TA was also found to be an effective O2−• generator in
vitro, as evidenced by dihydroethidium (DHE, cellular O 2−•
assay kit) staining of living cells under normoxia (for proposed
sensing mechanism, see Figure 2J). 46 For T47D cells treated
with NI-TA and DHE, followed by photoirradiation (white
light, 50 mW/cm 2
), a characteristic red fluorescence indicative
of the exposure of DHE to O 2−• was observed by confocal laser
scanning microscopy (CLSM, Figure 2J). Similar results were
seen in other cancer cell lines, including MDA-MB-231 and
BT474 (Figure S49). In contrast, only a limited probe signal
was observed for NI-DF under otherwise identical conditions
(Figure S50). Similar experiments carried out in T47D cells
using the singlet oxygen sensor green (SOSG) probe as an 1
O 2
indicator revealed no evidence of 1 O2 generation (Figures 2K,
S51, and S52).47

that would be expected to generate alternative ROS. Effective

In Type-II energy transfer processes as needed to produce
1 O2 (as needed for, e.g., PDT), the lowest energy PS triplet
state (i.e., 3
[PS*]) transfers energy to 3
O 2, allowing for its spin
correlated conversion to singlet oxygen ( 1
O 2), an activated
form that lies ∼94.5 kJ mol−1 above the ground state.52,53 A
consequence of this energy difference is that if the energy of
the 3
[PS*] species relative to its ground state is lower than
94.5 kJ mol−1 , the production of 1
O 2 becomes endoergic. 54 In
other words, if the vertical T1 to S0 emission energy (ΔET1−S0)
of a given PS is smaller than the 3
O 2/1 O2 energy gap, energy
transfer from a photo-excited triplet PS and 3

on thermodynamic grounds; in the limit, no 1
O 2 generation
would be seen. In the case of NI-TA, the ΔET1−S0 was
calculated at its T1 minimum to be 1.115 eV, which is much
lower than 1.610 eV required to promote 3 O 2 to its
corresponding singlet excited state (i.e., 1
O 2) (Figure 3D).
Thus, both experiment and theory lead to the conclusion that,
upon photoexcitation, NI-TA will undergo a Type-I process to
produce O 2−•. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first
time that rational design through the use of an electron-
donating π bridge has been used to modulate the ΔES0−T1
energy gap so as to favor near-exclusive O2−• photogeneration.
To validate the generality of our newly proposed strategy for
O 2−• photogeneration, two other D−B−A compounds that
contain π bridges were designed and synthesized (Scheme S3
and Figures S25−S34). The first of these systems, NI-TPA,
contains a benzene moiety as the π bridge; its congener, NI-
DA, incorporates a biphenyl spacer (Figure 3E). As true for
NI-TA, the ΔES1−T1 values for NI-TPA (1.833 eV) and NI-DA
(1.904 eV) are relatively small compared with those of NI and
NI-DF; this reflects the modest separation between the
respective LUMOs and HOMOs (Figure S53). In NI-TPA
and NI-DA, the ΔET1−S0 values were calculated at their T1
minimum to be 1.104 and 1.041 eV, respectively (Figure 3E
and Table S1). In accord with our design expectations, these
values are lower than the 1.610 eV gap for 3
O 2-to- 1
O 2 spin
state conversion. Thus, NI-TPA and NI-DA both produce
O 2−• under conditions of photoirradiation (Figure S54), rather
than 1
O 2. We take this finding as further support for our core
hypothesis, namely that modulating the intramolecular triplet-
ground state splitting energy by means of appropriately chosen
π bridges can lead to effective and specific O2−• photo-
generation.

Activation of Cell Pyroptosis. As an initial matter, CLSM
imaging was used to ascertain whether morphological changes
in T47D cells consistent with pyroptosis were produced by NI-
TA, which exhibited optimal O 2−• generation performance
(Figure S55). As can be seen from Figure 4A, after incubation
with NI-TA, photoexcitation of T47D cells (white light, 50
mW/cm 2
) was found to induce the formation of a number of
bubble-like protrusions known as pyroptotic bodies (as
visualized by red fluorescence staining with Annexin V Alexa
Fluor 647 conjugate; as indicated by white arrow). 1 Staining
with Hoechst33342 (providing blue fluorescence) revealed the
cell nucleus staying intact rather than undergoing fragmenta-
tion (one of the hallmarks of apoptosis),55 as expected for

In Vitro Cytotoxicity and Pyroptosis Activation under
Hypoxia. Tumor hypoxia often represents a limitation in the
case of light-based therapeutic strategies. We thus sought to
evaluate whether NI-TA would engender pyroptosis and
mediate an antitumor effect under conditions of hypoxia.
Biologically, O 2−• can initiate and participate in a series of
cascade bioreactions, thereby permitting a partial-O 2-recycling
MoA. First, under SOD catalysis, O 2−• can produce hydrogen
peroxide (H2O 2) and O2 via a classic disproportionation
(Reaction 1). Subsequently, the newly formed H 2O 2 can take
part in the Fenton reaction to generate hydroxyl radicals
(•OH) and ferric ion (Fe3+ ) by reacting with ferrous ion
(Fe 2+ ); while such Fe 3+ could further react with O 2−• to supply
oxygen (Reaction 2). Moreover, O 2−• is capable of modulating
the H 2O 2 reduction through the Haber−Weiss reaction, which
would also produce O 2 (Reaction 3). As the result of these
processes, it is expected that an O 2−• photogenerator, such as
NI-TA, would induce oxidative stress at even low O2 tension.

Antitumor Effects on 3D Tumor Spheroids with
Strong Stemness. Tumorigenesis, chemoresistance, and
reoccurrence are correlated with breast cancer stem cell
(BCSC) levels characterized by their self-renewal ability, which
severely limits the success of standard therapeutic interventions
(Figure 6A).59 At present, three-dimensional (3D) multi-
cellular spheroids (MCSs) are attracting interest in the context
of cancer stem cell (CSC) studies since they are of
intermediate complexity between 2D cell culture and in vivo
tumor models. 60 We thus evaluated the effect of NI-TA +
photoirradiation on T47D (human breast cancer cell line) 3D
MCSs with a diameter of 400 to 500 μm as a model for the
solid tumor microenvironment (Figure S66). Owing to their
strong stemness, T47D cells readily form 3D spheroids (cf.
Figure 6B, control group). CLSM imaging revealed that NI-TA
is able to penetrate into the core of 3D spheroids (Figure S67).
Subsequent photoirradiation (50 mW/cm 2
, 20 min) led to the
effective production of O 2−•, as evidenced by the intense DHE
fluorescence produced for the combination of NI-TA and light
(Figure S68). Moreover, a size reduction was seen in over 95%
of the tumor spheres under these conditions (Figure 6B, bright
field). Calcein AM/PI staining provided support for the design
expectation that the T47D cells in the 3D MCSs were killed as
the result of O2−• generation and pyroptosis activation (Figure
6B). Such a salutary outcome was not observed when NI-DF
was used in lieu of NI-TA (Figures S69 and S70). Further
support for the efficacy of NI-TA + light in MCSs came from
an ATP assay (Figure 6C). Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)
decomposes promptly after cell death. Thus, its concentration
is related to cell viability. As shown in Figure 6D, in the MCSs
treated with 2.5 μM NI-TA and subjected to photoirradiation,
the ATP level fell to less than 1% of that of the control group.
Presumably, this reflects the decomposition of ATP resulting
from cell disruption.
During the study of potential anti-MCSs mechanisms, it was
noted that the expression of CSCs markers, including
pentaspan transmembrane glycoproteins CD133 61 and alde-
hyde dehydrogenase 1 (ALDH1), 62 was reduced in the 3D
spheroids after treatment with NI-TA under conditions of
photoirradiation (Figure 6E). For example, in a study involving
the 5.0 μM NI-TA + light group (white light, 50 mW/cm 2
, 20
min), the level of CD133 and ALDH1 in the 3D spheroids
declined to 10.4% and 4.7% of that in the control group
(without addition of NI-TA) + light, respectively (Figure S71).
These results support the conclusion that the combination of
NI-TA + light is effective at inhibiting stemness and ablating
CSCs in MCSs (Figure 6A). We thus believe that NI-TA, as a
photoinitiator of pyroptosis, could have a role to play in
managing hypoxic CSCs in solid tumors.

conversion of triplet oxygen to singlet oxygen. CLSM imaging

fluorescence emission features of NI-TA are likely to limit its
utility in vivo. However, we believe that the present study
serves to highlight the potential utility of photoinduced
pyroptosis in the context of solid tumor therapy and that the
underlying design principles can be used to create yet-
improved photosensitizers. Work along these lines is in
progress in our laboratories.
