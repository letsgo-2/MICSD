Pyroptosis-Mediated Synergistic Photodynamic and
Photothermal Immunotherapy Enabled by a
Tumor-Membrane-Targeted Photosensitive Dimer
Yuqi Tang, Hari Krishna Bisoyi, Xu-Man Chen,* Zhiyang Liu,* Xiao Chen, Shu Zhang,
and Quan Li*
Overcoming the resistance to apoptosis and immunosuppression of tumor
cells is a significant challenge in augmenting the effect of cancer
immunotherapy. Pyroptosis, a lytic programmed cell-death pathway unlike
apoptosis, is considered a type of immunogenic cell death (ICD) that can
intensify the ICD process in tumor cells, releasing dramatically increased
tumor-associated antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns to
promote cancer immunotherapy. Herein, a tumor cell membrane-targeted
aggregation-induced emission photosensitive dimer is found to be able to
achieve highly efficient ICD under the synergistic effect of photodynamic and
photothermal therapy. The photosensitive dimer can efficiently produce type-I
reactive oxygen species (ROS) by photodynamic therapy in hypoxic tumor
tissue, leading to pyroptosis by direct cell membrane damage, which is further
reinforced by its photothermal effect. Furthermore, the enhanced ICD effect
based on the dimer can completely eliminate the primary tumor on the
seventh day of treatment and can also boost systemic antitumor immunity by
generating immune memory, which is demonstrated by the superior
antitumor therapeutic effects on both solid tumors and metastatic tumors
when healing 4T1 tumor mouse models with poor immunogenicity.

Pyroptosis is a lytic programmed cell
death triggered by inflammasomes, during
which dead cells release antigens and effectively trigger an
antigen-specific immune response.[8] Not only can pyropto-
sis bypass apoptosis resistance, but it can also initiate tumor-
specific immunity, making pyroptosis-mediated immunotherapy
a promising strategy to induce ICD, enhance immune response,
and eventually inhibit tumor growth. Pyroptosis is mediated by
gasdermin (GSDM) family proteins and caspases. [9] The genera-
tion of GSDM-N fragments through the cleavage of GSDM-D by
activating caspase-1 is the initial step, which then induces the for-
mation of crucial cell membrane pores, resulting in cell swelling,
membrane rupture, release of cell contents, and pyroptosis.[10]
The cell contents released include various tumor-relevant anti-
gens such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and inflammatory
cytokines (interleukin-1beta (IL-1𝛽) and interleukin-18 (IL-18),
to initiate an antitumor immune response. Pyroptosis can fur-

and continuous antitumor immune response. [11] Pyroptosis-
mediated immunotherapy is mostly affected by chemotherapy
drugs in previous works, whose application is limited due to
their serious side effects. Thus, it is important to develop an ef-
ficient, non-invasive, and targeted pyroptosis-mediated photoim-
munotherapy for cancer.

Phototherapy is a promising method for tumor interventional
therapy because of its adjustable light injury, non-invasiveness,
and low side effects. Photodynamic therapy and photothermal
therapy are two rational approaches to realize phototherapy.
During the photodynamic process, cytotoxic reactive oxygen
species (ROS) are produced by photosensitizers under light ir-
radiation while light energy is efficiently transformed to heat
energy through photothermal agents during the photothermal
process. [12] In this case, the synergistic strategy of combin-
ing photodynamic and photothermal therapy provides an opti-
mal way to accelerate cell death and improve therapeutic effi-
ciency. Photodynamic therapy can efficiently trigger the activa-
tion of inflammasome by generating ROS to lead to pyroptosis
to initiate ICD, while photothermal therapy can reinforce this
process, [13] which is a perfect cooperation for enhancing the ef-
ficiency of photoimmunotherapy. Due to the short lifespan of
ROS and the limited effective radius, [14] precise subcellular lo-
calization of photosensitizers is crucial for the in situ genera-
tion of ROS at targeted organelles to avoid rapid attenuation and
degradation.[15] Among various subcellular targeting photosensi-
tizers, cell membrane-targeting photosensitizers are thought to
increase membrane permeability, destroy membrane integrity,
induce lipid peroxidation, inactivate membrane-anchored signal
proteins, and finally lead to cell membrane rupture with the rapid
release of cell contents, which is a preferable option for pho-
tosensitizers for pyroptosis-mediated photoimmunotherapy.[16]
Although there are extensive studies on photoimmunotherapy
based on various photosensitizers, membrane-targeted photo-
sensitizers for pyroptosis-mediated photoimmunotherapy have
rarely been developed.
Aggregation-induced emission photosensitizers are widely
used for photoimmunotherapy, [5b] however, aggregation effect
of such photosensitizers for boosting pyroptosis-mediated pho-
toimmunotherapy is still a challenge. Herein, we report a tu-
mor cell membrane-targeted aggregation-induced emission pho-
tosensitive dimer D1 that is found to enable pyroptosis-mediated
photodynamic and photothermal immunotherapy (Scheme 1).
Triphenylamine, thiophene, and pyridinium groups are em-
ployed to form a 𝜋-conjugated photosensitive chromophore bear-
ing strong intramolecular charge transfer, and a flexible octyl
group is used to link two chromophore moieties to form the
dimeric photosensitizer D1. The photosensitive dimer D1 ex-
hibits near-infrared aggregation-induced emission and excel-
lent subcellular distribution on the cell membrane, making it a
promising candidate for photoinduced pyroptosis. Compared to
the monomer M1 with the same chromophore, D1 shows bet-
ter self-assembly and aggregation-induced emission, indicating
a greater potential for imaging and photoimmunotherapy. Syn-
ergistic photodynamic and photothermal processes can be real-
ized by D1 upon light irradiation, during which the generated
type-I ROS and heat energy are used to initiate and enhance the
pyroptosis of tumors, respectively. Moreover, pyroptosis induced
by synergistic phototherapy promotes the release of cell contents
and inflammatory cytokines to activate the antitumor immune
response. The production of a tumor-specific antigen and the
maturation of dendritic cells are promoted, leading to the pro-
liferation of activated T cells to provide systemic antitumor im-
munity. Such systemic antitumor immunity is highly effective
in inhibiting the growth of both primary tumors and distant tu-
mors, and this can eliminate tumors and prevent metastasis by
preventing immune escape. In the triple-negative breast cancer
4T1 mouse model with poor immunogenicity,[5b] an effective an-
titumor immune response and satisfactory therapeutic effect are
accomplished.

2. Results and Discussion
We first designed a photosensitive dimer D1 with a donor-𝜋-
acceptor (D–𝜋–A) conjugated structure by employing methyl-
substituted triphenylamino thiophene unit as a donor, a carbon-
carbon double bond as a 𝜋-bridge, and a pyridinium as an ac-
ceptor (Figure 1A). The molecular structure of D1 was character-
ized using 1 H NMR, 13 C NMR, and high-resolution mass spec-
trometry (Figures S1–S3, Supporting Information). Such a D–𝜋–
A structure without heavy atoms can effectively separate the dis-
tribution of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and
lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), and this has the
potential to promote the intersystem crossing process and induce
the production of ROS. [17] In the optimized molecular confor-
mations and molecular orbitals calculated by density functional
theory (DFT) (Figure 1B,C), the delocalized electron density of
the HOMO is distributed in the triphenylamine segment, while
that of the LUMO is mainly concentrated on the pyridinium unit,
indicating strong intramolecular charge transfer in D1. Further-
more, the introduction of thiophene enhances the donor-acceptor
strength and reduces the energy gap between the S 1 and T 1 states
(ΔES1T1 ), thus, promoting a spectral red-shift and boosting the
ability to generate ROS.[18] According to a Kohn–Sham frontier
orbit analysis, the ΔES1T4 of D1 is calculated to be 0.0001 eV, in-
dicating that dimeric D1 is more effective for triggering the inter-
system crossing process (Table S1, Supporting Information). In
addition, the theoretical calculation results show that ΔES1T1 of
D1 is only 0.6716 eV, which is much better than M1, and is more
conducive to the formation of non-radiative transitions. However,
the photothermal conversion efficiency is also expected to be en-
hanced owing to the more active intramolecular stretching vibra-
tions and rotations induced by the increase in the intramolecular
distance between the donor and acceptor groups. [19]
The photophysical properties of D1 were studied using UV–
vis absorption and fluorescence spectroscopy. As shown in Fig-
ure 1D, the absorption and emission spectra of a DMSO solu-
tion of D1 showed a main absorption peak at 497 nm and a flu-
orescence emission peak at 675 nm. Additionally, D1 showed
significant aggregation-induced emission in poor solvents, such
as toluene and water (Figure 1E; Figures S11 and S12, Support-
ing Information). The self-assembly behavior of D1 was further
demonstrated by observing the spherical shape of D1 by trans-
mission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron mi-
croscopy (SEM), and determining the average diameter to be
∼110.3 nm measured by dynamic light scattering (Figure 1F; Fig-
ure S15, Supporting Information). The zeta potential of D1 had
a positive value of +10.68 ± 0.66 mV (Figure S16, Supporting In-
formation). Therefore, the cationic dimer D1 has enhanced per-
meability and retention effects, and thus shows a good ability to
target cancer cells bearing negative charges.

acid (ABDA), hydroxyphenylfluorescein (HPF) and dihydrorho-
damine 123 (DHR123). The results showed that D1 showed

We investigated the cell imaging and subcellular distribution
of D1 using colocalization analysis in HeLa cells as a model can-
cer cell line. As shown in Figure 3A, when co-staining with 3,3′-

uptake time of D1 into the cancer cell membrane was quanti-

in the presence of ROS. As shown in Figure 3D and Figure S30

cence signals under light, demonstrating its ROS generating abil-
ity. In the presence of the ROS inhibitor N-acetylcysteine (NAC),
the fluorescence of D1 in 4T1 cells remained undetected, con-
firming its ability to generate ROS under light irradiation. Fur-
thermore, the quantitative results of ROS generation determined
by flow cytometry were consistent with those from confocal im-
ages (Figure S31, Supporting Information). The fluorescence sig-
nal of the D1 group was significantly enhanced under light irra-
diation, with the fluorescence signal of D1 being 40 times higher
than that of the control group.

Pyroptosis is performed by the GSDM family of proteins,
which contain an N-terminal pore-forming domain and a C-
terminal inhibitory domain, the latter being activated by cer-
tain caspases to form pores on the cell membrane, leading to
its rupture. [23] To directly investigate pyroptosis induced by cell
membrane-targeted D1, we observed the morphological changes
in cells treated with D1 using confocal microscopy (Figure 4A;
Figures S36 and S37, Supporting Information). Interestingly,
when D1-stained cancer cells were irradiated with light for 8 min,
the cells swelled (evidence of pyroptosis) rather than shrinking
(evidence of apoptosis), and the bubbles were generated from
the plasma membrane and gradually expanded, demonstrating
an obvious pyroptosis process. When the cell membrane was en-
tirely damaged, D1 penetrated the cells to image all pyroptosis-
mediated dead cells (Figure 4E). Furthermore, when Trypan Blue
as a staining agent of dead cells was co-incubated with D1-stained
4T1 cells, light irradiation led to evident blue nuclear staining,
indicating that the plasma membrane was damaged (Figure S38,
Supporting Information). We further identified several key mark-
ers to investigate the mechanism of pyroptosis induced by D1. As
shown in Figure 4B,D and Figures S39 and S40 (Supporting In-
formation), western blot analysis revealed that the concentrations
of cleaved GSDMD-N and cleaved caspase-1 increased signifi-
cantly in D1-stained 4T1 cells under light irradiation. Moreover,
lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and inflammatory cytokines (IL-1𝛽
and IL-18) [24] released during pyroptosis were also detected (Fig-
ure 4G–I). These results demonstrate that D1 produced ROS un-
der light irradiation, activating caspase-1 on the cell membrane
to cleave GSDMD into GSDMD-N domains. GSDMD-N translo-
cates to the cell membrane and forms membrane pores to induce
pyroptosis, while the photothermal effect of D1 accelerates the
process of pyroptosis leading to the fast release of inflammatory
cytokines. However, M1 induced weaker pyroptosis because of its
lower aggregation ability (Figure S41, Supporting Information).
Subsequently, the pyroptosis-mediated ICD process was fur-
ther determined by detecting two important markers, CRT and
HMGB-1.[5] As shown in Figure 4C,F and Figures S42 and S43
(Supporting Information), D1-stained 4T1 cells significantly pro-
moted the exposure of CRT on the cell surface under light irra-
diation. Moreover, HMGB1 was initially detected in the cell nu-
cleus, but it migrated to whole cells after irradiating D1-stained
4T1 cells. These results clearly demonstrated that through light-
induced pyroptosis, D1 efficiently induced ICD and initiated
adaptive immune responses.

3. Conclusion
We have successfully developed a pyroptosis-mediated synergis-
tic photodynamic and photothermal immunotherapy enabled
by employing tumor cell membrane-targeted dimeric photosen-
sitizer D1. The aggregation-induced emission photosensitizer
dimer D1 has a terrific ability to generate type-I ROS, which is
an excellent pyroptosis because it targets the tumor cell mem-
brane well, and its photothermal performance further accelerates
the pyroptosis process for drastic ICD of cancer cells, with a long-
term immune response to prevent tumor recurrence. Tumor cells
produce DAMPs when ICD occurs, which can promote the mat-
uration of dendritic cells, initiate T-cell cloning and expansion,
prime adaptive antitumor effects, and produce obvious immune
memory. Owing to the preferable aggregation capability of pho-
tosensitive dimer D1 compared to the monomer M1 with the
same chromophore, the dimer shows better ROS generation ef-
ficiency, an improved photothermal effect, and enhanced perme-
ability and retention effects. This powerful pyroptosis-mediated
photothermal/photodynamic immunotherapy is further demon-
strated by antitumor experiments in vivo on the 4T1 tumor
mouse model with poor immunogenicity. On the seventh day of
treatment, the primary tumors are completely eliminated, and
the growth of distant tumors is effectively inhibited, thus in-
hibiting tumor recurrence and metastasis. Therefore, as a con-
sequence of the comprehensive characterization of the photo-
sensitive dimer for phototherapy, the research, that we disclose
here, would provide a promising strategy for synergistic photoim-
munotherapy.
