SUMMARY
Caspase-4 is an intracellular sensor for cytosolic bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and underlies infection-
elicited pyroptosis. It is unclear whether and how caspase-4 detects host-derived factors to trigger pyropto-
sis. Here we show that mitochondrial permeability transition (MPT) activates caspase-4 by promoting the
assembly of a protein complex, which we term the Apaf-1 pyroptosome, for the execution of facilitated py-
roptosis. MPT, when induced by bile acids, calcium overload, or an adenine nucleotide translocator 1 (ANT1)
activator, triggers assembly of the pyroptosome comprised of Apaf-1 and caspase-4 with a stoichiometry
ratio of 7:2. Unlike the direct cleavage of gasdermin D (GSDMD) by caspase-4 upon LPS ligation, caspase-
4 activated in the Apaf-1 pyroptosome proceeds to cleave caspase-3 and thereby GSDME to induce pyrop-
tosis. Caspase-4-initiated and GSDME-executed pyroptosis underlies cholestatic liver failure. These findings
identify Apaf-1 pyroptosome as a pivotal machinery for cells sensing MPT signals and may shed light on un-
derstanding how cells execute intrinsic pyroptosis under sterile conditions.
INTRODUCTION
Pyroptosis is typically featured with pore formation on plasma
membrane, cell lysis, and swelling, and is mediated by inflam-
matory caspases (Jorgensen and Miao, 2015). Both caspase-
1-related canonical inflammasome (Miao et al., 2010) and
caspase-4/5/11 in non-canonical inflammasome have been
found to play key roles in pyroptosis (Kayagaki et al., 2011).
Caspase-1 is activated on the protein complex platform called
inflammasome, among which various pattern recognition re-
ceptors 	sense 	pathogen-associated 	molecular 	patterns
(PAMPs) or host-derived danger-associated molecular pat-
terns (DAMPs) (Man and Kanneganti, 2015). Activated cas-
pase-1 cleaves IL-1b or IL-18 to trigger inflammation, and
gasdermin D (GSDMD) to induce pyroptosis (Bergsbaken
et al., 2009; Russo et al., 2016; Sborgi et al., 2016; Shi et al.,
2015; Taabazuing et al., 2017). In contrast to the extensively
studied mechanisms about activation of canonical inflamma-
somes, the understanding of how caspase-4, previously
defined as noncanonical inflammasome, is activated is only
emerging. Caspase-4/11 can directly detect cytoplasmic LPS
via their CARD domain interacting with a carbohydrate chain
of LPS called lipid A. This interaction leads to caspase-4/11
auto-cleavage 	and 	subsequent 	activation 	of 	GSDMD,
providing a mechanistic rationale for understanding why cas-
pase-4/11 plays pivotal roles in pyroptotic cell death implicated
in infectious diseases (Hagar et al., 2013; Shi et al., 2014).
Notably, in addition to infectious diseases, pyroptosis has also
been widely observed in many other types of diseases charac-
terized with sterile inflammation. The mechanism and signal
pathways underlying pyroptosis under sterile conditions had
been previously attributed to the activation of canonical inflam-
masome and caspase-1, considering that canonical inflamma-
some is able to sense diverse DAMPs and endogenous bioactive
metabolites such as fatty acids, amino acids, and bile acids (Hao
et al., 2017; Levy et al., 2015; Pan et al., 2018). Of interest, cas-
pase-4/11 can also detect endogenous oxidized phospholipids
facilitating IL-1b release, but not pyroptosis (Zanoni et al.,
2016). It is important to note that caspase-4/11 is widely distrib-
uted in diverse organs/tissues where its pathophysiological sig-
nificance remains largely elusive. A critical question is whether
and how caspase-4/11 detects other host-derived endogenous
factors, thereby triggering pyroptosis implicated in noninfective
diseases.
Mitochondrion is a central hub sensing various stimuli and
thereby controlling cell fates including cell death, differentiation,
proliferation, and immunological responses (Kroemer et al.,
2007; Mehta et al., 2017). Mitochondrial outer membrane perme-
abilization (MOMP) usually triggers apoptotic cell death. Mito-
chondria also regulate other forms of cell death, including
necroptosis and ferroptosis (Bock and Tait, 2020). Increasing ev-
idence indicates that the mitochondrial permeability transition
(MPT) acts as a key nodal in regulating necrosis (Nakagawa
et al., 2005). MPT is induced by the persistent opening of the

RESULTS
Bile Acids Trigger Caspase-4/11-Dependent Pyroptosis
We have recently identified bile acids as a class of distinctive
DAMPs that can activate both signal 1 and 2 of NLRP3 inflamma-
some in mature macrophages, but not in monocytes (Hao et al.,
2017). Bile acids are known for their cytotoxicity but without clear
molecular events (Katona et al., 2009). Because inflammasome
activation is connected to pyroptosis, we asked whether bile
acids might also trigger pyroptotic cell death. Both deoxycholic
acid (DCA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) activated cas-
pase-11 (caspase-4 in THP-1 cells) and promoted the release
of HMGB1, IL-1a, and cell death in mouse bone marrow-derived
macrophages (BMDMs) (Figures 1A–1F) as well as in THP-1 cells
(Figures S1A–S1D). Morphological assessment and propidium
iodide (PI)-positive staining further support that bile acid-
induced cell death was typical pyroptosis (Figures 1G, 1H, and
S1E). Cytotoxicity of bile acids at concentrations higher than
400 mM might be partially related to their detergent effects (Ben-
edetti et al., 1997). We demonstrated that equivalent amount of
SDS to that of 200 mM bile acid was unable to activate caspases,
excluding the possibility of detergent effects being involved in
caspase activation by bile acids (Figures S1F–S1I). Moreover,
neither RIP1 inhibitor nor MLKL inhibitor blocked bile acid-
induced cell death, suggesting that bile acid-induced cell death
was not programmed necroptosis (Figures S1J and S1K).
Notably, canonical inflammasome represented by caspase-1
activation and IL-1b secretion was not influenced by bile acid
treatment without LPS priming in BMDMs (Figures 1D and 1F),
which agrees with our previous findings that bile acids alone
without LPS priming were hard to activate NLRP3 inflammasome
in BMDMs, in spite of caspase-1 activation in THP-1 cells (Hao
et al., 2017) (Figure S1A). Tauro-conjugates of DCA and CDCA
(TDCA and TCDCA), both of which were capable of activating
signal 2 of NLRP3 inflammasome (Hao et al., 2017), could not
activate caspase-4/11 and were unable to trigger pyroptosis
(Figures S1C–S1E and S1L–S1O). Moreover, bile acid-induced
cell death was independent of NLRP3 (Figures S1P–S1T), and
knockout of Caspase-1 exerted little influence on bile acid-
induced cell death and IL-1a secretion, confirming that the
featured cell death triggered by bile acid is independent of ca-
nonical NLRP3 inflammasome (Figures 1I–1L). In contrast, bile
acid-induced cell death, LDH release, and IL-1a secretion
were dramatically reduced in BMDMs isolated from Caspase-
11/ mice in comparison with that from wild-type (WT) mice
(Figures 1I–1L). Collectively, these results support that bile acids
induce 	caspase-4/11-dependent 	pyroptosis 	of 	mature
macrophages.
GSDME Is Involved in Bile Acid-Induced Pyroptosis
There are two key executioners, GSDMD and GSDME, known to
trigger pyroptosis. GSDMD is necessary for caspase-1/4/8/11-
induced pyroptosis (Feltham and Vince, 2018; Lee et al., 2018;
Shi et al., 2015) and caspase-3 was recently shown to cleave
GSDME (Rogers et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017). Unexpectedly,
bile acids activated caspase-11 but were incapable of cleaving
GSDMD to a p30 fragment, which is essential for pore formation
in LPS-induced cell lysis (Shi et al., 2014). Instead, GSDMD was
found cleaved to a p43 fragment by caspase-3 in bile acid-
treated cells (Figure 1F). Of interest, bile acids triggered cleavage
of GSDME to a p30 fragment and is dependent on caspase-11
(Figures 1F and 1M). BMDMs isolated from GSDME/ rather
than GSDMD/ mice were resistant to bile acid-induced pyrop-
tosis as evidenced from the test of cell viability, LDH release,
IL-1a secretion, and PI staining (Figures 2A–2F). Consistently,
CASPASE-3- and GSDME-silenced HepG2 cells were also
resistant to bile acid-induced pyroptosis, supporting that
GSDME cleavage by caspase-3 is pivotal for bile acid-induced
pyroptosis (Figure S2). We then asked why GSDMD was not
cleaved in conditions of bile acid-triggered pyroptosis. Our re-
sults showed that pro-caspase-11, in conditions of bile acid acti-
vation, could not bind with GSDMD (Figure 2G), and GSDMD
was instead cleaved by caspase-3 to produce a p43 fragment
(Figure 2H). In contrast, LPS promoted binding of pro-cas-
pase-11 with GSDMD, and in the cell-free system of LPS/cas-
pase-4 incubation, caspase-3 was unable to compete with
GSDMD and thereby incapable of being cleaved by caspase-4
(Figure 2I). These results indicate that pro-caspase-11/4 first in-
teracts with GSDMD upon LPS ligation for subsequent oligomer-
ization and activation, and explains why caspase-11/4 activated
by bile acids cannot cleave GSDMD.
MPT Determines Bile Acid-Induced Pyroptosis
A critical question is how caspase-4 detects the bile acid signal
to trigger pyroptosis. LPS transfected to the cytoplasm directly
binds with caspase-4/11, inducing oligomerization and activa-
tion for triggering pyroptosis (Shi et al., 2014). Microscale ther-
mophoresis (MST) analysis showed that bile acids, unlike LPS,
could not directly bind with recombinant human caspase-4 or
murine caspase-11 (Figure S3A). In the cell-free system, LPS,
but not bile acids, directly activated caspase-4 and -11 (Figures
S3B and S3C). These results indicate that bile acids activate cas-
pase-4 in a manner distinct to that of LPS, and suggest that
certain cell-intrinsic factors might be involved in activation of
caspase-4.
The mitochondrion is a pivotal organelle in controlling cell
fates, and bile acids are known for their effects in inducing mito-
chondrial stress. Bile acid treatment at concentrations capable
of triggering pyroptosis markedly induced MPT as evidenced

mitochondrial membrane (Cle´ menc¸ on et al., 2013; Halestrap

featured with an immediate release of ATP to the cytoplasm
within 30 min post-bile acid treatment (Figure 3N). Cytoplasmic
ATP levels declined to levels lower than the basal level (Figure 3N)
from 60 min post-bile acid treatment, in line with previous reports
that MPT inhibited ATP generation (Cogliati et al., 2013). All these
results support that bile acid-induced pyroptosis may be initi-
ated by ANT-facilitated MPT.

necrosis without clear molecular events (Baines et al., 2005;
Ying and Padanilam, 2016). Our results indicated that MPT trig-
gered by bile acids resulted in caspase-4- and GSDME-medi-
ated pyroptosis. We next asked whether caspase-4 represents
a general sensor of MPT and whether MPT-elicited necrosis
was typically caspase-4-mediated pyroptosis. As expected,
mitochondrial calcium overload induced by thapsigargin (TG)

Apaf-1/Caspase-4/11 Pyroptosome Determines MPT-
Triggered Pyroptosis
Unlike bile acids, chemotherapeutic drugs could not induce MPT
and caspase-4 activation (Figures S5A–S5D). We asked how
MPT activates caspase-4. Caspase-9 and caspase-1 are acti-
vated on a protein complex platform known as apoptosome
and canonical inflammasome, respectively. We hypothesized
that the activation of caspase-4/11 may also necessitate a
protein complex as a scaffold. To this end, we performed an
immunoprecipitation (IP) assay using a caspase-11 antibody to
determine possible interacting proteins. Surprisingly, Apaf-1,
the key adaptorin of intrinsic apoptosis (Hu et al., 2014; Hu
et al., 1998; Pang et al., 2015), was co-precipitated with cas-
pase-11 in BMDMs (Figure 5A). To exclude the involvement of
caspase-9 in mediating the assembly of Apaf-1-caspase-4/11
complex, we performed coIP and His-tag pull-down analysis in
CASPASE-9 knockout HepG2 cells. The results showed that
the interaction of both endogenous and exogenous caspase-4
with Apaf-1 was not affected by CASPASE-9 knockout (Figures
S5E and S5F), supporting that caspase-9 is unlikely involved in
MPT-triggered assembly of Apaf-1-caspase-4/11 complex.

Mitochondrion Differentially Dictates Apoptosome and
Pyroptosome Assembly
A critical question is how cells detect different types/extents of
mitochondrial stress and thereby dictate the choice between
apoptosis and pyroptosis. We hypothesized that different mito-
chondrial stress may lead to distinct modes of caspase activa-
tion. To this end, we first performed a time course study of
caspase activation in typical conditions of MOMP and MPT.
Low dose of bile acids (50 mM) and doxorubicin (DOX), which
are known for inducing MOMP-initiated apoptosis, activated
caspase-9 and caspase-3 time dependently, but not caspase-
11 and caspase-1 (Figures 6A–6C). By contrast, high dose of
bile acids (200 mM), characterized by MPT-triggered pyroptosis,
induced a very quick activation of caspase-11 and caspase-3,
peaking at 60 min post-treatment, while caspase-9 and cas-
pase-1 were not activated in MPT conditions (Figures 6D and
6E). In contrast, intracellular LPS activated caspase-11 and cas-
pase-1 time dependently (Figure 6F). Consistently, bile acids at
low dose promoted Apaf-1/caspase-9 apoptosome assembly
while at high dose promoted Apaf-1/caspase-11 pyroptosome
assembly (Figure 6G).
Time course assay of enzyme activities indicated that the acti-
vation of caspase-4 and -3 induced by 200 mM bile acid treat-
ment was also much stronger than that of caspase-9 and -1 in
HepG2 cells (Figures S6A and S6B). Knockout of CASPASE-4,
but not CASPASE-9, significantly reduced MPT-elicited pyrop-
totic cell death (Figures 6H–6K and S6C–S6E). In addition,
knockout of CASPASE-4 and CASPASE-9 showed no effect
on activity of each other (Figures S6F and S6G), indicating that
these two initiating caspases are differentially activated. In
view of the fact that caspase-8 was recently reported as a
switcher of apoptosis and pyroptosis (Fritsch et al., 2019), we
tested whether it was also implicated in MPT conditions. The re-
sults showed that bile acids did not induce significant release of
TNF-a and TRAIL, and could not activate caspase-8 (Figures
S6H and S6I). Moreover, in comparison with TNF-a/CHX-
induced cell death, which was dependent on caspase-8, CAS-
PASE-8 knockdown had negligible effects on bile acid-induced
cell death (Figures S6J–S6O). These results indicate that cas-
pase-8 is unlikely involved in MPT-triggered pyroptosis. As a
further support, knockout of either BAX or BAK, or both, had
negligible effects on bile acid- and LND-induced and MPT-initi-
ated caspase-4 and -3 activation, GSDME cleavage, and pyrop-
tosis, but largely abolished DOX-induced and MOMP-triggered
caspase-9, -3, and PARP activation, and the resultant cell death
(Figures S6P–S6S). In contrast, DOX-induced apoptotic events
were independent of MPT, as blockage of MPT by BKA showed
no impact on DOX-induced caspase-9, -3, PARP activation,
and cell death (Figures S6T–S6V). All these results support
that MOMP promotes the assembly of Apaf-1/caspase-9 apop-
tosome 	while 	MPT 	mainly 	facilitates 	Apaf-1/caspase-4
pyroptosome.
Because the assembly of both apoptosome and pyroptosome
depends on mitochondrial release of cytochrome c and ATP for
the activation of Apaf-1, we asked how this molecular event is
differently regulated. To this end, a time course analysis of cyto-
chrome c and ATP release was performed. MPT led to an earlier
increase of cytoplasmic cytochrome c in comparison with that by
MOMP (Figure 6L). MPT, but not MOMP, induced an immediate
release of ATP to the cytoplasm (Figures 6M and 6N). Moreover,
caspase-4 recruited to Apaf-1 necessitated a much higher ATP
level than that of caspase-9. Of interest, caspase-4 competi-
tively inhibited caspase-9 activation at conditions with high
ATP level (R2 mM) (Figure 6O), which was in line with the time
course assay of enzyme activities (Figures 6D and 6E).
Next, we asked how Apaf-1 differentially recruits caspase-9
and caspase-4. To provide direct evidence, a fixed amount of pre-
assembled Apaf-1 was incubated with different concentrations of
caspase-4 or caspase-9, and then the protease activity of cas-
pase-4/9 was measured and plotted against the molar ratios of
caspase-4/9 over Apaf-1. Caspase-4 activity reached the
maximum at the molar ratio of approximate 2:7 in contrast with
4:7 for caspase-9 (Figure 6P). Moreover, Kcat/Km of caspase-4
on Apaf-1 pyroptosome scaffold was 22% higher than that of
caspase-9 on apoptosome (Figure S6W). To further determine
the stoichiometric ratio of Apaf-1 and caspase-4 in pyroptosome,
Apaf-1(1–591 aa)-His and caspase-4(CARD)-Fc-HA were incu-
bated to form a stable complex and quantified by size exclusion
chromatography. The molar ratio of Apaf-1 to caspase-4 was
calculated to be 3.43.9 in three independent experiments, which
corresponds to 1.82.1 molecules of caspase-4 for each Apaf
heptamer (Figures 6Q and 6R). Together, these results support
a 7:2 molar ratio between Apaf-1 and caspase-4 in the pyropto-
some platform, which is different from that of the apoptosome
stoichiometry of 7:4 between Apaf-1 and caspase-9 (Figure 6Q)
(Hu et al., 2014). Our results indicate that the differential stoichi-
ometry for Apaf-1 in constructing apoptosome and pyroptosome
in conditions of MOMP and MPT may dictate the choice between
apoptosis and pyroptosis (Figure 6S).

DISCUSSION
Caspase-4/11 is a sensor of intracellular LPS, explaining why mi-
crobial infections trigger pyroptotic cell death. Here, we establish
a causal link of MPT with caspase-4/11 activation and pyroptosis
under sterile conditions. Upon MPT stimulation, cytochrome c
and ATP released into the cytoplasm promote the assembly of
pyroptosome, consisting of Apaf-1 and caspase-4/11, and there-
after the activation of caspase-3, which cleaves GSDME for trig-
gering pyroptosis.
MPT can be induced by calcium overload, oxidative stress,
and the accumulation of some toxic endobiotics like bile acids,
the conditions of which are implicated in many kinds of noninfec-
tive diseases including cholestatic liver failure, ischemic reperfu-
sion injury, and neurologic diseases (Gandhi et al., 2009; Izzo
et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2019). MPT-driven cell death was pre-
viously referred to as regulated necrosis without a clear mecha-
nism. Our study establishes that MPT can be sensed by Apaf-1
pyroptosome to trigger caspse-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis
and provides insights into understanding how mitochondrion
controls pyroptosis, in addition to a previously well-established
role in apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis (Bock and Tait,
2020). Although ANT may not represent an indispensable
MPTP component (Kokoszka et al., 2004), our results support
that ANT1 is an important facilitator in bile acid-induced MPT
and thereafter pyroptotic cell death. ANT1, as an adenine nucle-
otide translocator, may facilitate the immediate release of ATP
from the mitochondria to cytoplasm that is pivotal for the pyrop-
tosome assembly in the cytoplasm.

to be activated in Apaf-1-adapted apoptosome. Here we found
that caspase-4/11 can be also recruited and activated in the
Apaf-1 protein complex, resulting in a facilitated pyroptosis
within 4 h instead of apoptosis, in spite of caspase-3 serving
as the executioner protease in both cases. Therefore, we term
here the protein complex that is assembled in the condition of
MPT as the Apaf-1 pyroptosome. It seems that the more exten-
sive and much earlier activation of caspase-3 triggered by cas-
pase-4 pyroptosome preferably cleaves GSDME to induce
pyroptosis, which is an irreversible process and thereby sur-
passes apoptotic events. Although the exact nature of how cells
differentially dictate the choice between apoptosis and pyropto-
sis warrants extensive future research, our results indicate that
pyroptosis may necessitate much stronger stimuli and higher
threshold. Notably, MPT-elicited pyroptosis takes place at a
much more narrow time window (<4 h) than that for MOMP-
induced apoptosis (usually >24 h), and also chemotherapeutic
drugs in GSDME-positive cells (usually >18 h) (Wang et al.,
2017). Thus, the assembly of caspase-4 pyroptosome may
represent an immediate responsive mechanism for cells detect-
ing an extremely dangerous signal for facilitated removal of
dying cells.
Caspase-4 can be also auto-activated upon ligation of LPS
and thereby cleave GSDMD for triggering pyroptosis. In
contrast, caspase-4 activated in the Apaf-1 pyroptosome prefer-
entially activates caspase-3 and thereby GSDME; GSDMD in
this setting is erroneously cleaved to be an inactive form by cas-
pase-3. We provide evidence that GSDMD is needed to be first
bound with LPS/caspase-4 complex for correct cleavage while
GSDMD cannot be recruited to caspase-4 pyroptosome and
thus cannot be correctly cleaved. It awaits future research at
the atom level to elucidate the exact nature of how caspase-4
is activated in conditions of LPS ligation and in the Apaf-1 pyrop-
tosome as that for caspase-9 activation in apoptosome (Hu
et al., 2014). Such research will also be important for intensive
understanding of how cells dictate the choice between
apoptosis and pyroptosis.
Together with previous findings, our results indicate that cas-
pase-4 can detect danger signals (PAMPs and DAMPs) via
distinct modes either by direct binding or Apaf-1 pyroptosome
assembly. These findings may shed light on understanding
how pathogen infection meets with host-derived DAMPs in
aggravating the pathological processes, and indicate that the
mitochondria have evolved a precise machinery in sensing
different types/extents of stress for culminating appropriate
types of cell death. Moreover, the definition of MPT-driven cell
death as caspase-4-sensed pyroptosis may provide insights
into the development of therapeutics for MPT-driven diseases
such as cholestatic liver failure and ischemia tissue damage.
Limitations of Study
Data collected from this study indicate that the mitochondria
should have evolved a machinery in response to different
extents/types of stimuli in executing MOMP and MPT and
thereby for the choice between apoptosis and pyroptosis. How-
ever, the exact molecular events underlying this choice have not
been clarified in this study. Moreover, extensive research is war-
ranted to structurally understand how the same adaptor Apaf-1
differentially recruits caspase-9 and caspase-4 under different
conditions.

on incision to improve recovery and survival after operation. Mice were kept warm at 37.5C until recovery. Mice were then used for
experiment 7 or 14 days after surgery; survival was monitored every day after surgery until 14 th day. Biomarkers for the liver functions
