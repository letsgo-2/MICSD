Cancer-Cell-Activated In-Situ Synthesis of Mitochondria-Targeting
AIE Photosensitizer for Precise Photodynamic Therapy
Yuanbo Wang,+[a] Shidang Xu,+[a] Leilei Shi, Cathleen Teh,[b] Guobin Qi,[a] and Bin Liu*[a]
Abstract: Maximization of phototoxic damage on tumor with
minimized side effect on normal tissue is essential for effective
anticancer photodynamic therapy (PDT). This requires highly cancer-
cell-specific or even cancer-cell-organelle-specific synthesis or
delivery of efficient photosensitizers (PSs) in vitro and in vivo, which
is difficult to achieve. Herein, we report a strategy of cancer-cell-
activated PS synthesis, by which an efficient mitochondria-targeting
photosensitizer with aggregation-induced-emission (AIE) feature can
be selectively synthesized as an efficient image-guided PDT agent
inside cancer cells. MOF-199, a Cu(II)-based metal-organic
framework, was selected as an inert carrier to load the PS precursors
for efficient delivery and served as a Cu(I) catalyst source for in-situ
click reaction to form PSs exclusively in cancer cells. The in-situ
synthesized PS showed mitochondria-targeting capability, allowing
potent cancer-cell-specific ablation under light irradiation. The high
specificity of PSs produced in cancer cells also makes it safer post-
treatment.
Introduction
Photodynamic therapy (PDT) represents an attractive modality for
oncotherapy since it is spatiotemporally precise, readily
controllable, non-invasive, and with minimum side effects.[1] It
mainly relies on photosensitizers (PSs) to generate reactive
oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide, hydroxyl radical,
singlet oxygen (1O2), etc., to ablate cancerous cells or bacteria.[2]
Although traditional PSs have been successfully used for
biomedical applications, the planar structures of these PSs often
lead to quenched fluorescence and reduced photosensitization in
the aggregate state, which affects their therapeutic
performance.[3] In this regard, PSs with aggregation-induced
emission have attracted much research interest as they could be
designed to show high fluorescence and effective
photosensitization at the same time.[4] However, most of the PSs
do not necessarily localize in the targeted lesions only, and the
unspecific uptake of PSs on skins and in non-malignant tissues
brings in off-target phototoxic damage.[5] Due to modest selectivity
associated with the PSs, cancer patients who underwent PDT
need to keep away from light for extended period of time to avoid
undesired phototoxicity post-treatment.[1a, 6] Hence, researchers
have recently developed activatable PSs which can be activated
for ROS production in targeted lesions for their specific ablation
of cancer cells rather than normal ones.[7] Since ROS-mediated
mitochondria damages and dysfunctions have been reported to
readily induce cell apoptosis,[8] efficient PSs with mitochondrial
targeting feature have recently been designed to target
mitochondria of cells and generate ROS under light irradiation for
potent cell-killing effect.[9] In this regard, efficient activatable PSs
with mitochondria-targeting feature are highly desirable for
effective PDT.[10] However, the complexity involved in the design
of mitochondria-targeted activatable PSs makes it a significant
but challenging research topic.
In addition to the activatable photosensitization, another strategy
to minimize side effect of therapeutics is via in-situ synthesis of
anticancer drugs (e.g. chemodrugs, PSs) inside specific cells,
which allows the localized drugs to perform corresponding
treatment with reduced off-target toxicity.[11] The drugs have been
reported to be intracellularly synthesized from precursors after
cell uptake of both precursors and catalysts.[11b, 12] Regretfully, so
far such in-situ drug syntheses typically lack selectivity between
cancerous and normal cells. In addition, specific delivery of
precursors and catalysts at the same time to the same location
remains challenging.[13] Therefore, the study is limited to cellular
level or the intralesional administration of precursors and
catalysts, which can only be adopted for localized drug synthesis
with great limitations in practical applications.[13] In this regard, a
strategy to realize cancer-cell-specific PS synthesis following
systemic administration of precursors and catalysts at the same
time is in high demand for optimal PDT with practical
significance.[14]
Nanoscale metal-organic frameworks (nMOFs) with active metal
nodes and structural diversity have recently demonstrated their
great potentials to serve as nanocarrier and also as catalytic
system for biomedical applications.[14-15] The structure and
property of nMOFs can easily be modulated through different
combinations of metal inorganic subunits (clusters, chains or
layers of transition metals, etc.) and organic ligands (carboxylates,
phosphonates, azolates, etc.), leading to versatile nMOFs with
unique functions important for living systems.[14b, 14c] For
intracellular drug synthesis, Cu-embedded nMOFs have recently
been designed by Qu’s group to catalyze intracellular anticancer
drug synthesis, which exhibits satisfactory catalytic activity.[13a]
Besides, for drug delivery applications, the high porosity and
oriented structure in nMOFs allows relatively large loading
efficiency of various biomedical cargos.[16] The diverse
functionalities originated from metal ions or organic linkers can

As a proof-of-concept, herein, a design strategy of cancer-cell-
activated PS synthesis is presented, for highly specific cancer
PDT. We designed a simple and efficient mitochondrial targeting
PS with aggregation-induced emission (AIE) (TPATrzPy-3+) as
the image-guided PDT agent. It can be uniquely synthesized from
two photochemically inert precursors, i.e. TPA-alkyne-2+ and
MePy-N3, through a click reaction under the catalysis of Cu(I)
generated from GSH-reduced-MOF-199 (Scheme 1A). As
indicated in Scheme 1B, we loaded both TPA-alkyne-2+ and
MePy-N3 into MOF-199 and coated the precursor-loaded MOF-
199 (PMOF) with F-127 to yield F-127-coated PMOF (PMOF NPs).
In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrate that after intravenous
administration of PMOF NPs, they could be effectively enriched
in tumor, where the two precursors, TPA-alkyne-2+ and MePy-N3
were released from the GSH-collapsed MOF-199 to in vivo
produce TPATrzPy-3+ through click reaction under the catalysis
of Cu(I) generated in the cancerous cells (Scheme 1C). Under
light irradiation, the mitochondria-targeting TPATrzPy-3+ emitted
bright red fluorescence with efficient 1O2 generation for image-
guided cancer-cell ablation.
Results and Discussion
To ensure effective intracellular PS synthesis, precursors with
good water solubility are desirable for ideal dispersity inside cells
to endow the in vivo click reaction between azides and alkynes
with high yields. Besides, in order to realize mitochondrial
targeting for maximized PDT outcome, the precursors should be
designed to carry positive charges.[9b, 9c] It is because owing to the
negative membrane potential of mitochondrial inner membrane,
positively charged compounds have been well-recognized for
superior accumulation in the mitochondrial matrix against their
concentration gradient.[10c] In addition, the PS product itself is
expected to be efficient in 1O2 generation. In accordance with
these requirements, TPATrzPy-3+ was rationally designed as a
PS product to incorporate four parts: an electron-donating
diphenylamine unit, an electron-withdrawing triazole unit, a
pyridinium unit, and two propyl chains with two quaternary
ammonium units at the respective end. The formed D (donor)–A
(acceptor) structure of TPATrzPy-3+ favors HOMO/LUMO
separation to realize a small energy gap between the S1 and T1
state (ΔEst) which facilitates effective intersystem crossing
(ISC).[19] As indicated in Figure S1, compared with TPA-alkyne-
2+ and MePy-N3 as two precursors, TPATrzPy-3+ possesses a
much smaller overlap between HOMO and LUMO to bring a
notably lower ΔEst value of 0.196 eV, suggesting a high ISC rate
for efficient ROS generation.[7a, 19] Moreover, the donor and
acceptor of TPATrzPy-3+ are separated from each other by a
phenyl moiety,[20] which gives rise to a larger torsional angle
between them to further enhance the photosensitization of
TPATrzPy-3+. The quaternary ammonium and pyridinium units in
TPATrzPy-3+ not only provided good water-solubility of the PS
but also offered mitochondria-targeting feature to amplify its ROS-
mediated cell-killing efficacy. In addition, owing to the
triphenylamine unit, TPATrzPy-3+ is expected to show AIE
property,[20] which allows light-up imaging and image-guided PDT.
The lack of acceptor and donor in TPA-alkyne-2+ and MePy-N3,
respectively, ensures negligible ROS generation for both
precursors, which is ideal to show low toxicity during their delivery
to tumor region.

Nanoscale MOF-199 was fabricated via a modified method under
room temperature.[21] The as-synthesized cubic MOF-199 has a
diameter of around 80 nm (Figure S3) with high crystallinity
(Figure 1E). The reduction of Cu(II) to Cu(I) was monitored by
electron spin resonance (ESR) signals of MOF-199 before and
after GSH treatment (Figure 1F). Before GSH exposure, the Cu
nodes in MOF-199 are paramagnetic, indicating that the Cu has
[Ar]3d9 electron configuration and is divalent. In contrast, after
GSH treatment, Cu in MOF-199 became diamagnetic, denoting
that it is with [Ar]3d10 electron configuration and has been reduced
to be monovalent. Such ESR results verify that GSH can reduce
divalent Cu ions in MOF-199 to generate monovalent Cu ions that
could potentially catalyze click reaction between azides and
alkynes. We subsequently loaded the two precursors, TPA-
alkyne-2+ and MePy-N3, in the synthesized nanoscale MOF-199
via de novo approach at a weight percentage of 51.3% (Figure
S4) to yield PMOF. Thereinto, TPA-alkyne-2+ was loaded at a
weight percentage of 42.8%, while MePy-N3 shared a weight
percentage of 8.5% (Figure S5). Transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscope (SEM)
images of PMOF and MOF-199 (Figures S3, S6, and S7)
indicated a similar size and shape of PMOF to those of MOF-199.
The similar trend was also observed in dynamic light scattering
(DLS) studies (Figure S8), which agreed with their SEM and TEM
images. Zeta-potential of PMOF was detected to be slightly higher
than that of MOF-199 (Figure S9), which could be brought by the
encapsulation of the positive-charged molecules by MOF-199.
Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was adopted to
show that azide and alkyne groups were in PMOF (Figure S10),
suggesting the existence of precursors in the as-prepared PMOF.
N2 adsorption and desorption isotherms of PMOF at 77 K were
measured to be greatly decreased, as compared to pristine MOF-
199 (Figure S11), demonstrating that precursors were trapped in
the pores and channels of MOF-199 rather than adsorbed on its
surface. Results of energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS)
in scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) were
shown in Figure S6. Given that MOF-199 itself contains C and O
elements while the loaded precursors exclusively contain N
element, the uniform distribution of N element and good overlap
among C, O, and N elements denoted that precursors were
uniformly distributed in MOF-199 other than being adsorbed at the
surface of MOF-199 particles. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD)
analysis of PMOF before and after GSH treatment (Figure 1E)
indicated that PMOF inherited good crystallinity from MOF-199,
while upon GSH exposure, PMOF lost its crystallinity and almost
became amorphous. This discovery accords with the
phenomenon of GSH-induced MOF-199 decomposition reported
earlier.[18] The degradation kinetics of PMOF in biological milieu
including water, Eagle's minimal essential medium (DMEM), fetal
bovine serum (FBS), phosphate buffered saline (PBS) 7.4, PBS
5.5, and GSH solution were studied (Figure S12). Similar to MOF-
199, PMOF underwent modest degradation in the biological
media except GSH solution for 1440 min (24 h), denoting that
PMOF retained acceptable stability prior to reaction with GSH.
To improve its dispersity and biocompatibility, PMOF was further
encapsulated by F-127 to produce PMOF NPs in which the weight
percentage of F-127 is 18% (Figure S4). For characterizations
and assays regarding PMOF NPs and precursors, concentrations
of PMOF NPs and precursors were based on MePy-N3. PMOF
NPs have a core-shell structure, a hydrodynamic diameter of
around 110 nm, and a zeta-potential of around -15 mV (Figures
1G and S9). After immersed in PBS (1×) 7.4, PBS (1×) 5.5, FBS
7.4, or FBS 6.5 for up to 24 h, PMOF NPs showed modest
changes in hydrodynamic diameters and zeta-potentials, which
could be attributed to the good colloidal stability of both MOF and
PMOF in biological milieu within 24 h (Figure S13). The results
denoted that PMOF NPs are also stable in PBS and FBS under
both neutral and acidic milieus. Both absorption and emission of
PMOF NPs in water are dominated by the loaded TPA-alkyne-2+
with peaks at 335 nm and 435 nm, respectively (Figures 1A and
1B). As indicated in Figure 1D, under light illumination, PMOF
NPs had a similar modest ABDA-degradation rate as the two
precursors, whereas after treatment by GSH, they degraded
ABDA efficiently at a rate of 25 μM mL-1, same as TPATrz-Py-3+
did. Pristine MOF-199 treated with GSH showed very low 1O2
generation efficiency, indicating that the potent 1O2 generation of
PMOF NPs was not originated from MOF-199. To understand the
GSH-activated 1O2 production of PMOF NPs, ESR analysis on
PMOF NPs with or without GSH treatment was performed (Figure
1F), which revealed that in the existence of GSH, divalent Cu ions
in PMOF NPs were reduced to monovalent Cu ions. Besides,
under GSH reduction, PMOF NPs were observed to lose their
core-shell structure and broke into pieces with a greatly reduced
hydrodynamic diameter and increased zeta-potential, as
compared with those of pristine PMOF NPs (Figures 1H and S9).
After the collapse of PMOF NPs, all the loaded precursors and
copper ions were released from MOF-199 upon GSH exposure
for 20 min in similar profiles (Figure S14A), denoting an almost
simultaneous release of all the precursors and copper ions from
PMOF NPs in the presence of GSH. On the contrary, less than
15% of the two precursors and copper ions could be released
from PMOF NPs treated by PBS 7.4 or 5.5 for 24 h (Figures S14B
and S14C). Meanwhile, it was observed that different from free
precursors upon treatment with GSH (Figure S15), only MOF-
199-loaded and GSH-treated precursors could give rise to an
emission centered at around 595 nm in water (Figure 1I). It is
noteworthy that the gradually emerged emission peaks matched
well with that of TPATrzPy-3+ in water (Figure 1B), indicating the
successful in-situ TPATrzPy-3+ synthesis brought by PMOF NPs
in the existence of GSH.

Moreover, the click reaction inside HeLa cells subjected to PMOF
NPs was monitored through HPLC analysis on the cell lysates. As
shown in Figure 2C, the lysate of HeLa cells mixed with MePy-N3,
TPATrzPy-3+, and TPA-alkyne-2+ was firstly analyzed to obtain
the standard curves of absorption peak area vs concentration for
the two precursors and TPATrzPy-3+. Subsequently, HeLa cells
were incubated with PMOF NPs for different time ranging from 4
to 24 h, before the cell lysates were respectively analyzed by
HPLC. From the obtained spectra of the cell lysates at different
treatment time, peak areas of intracellular precursors and
TPATrzPy-3+ at each time point could be calculated as well.
Referring to the standard curves, the specific concentrations of
the precursors and TPATrzPy-3+ in PMOF-NP-treated cells at
different treatment time could be further determined. This also
allows the access of the initial overall endocytosed quantity of the
two precursors before the catalytic click reaction occurred. As
shown in Table S1, the endocytosed quantity of precursors
(based on MePy-N3) reached a maximal value of 5.81 μM per
million HeLa cells 8 h post incubation with PMOF NPs. Based on
the maximal endocytosed quantity, the yield of intracellular
TPATrzPy-3+ synthesis was calculated to be 21.4%, 61.2%,
70.4%, and 70.5% at 8, 12, 16, and 24 h post incubation with
PMOF NPs, respectively (Figure 2C). This indicates that the in
vitro TPATrzPy-3+ production can be completed 16 h post PMOF-
NP incubation with a high yield. It is noteworthy that such a high
yield is because intracellular GSH is depleted by MOF-199 along
with the production of Cu(I) from Cu(II),[18] which mitigates the
coordination between Cu(I) and GSH that can potentially
compromise the catalytic ability of Cu (I) in intracellular click
reaction.

toxicity of PMOF NPs were further examined via the tumor-

liver-tumor-bearing zebrafishes soaked in precursors, PMOF NPs,

greatly reduced unspecific light toxicity because of their cancer-
cell-specific PS generation feature to realize effective image-
guided PDT with minimal side effects.
To examine the long-term toxicity and biodegradation of PMOF
NPs, BALB/c nude mice were intravenously injected with 100 μL
of PBS or PMOF NPs (500 μg mL-1 based on MePy-N3, the same
dosage as zebrafish embryos per unit weight) on Day 0. On Day
18, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and analyses of blood
biochemistry were conducted on the mice. As shown in Figures
S21 and S22, negligible impacts on normal organs and blood
biochemistry of mice treated by the NPs were observed, which
indicated low toxicity of the NPs toward mice at the administrated
dosage. Besides, at 0.5, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post injection, the
blood of each injected mouse was collected for pharmacokinetic
analysis. The results show that PMOF NPs can be degraded by
mice with time and do not tend to accumulate in the body under
the administrated dose (Figure S23), denoting good
biodegradability of PMOF NPs. Along with the in vivo results
found in the zebrafish model, it could be concluded that PMOF
NPs are able to perform effective and precise PDT with low
systemic toxicity.
Conclusion
In conclusion, we have developed PMOF NPs as cancer-cell-
activated PS generators after their systemic administration for
cancer-cell-specific PDT. After intravenously administrated
PMOF NPs entered tumor and cancer cells, Cu(II)-based MOF-
199 in PMOF NPs was selectively reduced by upregulated GSH
to generate Cu(I) in the cancer cells. Simultaneously, GSH-
reduced PMOF NPs collapsed to release the loaded two
precursors, TPA-alkyne-2+ and MePy-N3, to synthesize an
efficient mitochondrial targeting AIE PS, TPATrzPy-3+, under the
catalysis of generated Cu(I) through intracellular click reaction.
Under light irradiation, the synthesized TPATrzPy-3+ could
efficiently generate 1O2 and emit bright red fluorescence at the
mitochondria of cancer cells for potent cancer-cell-specific
ablation with image guidance. Such a “cancer-cell-activated PS
synthesis” strategy accordingly renders image-guided PDT with
both maximal therapeutic effects and minimal unspecific
phototoxicity in tumor-bearing zebrafish studies. Different from
transparent zebrafish embryos, mice and human beings tend to
encounter light penetration issue during phototherapy. However,
the emergence of new concepts and technologies in light delivery
