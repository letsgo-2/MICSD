A General Molecular Structural Design for Highly Efficient
Photopyroptosis that can be Activated within 10 s Irradiation
Qingsong Liu, Xianxian Yao, Lulu Zhou, Wenfeng Wu, Jianshuo Cheng, Zexin Zhang,
Zhongyu Li, Hao Sun, Jian Jin, Man Zhang, Hongwei Wu, Shihui Zhu,* Wuli Yang,*
and Liangliang Zhu*
Photopyroptosis is an emerging research branch of photodynamic therapy
(PDT), whereas there remains a lack of molecular structural principles to
fabricate photosensitizers for triggering a highly efficient pyroptosis. Herein, a
general and rational structural design principle to implement this hypothesis,
is proposed. The principle relies on the clamping of cationic moieties (e.g.,
pyridinium, imidazolium) onto one photosensitive core to facilitate a
considerable mitochondrial targeting (both of the inner and the outer
membranes) of the molecules, thus maximizing the photogenerated reactive
oxygen species (ROS) at the specific site to trigger the gasdermin E-mediated
pyroptosis. Through this design, the pyroptotic trigger can be achieved in a
minimum of 10 s of irradiation with a substantially low light dosage (0.4
J cm−2 ), compared to relevant work reported (up to 60 J cm−2 ). Moreover,
immunotherapy with high tumor inhibition efficiency is realized by applying
the synthetic molecules alone. This structural paradigm is valuable for
deepening the understanding of PDT (especially the mitochondrial-targeted
PDT) from the perspective of pyroptosis, toward the future development of
the state-of-the-art form of PDT.

In this work a general structural design principle for fabricat-
ing photosensitive reagents is proposed, aiming to achieve pho-
topyroptosis and immunotherapy with high efficiency. Our de-
sign relies on the clamp of cationic moieties (e.g., pyridinium
or imidazolium) onto one photosensitive core in the molecu-
lar structure (Figure 1a,b). Therefore, the synthesized molecules
can sufficiently reach both the inner and outer membranes of
the mitochondria via strong electrostatic interactions.[21,22] In this
way the maximized photoproduced ROS at the specific site can
be expected to sufficiently trigger the gasdermin E-mediated py-
roptosis. A series of photosensitizers were synthesized to ex-
tract the universality of structural design (Compound 1a∼1d con-
forming to the structural design, and 2a∼2b, 3, 4 with reference
to the design, see the structures and synthesis procedures in
Figures 1a,2a; Schemes S1–S6 and Figures S18–S26, Support-
ing Information). This structural design is also aimed at improv-
ing the water solubility of the compounds, making them easy to
disperse in cells and in vivo. Meanwhile, photosensitizers with
a broad range of absorption maxima spanning from UV to red
spectral region can be utilized. We eventually chose 1d, which can
be excited by red light with deeper tissue penetration and low en-
ergy, as a representative reagent to study the specific mechanism
as well as for immunotherapy (see illustration in Figure 1c,e,f).
Photopyroptosis efficiency is closely related to the reagent con-
centration, excitation wavelength, and light dosage. Excellent
photosensitizers need to show good biocompatibility with high
light toxicity, long excitation-wavelength responsiveness, and low
active light dosage (light dosage = laser power intensity × irradi-
ation time), [9] to reduce harmful damage of light to biological tis-
sues. We found that 1d was significantly better than the reported
relevant photosensitizers (see a summary of the 3-D diagram in
Figure 1d). [15,23–26] An immunotherapy triggered in vivo with the
sole use of 1d (without any other immunoadjuvants) was then ex-
emplified through photopyroptosis and achieved excellent tumor
treatment efficacy.

In addition to the photosensitive cores, the mitochondrial tar-
geting ability of the molecules is an important consideration. 1a
and 1b both contain the hexathiobenzene moiety as the photo-
sensitive core, which has an absorption band of 200–410 nm
(Figure 2a,b). After their entering cells, cell bubbling was ob-
served from the confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) im-
ages under 405 nm laser irradiation (Figure 2c), and the lactate
dehydrogenase (LDH) significantly released at the fourth-hour
post cell bubbling (Figure 2d). Similarly, 1c and 1d can be ex-
cited by irradiation of 488 nm and 640 nm respectively, because
of their photosensitive cores with longer wavelength absorption
(Figure 2b), and produce similar cellular phenomena (Figure 2c).
These behaviors typically connect to a possible photopyroptotic
process. To highlight the role of the molecular structural de-
sign, we applied a series of reference molecules for comparison
(2a∼2b, 3, 4 with some functional group change, see the struc-
tures and results in Figure 2). Even if the same photosensitive
cores (hexathiobenzene and porphyrin) are used to produce 1 O2
upon irradiation, no pyroptotic phenomena were observed for
2a and 2b (Figure 2c,d). This could be explained by the possible
lack of mitochondria-targeting ability (see also the detailed explo-
ration vide infra) of these compounds without cationic periphery.
Such phenomena were also not observed in cells containing 3,
because this compound has no photosensitive core and cannot
produce 1 O2 under irradiation (Figure 2).
Furthermore, the electrostatic interaction strength was ex-
plored (Figures S2 and S3, Supporting Information). The amide
groups in 1a enhanced the electron withdrawing of the pyrid-
ium salts, resulting in the total Mulliken charge value of the pyri-
dine salts reaching 7.059 (Figure S2, Supporting Information).
This characteristic improved the electrostatic interaction (moni-
tored by the binding constant between ATP, a standard anionic
model, and 1a, K1a = 2.6 × 105 M−1 ; Figure S1e, Supporting Infor-
mation). For compound 4 with the ester group instead, the less
electron-withdrawn ability and a lower sum of Mulliken charge
value reaching 6.466 (see Figure S2, Supporting Information),
cause a relatively weak electrostatic interaction (K4 = 3.3 × 104
M−1 ). To further clarify the generality of the design rule, 1b∼1d,
differing in the cationic moiety or the photosensitive core, were
compared from this perspective. The imidazole cations also en-
sure a strong electrostatic interaction for 1b and improve the
binding constant (K1b = 3.7×105 M−1 ), while the dipyridinium
phenolate (1c) and the multiple pyridinium modified porphyrin
(1d) even have stronger binding constants (K1c = 2.4 × 106 M−1 ,
K1d = 7.4 × 105 M−1 ). As the electrostatic interaction strength
considerably affects the targeting ability between the compounds
and the mitochondria, it can be found that compounds 1a∼1d
with a high electrostatic interaction strength can strengthen the
mitochondrial targeting to facilitate a possible photopyroptosis
(Figure 2c,d). In contrast, a relatively weak electrostatic interac-
tion of 4 may cause insufficient mitochondrial targeting and less
pyroptosis. These control studies confirmed the rationality of the
molecular design of the synthetic reagents.
Photopyroptosis: To further verify the strong electrostatic in-
teraction related to mitochondrial targeting as well as the pos-
sible pyroptotic trigger, the intracellular study was performed.
The mouse breast cancer cell EMT-6 cells were incubated with
1d and Mito-Tracker Red, Lyso-Tracker Red and LD-Tracker Red
(Figure 3a). Compared to the results of other organelle studies,
the Pearson correlation coefficient of the mitochondrial study
(0.91) stands out, fully demonstrating the mitochondrial target-
ing ability of the photosensitizers in our work. To explore a deep
distribution, a highly intelligent and sensitive SIM (HIS-SIM)
imaging was introduced here as a microscope with ultra-high res-
olution. Through HIS-SIM, we can clearly see that molecule 1d
has simultaneously located both the inner and outer membrane
of mitochondria (Figure 3b, Supporting Information), superior to
commercial mitochondrial probes mostly targeting cristae (see
blue pseudocolor remaining after the merging of the HIS-SIM
images; Figure S4a, Supporting Information). On this basis, the
evidence for the mitochondrial ROS photoproduction in the 1d +
L group (cells co-incubated with 1d upon irradiation) was found
by adding a DCFH-DA probe,[31,32] which showed a >16-fold flu-
orescence intensity to the 1d group (cells co-incubated with 1d)
(see the increment of the green pseudocolor in Figure 3c). Since
there was no change in the molecular structure of 1d before and
after irradiation (see exactly the same 1 H NMR relationships in
Figure S4b, Supporting Information), it further suggests that the
ROS photoproduction is a dominant factor. As it is known that
the formation of cristae prodigiously increases the specific sur-
face area of the mitochondrial membrane, it can be concluded
that such considerable targeting caused a centralized ROS pho-
toproduction from mitochondria, which benefits the subsequent
trigger of pyroptosis. Such high mitochondrial targeting and ROS
photoproduction can also be observed from those compounds
that can successfully enable the cell bubbling phenomena (see
those results of 1a for example; Figure S4c,d, Supporting Infor-
mation), whereas less observed from those compounds that can-
not lead to the pyroptosis phenomenon (Figure S4e, Supporting
Information). Moreover, we tested the specific ROS produced in
cells during PDT. As shown in Figure 3d, pre-incubating cells
with NaN3 and Tiron ( 1 O2 and ·O2− specific scavenger, respec-
tively) only caused a slight decrease in cell viability, indicating
that 1 O2 and ·O2− accounted for the main part of the total ROS
induced by 1d. [23] We also tested the cellular uptake of 1d, which
reaches a maximum degree at 10 h (Figure S5, Supporting In-
formation). The release of LDH and the activity of ATP were per-
formed in the control group (untreated cells), 1d group and 1d +
L group. The results showed that the LDH release in the 1d + L
group was significantly higher than that in both the control group
and 1d group (Figure 3e), whereas the ATP activity (Figure 3f) was
reduced and the extracellular ATP content was increased (Figure
S6, Supporting Information). These behaviors remarkably indi-
cated the occurrence of cell death.

ducing more luminescence signals in the Green channel rather
than in the Red one (Figure S7a,b, Supporting Information).
Meanwhile, the nucleus shrunk significantly and a remarkable
cellular contents outflow could be observed with the internal
structure of the mitochondria destroyed (Figure S7c, Support-
ing Information). This proved mitochondrial damage caused by
the possible photopyroptosis. Besides, western blot (WB) data
demonstrated the expression of high mobility group box 1 protein
(HMGB-1) upregulated in the 1d + L group compared with the
other two groups (Figure 3h). These results revealed a successful
pyroptosis upon irradiation for a series of tumor cell lines.[16,17]
As shown in Figure 3i and Figure S8 (Supporting Information),
there was no significant difference in caspase-3 and GSDME pro-
tein levels between the control group and the 1d group. How-
ever, the 1d + L group showed a high level of caspase-3 and ob-
vious GSDME-N expression, indicating the activation of pyrop-
tosis. The activation of effector caspase-3 is able to set off explo-
sive feedback amplification of upstream cell death events,[35,36]
which may be a key factor for efficient pyroptosis. To fully con-
firm the pyroptotic process, a series of tumor cell lines including
EMT-6, mouse liver cancer cell Hepa1-6, and human breast can-
cer cell MCF-7 were chosen as models for further exploration. As
shown in Figure 3j, the dark cytotoxicity of 1d was weak, indi-
cating there was no pyroptosis initially. However, it exhibited re-
markably higher cytotoxicity after irradiation in a concentration-
depended manner (IC50 MCF-7 = 6.67 μg mL−1 , IC50 EMT-6 =
4.24 μg mL−1 , IC50 Hepa1-6 = 13.21 μg mL−1 ).
High Efficiency of the Photopyroptosis: After incubating with
1d for 4 h, all three tumor cells presented bubbling and fi-
nally rupturing under 640 nm light irradiation when observed
by CLSM (Figure 4a; Videos S1–S3, Supporting Information).
We found that the cells under CLSM laser irradiation for only
10 s could swell, accompanied by a membrane bubbling up
(Figure 4b). Even if the laser was turned off, this process still kept
going on in the dark. The nature of the cell death was confirmed
by flow-cytometry analyses of annexin V and propidium iodide
(PI) staining. From the results (Figure 4c), the pyroptosis pro-
portion of EMT-6 cells that co-incubated with 1d continuously
increased after light irradiation (LED light source), and the ma-
jority of cells went to pyroptosis (double-positive cells: annexin
V+/PI+) in 5 h. Moreover, while the co-incubated cells were
placed under a CLSM laser, nearly all the cells appeared bubbling
(see a series of statistical CLSM observations with a large num-
ber of samples in Figure 2c; and a larger number of samples in
Figure S9, Supporting Information). This data suggests that our
photosensitizer can cause the pyroptotic morphology of almost
all the observed cells under laser irradiation at an indicated time
point.[18] The CLSM laser power density required for pyroptosis
could even be reduced from its maximum value (40 mW cm−2 )
to an extremely low attenuation ratio (2 mW cm−2 ), although
longer irradiation period is required for pyroptosis (Figure 4d).
All these results featured high efficiency to trigger pyroptosis by
our strategy. Different cell deaths may coexist under the influ-
ence of cellular cross-linked signaling networks, [9] as caspase-
3 has long been regarded as the hallmark of apoptosis. However,
if the caspase-3 has been used to cleave GSDME by induced ROS
generated from the photosensitizers under light irradiation, the
content of the caspase-3 used to induce cell apoptosis will be
greatly reduced (trade-off), as GSDME is cleaved and this cleav-
age uniquely causes pyroptosis.[17]
The whole photopyroptotic process can also be visualized by
the dynamic signal change of the photoluminescence since our
compounds are typical luminogens.[37] At the beginning of the
pyroptosis, it could be seen that 1d first targeted the mitochondria
by a bright luminescence signal (Figure 4e). Then, it was found
that the luminescence area gradually transferred to the entire cy-
toplasm, because the synthetic reagent was dispersed throughout
the cytoplasm upon the loss of MMP. Subsequently, as the pyrop-
tosis proceeded, the luminescence position was transferred from
the cytoplasm to the nucleus, which is probably caused by the cell
membrane rupturing and the selectivity loss. In the end, due to
the loss of all the cell contents, the luminescence became com-
pletely quenched.
Immune Effect and Immunotherapy: As mentioned above,
HMGB-1 related immune key can be released accompanied by
photopyroptosis. Therefore, it can be considered an effective way
to activate the cellular immune response. For further verification,
transcriptome analysis was performed among control, 1d, 1d +
L cell samples. Venn diagram (Figure S10a, Supporting Informa-
tion) and t-SNE diagram (Figure S10b, Supporting Information)
showed that there were significant differences in the primary
transcripts among these three groups of cell samples. With the
keywords of innate immune response and adaptive immune re-
sponse, the immune-related genes were selected from the gene
ontology database, 38 of which with the largest differences be-
tween control and 1d + L were listed as a heatmap (Figure 5a).
The results showed that these genes in the 1d + L group were up-
regulated compared with the control group. The Kyoto Encyclo-
pedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis (Figure
S10c, Supporting Information) demonstrated that these differen-
tial genes are largely enriched in the cellular immune pathway,
such as the IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway and
NOD-like receptor signaling pathway. The protein-protein inter-
actions network data (Figure S10d, Supporting Information) il-
lustrated that most of these differential genes were involved in
various cellular immune pathways. In addition, the CASP3 and
GSDEM genes in the 1d + L group had an up-regulated trend
compared with other groups (Figure S10f, Supporting Informa-
tion). In conclusion, it is reliable that this photopyroptosis can
induce a cellular immune response, according to these transcrip-
tome analysis data. [38]

3. Conclusion
In this study, a general and rational structural design principle
was developed for the fabrication of photosensitizers enabling a
highly efficient photopyroptosis. The structural factors, with the
integration of photosensitive cores, cationic moieties (e.g., pyri-
dinium/imidazolium for mitochondrial targeting), and strong
electrostatic interactions between the synthetic molecules and
mitochondria, play a significant role in the recognition of the in-
ner and outer membranes of the mitochondria as well as the cen-
tralized ROS photoproduction. Consequently, while under light
irradiation, ROS from mitochondria can be sufficiently utilized
for pyroptosis triggers. The pyroptosis followed by our strategy
can be triggered within 10 s under CLSM laser (0.4 J cm−2 ), prov-
ing to be extremely efficient compared to traditional pyroptosis
methods. Besides, excellent therapeutic efficacy can be attained
by the sole use of photosensitizers. These results could probably
turn over the old notion regarding mitochondrial-targeted PDT,
and would be of significant value for precisely mediating cellular
pathways for benefiting immunotherapy.
