A B S T R A C T
The recognition of pathogen- or damage- associated molecular patterns (PAMPs/DAMPs) signals a series of
coordinated responses as part of innate immunity or host cell defense during infection. The inflammasome is an
assemblage of multiprotein complexes in the cytosol that activate inflammatory caspases and release pro-
inflammatory mediators. This review examines the two-edged sword activity of gasdermin D (GSDMD). Since
its discovery in 2015, GSDMD has played a crucial role in the programmed necrotic type of cell death called
pyroptosis. Pyroptosis is an important response in host self-protection against danger signals and infection.
Although excessive pyroptosis has a deleterious effect on the host, it proves to have a game-changing therapeutic
application against pathogenic invasion when controlled. Here, we explore the mechanism utilized by GSDMD,
the best studied member of the gasdermin protein family, in host immune defense against many bacteria. While
the protein contributes to the clearance of some bacteria, we also discussed results from previous studies and
research, that its presence might hinder effective immunity against other pathogens, thus aiding pathogenic
invasion and spread.
1. Introduction
Gasdermin D (GSDMD) is a crucial mediator of pyroptosis, a lytic
form of cell death that plays an important role in defending host cells at
the onset of pathogenic infection and danger signals (Shi et al., 2015;
Kayagaki et al., 2015; Shi et al., 2017; He et al., 2015). For GSDMD to
perform its role of mediating innate immunity as part of the defense
system, it undergoes cleavage for activation (Ding et al., 2021). The
cleavage of the full length GSDMD is preceded by a series of other
protein actions. Pyroptosis differs from other cell death types such as
apoptosis in that, while there is an eventual loss of membrane integrity
in apoptosis, the onset of pyroptosis is characterized by the rupture of
the cell membrane after swelling, and ultimately cell death (Tsuchiya
et al., 2019). Pyroptosis is triggered by the formation of GSDMD pores in
the membrane (X. Yang et al., 2019; B. Yang et al., 2019). It occurs both
in macrophage and non-macrophage cells after activation of inflam-
matory caspases and is followed by the leakage of cytosolic contents (Shi
et al., 2015). At the onset of an invasion such as microbial infection,
inflammatory caspases (1, 4, 5, and 11), which are usually in the
zymogen form as pro-caspases, are activated. The activation of these
caspases leads to the cleavage of both human and mouse GSDMD at the
Asp276 and Asp 275 regions respectively (Liu et al., 2016; Kuang et al.,
2017). The recognition of the cleavage site on GSDMD by this family of
cysteine proteases involved in cell death regulation is predicated on
their ability to carry out a nucleophilic attack on a protein at the aspartic
acid residue (Tsuchiya et al., 2019). After the cleavage of GSDMD by
caspases, the activated N- terminal domain (GSDMD-N) of full length
GSDMD is separated from the C-terminal domain (GSDMD-C) (Tsuchiya
et al., 2019). Without the separation, GSDMD-C inhibits the activity of
GSDMD-N. Like all other members of the gasdermin family, GSDMD-N is
responsible for its pore-forming activities. The structural stability of the
full-length GSDMD is enhanced by the insertion of the first loop of
GSDMD-C into GSDMD-N. The replacement of this part of GSDMD by a
short linker sequence raises the cell death capacity of GSDMD-N (Kuang
et al., 2017). Upon activation, GSDMD-N moves to the plasma mem-
brane where it lyses lipids such as cardiolipin, forming a pore of about
21 nm in diameter during pyroptosis. The pore formed contains about 33
symmetric protomers.

3. Caspases
Caspases coordinate programmed cell deaths such as apoptosis and
pyroptosis (Man et al., 2017). Since its discovery in Caenorhabditis ele-
gans more than 20 years ago, the functions of caspases in innate im-
munity have been studied in various organisms (Bateman et al., 2021;
Lamkanfi, 2002). Most caspases have a death effector domain or caspase
activation and recruitment domain (DED or CARD) with which they
carry out their activation in protein complexes. The protease domain
contains a large subunit (α) of about 20 kDa and a small 10 kDa subunit
(β) both of which interact closely to form the catalytic subunit (Van
Opdenbosch and Lamkanfi, 2019). A total of fourteen caspases have
been identified, 10 of them are found in mice and 12 in humans (Bate-
man et al., 2021) and these all share some characteristics: they all have a
similar pentapeptide active site, they are all cysteine proteases with a
preference for the aspartate site, and in their zymogen, form are all
referred to as procaspases (Fan et al., 2005). All the procaspases can
either self-activate or activate one another. The activation of inflam-
matory caspases in the plasma membrane leads to the formation of pores
of 1.1 – 2.4 nm (Fink and Cookson, 2006) which ultimately increases cell
permeability to dyes such as 7-Aminoactinomycin and propidium iodide
which are normally impermeable to the cell (Jorgensen and Miao,
2015). Prior studies carried out by Man et al. (2017) on the role of
caspase   1 in cell death induced by bacteria showed that the activation
of pro-inflammatory cytokines pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 is mediated by
caspase-1 where the term pyroptosis was coined in 2001(Man et al.,
2017). Prior classifications grouped caspases into “apoptotic” and
“pro-inflammatory.” However, a greater diversity in function of these
caspases has since been elucidated, with some caspases initially classi-
fied as “apoptotic” now exhibiting some non-apoptotic functions. Thus,
caspases are subdivided into three main groups: “apoptosis activator”
which includes caspases 2, 8, 9, and 10; “apoptosis executioner” which
consists of caspases 3, 6, and 7; and “inflammatory mediators” caspases
1, 4, 5, 11, 12, 13, and 14 (Fan et al., 2005). Generally, inflammatory
caspase activation is induced by the formation of multiprotein com-
plexes known as inflammasomes, which consist of a sensor protein, an
adapter protein, and the inflammatory caspase itself. The sensor pro-
teins may detect a variety of danger signals, including bacterial toxins
and extracellular ATP, and activate the inflammasome pathway. The
activation of the inflammatory caspases is expected to cleave and acti-
vate GSDMD (Man et al., 2017).
Of all caspases, the most studied inflammatory caspase is caspase-1,
which was initially called Interleukin-1 converting enzyme (Bateman
et al., 2021; Pop and Salvesen, 2009). Monomeric procaspase-1 requires
dimerization for its full activity. The dimerization process is regulated
by inflammasomes that assemble in response to insults like pathogen- or
damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs or DAMPs)(Bateman
et al., 2021).
4. Inflammasome in bacteria
Inflammasomes are cytosolic multimolecular signaling platforms
that respond to infection and cellular distress. Using multiple pattern
recognition receptors, they identify PAMPs or DAMPs and then release
pro-inflammatory cytokines while inducing cell death via pyroptosis.
Inflammasomes can respond to an infection in one of two ways: either by
exacerbating inflammation through the release of interleukins 1 and 18
(IL-1β and IL-18), or by activating GSDMD, the primary mediator of
pyroptosis. (Gan et al., 2022; Broz et al., 2012; Rathinam and Fitzgerald,
2016; Downs et al., 2020). There are two major pathways for inflam-
masome activation: the canonical and non-canonical pathways.Fig. 1.

5. Non-canonical inflammasome pathway
Inflammasome activation can also occur through the non-canonical
pathway when the induction of endotoxic shock is triggered by cyto-
plasmic LPS. This is in response to gram-negative bacteria, but not gram-
positive (Hagar et al., 2013; Kayagaki et al., 2011). Human caspase 4/5
and murine caspase 11 are involved in the non-canonical pathway which
occurs when the pathogen bypasses TLR4.
Extracellular LPS can be detected by TLR4 and enter the host cell as
part of a gram-negative bacterial membrane. Intracellular LPS can either
be directly sensed by caspases 4/5/11 or by interaction with guanylate-
binding proteins (GBPs). To activate inflammasome signaling, GBP1
coats the bacterial surface and collaborates with GBP4 to recruit
caspase-11, the mouse counterpart of human caspase 4 and 5, to the
bacterial surface. When GBP4 targets the bacterial membrane, it rup-
tures and bacterial LPS is released into the cytoplasm to activate
caspase-11. Caspase-11 in mice sense special forms of intracellular LPS,
such as pent-acylated and hexa-acylated lipid A, to initiate non-
canonical pyroptosis. The lipid A moiety binds to and oligomerizes
caspase-11 using its CARD domain for activation. Caspase-11 subse-
quently cleaves GSDMD, releasing the GSDMD-N and initiating pyrop-
tosis. Extracellular ATP can also activate caspase-11 by promoting
K+ efflux via the P2X7 receptor-mediated creation of a pore. The efflux
of K+ ions cause a reduction in intracellular K+ concentration, which is
detected by the NLRP3 inflammasome and promotes caspase-11 acti-
vation. The release of ATP from the cell is facilitated by pannexin-1, a
protein channel for ATP release, and GSDMD pore during pyroptosis
(Balakrishnan et al., 2018; Broz et al., 2012; Hagar et al., 2013; Shi et al.,
2017; Kayagaki et al., 2013).

6. Alternative pathway
Recently, an alternative inflammasome pathway independent of
canonical or non-canonical pyroptosis and K+ efflux has been identified
in human monocytes. Unlike the classical inflammasome model which
involves a two-step model to transcribe, translate and mature IL-1β, this
alternative pathway requires only one stimulus. Upon sensing LPS,
TLR4-TRIF-RIPK1-FADD-CASP8 	signaling 	is 	induced, 	triggering
caspase-8 catalytic activity. Subsequently, NLRP3 is activated and fol-
lowed by IL-1β released independent of GSDMD. In this pathway,
caspase-1 matures and cleaves the secreted IL-1β without initiating
pyroptosis. The details of the mechanisms by which caspase-8 activates
NLRP3 have yet to be elucidated. The alternative and classical pathways
both involve maturation and secretion of caspase-1 and IL-1β and utilize
NLRP3-ASC-caspase-1 signaling; however, the alternative pathway
proceeds independently of K+ efflux, GSDMD, and cell death. (Gaidt
et al., 2016).
This alternative pathway presents an uncoupling of inflammasome
activation and pyroptosis,demonstrating greater variation in host cell
defense mechanisms. Notably, this pathway is present in human cells but
not murine cells, suggesting a species-specificity. While studies have
primarily focused on human monocytes, human tissue macrophages
have also demonstrated a capability to activate the alternative pathway.
(Pioli et al., 2006; Wewers and Herzyk, 1989) This suggests that the
pathway plays a wider role in homeostasis and maintenance of sterility.
(Gaidt and Hornung, 2017).
6.1. Bactericidal role of Gasdemin D
6.1.1. GSDMD in Staphylococcus aureus
Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is a major challenge to the health-
care system. It is a pathogen contracted within the hospital environment
referred to as nosocomial infection and known to exhibit resistance to
antibiotic treatment (Lister and Horswill, 2014; Liu et al., 2021). The
severity of S. aureus infection ranges from mild to severe sepsis
(Ahmad-Mansour et al., 2021; Lister and Horswill, 2014). S. aureus is
commonly found in humans and is usually in a harmless form (Cham-
bers, 2001). It can exist in humans as a commensal without posing any
risk and can become pathogenic with about 30% of the human popu-
lation being colonized with it (Tong et al., 2015). Studies have shown
that the number of people who die from S. aureus infection yearly out-
weighs the total number of people whose deaths are attributed to AIDS,
hepatitis, and tuberculosis combined (Van Hal et al., 2012). The path-
ogen is the main cause of diverse diseases such as osteoarticular related
infection and pleuropulmonary infection. It is common for the pathogen
to be transmitted through medical devices implanted in hosts (Lister and
Horswill, 2014; Tong et al., 2015). When a biofilm of the pathogen forms
on host tissues like bones and heart valves, S. aureus can cause chronic
staphylococcal infection and diseases like osteomyelitis and endo-
carditis. The bacterial survival is contingent upon its fastening to the
host’s proteins, which leads to biofilm development. The biofilm de-
velops in three stages: 1) attachment, 2) maturation, and 3) dispersal
(Lister and Horswill, 2014).
The resistance of S. aureus to antibiotics is common amongst the
variant of the infection which could be nosocomial, or community ac-
quired. Some of the strains of the bacteria are resistant to vancomycin,
and vaccines have not yielded any promising outcomes thus far
(McGuinness et al., 2017). Staphylococcal infections occur when there is
a breach in the host’s outermost defense, such as tissue injury. The
infection can be promptly surmounted when a strong host innate im-
munity is leveled against it. However, S. aureus can evade complete
clearance by macrophages that are present within the host cell and
stimulated during infection. When this happens, the pathogen uses the
macrophages as a shield, thus having a complex host-pathogen rela-
tionship which is responsible for its resistance to antibiotics (Pidwill
et al., 2021).
6.1.2. Mechanism of action of GSDMD in S. aureus infection
At the onset of S. aureus infection, inflammatory caspases (1, 4, 5,
and 11) are activated in hosts. The activation of these caspases cleaves
GSDMD after Asp 276 of human GSDMD (hGSDMD) and Asp 275 of
mouse GSDMD (mGSDMD), thereby releasing the GSDMD-Nfrom
GSDMD-C, which confers an autoinhibitory effect on GSDMD-N. The
activated GSDMD-N kills S. aureus without any harm to the bystander
cells of the host. The killing of the pathogen occurs from outside the cell
(Ding et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2016).
In an in vitro study by Liu et al. (2016) it was discovered that before
the GSDMD-N carries out its bactericidal activity on S. aureus, an olig-
omerization of GSDMD-N in the plasma membrane occurs. Inter- and
intra-molecular disulfide bonds were found to play an active role in this
process. After oligomerization, GSDMD-N binds to the membrane lipids
(Liu et al., 2016).
To assess the membrane lipid preference of GSDMD-N, Liu et al.
(2016) incubated various forms of GSDMD, including full-length
GSDMD, GSDMD-N, and GSDMD-C, with liposomes containing various
types of membrane lipids. They also tested the pore-forming proteins
perforin and granulysin in addition to GSDMD.
Kayagaki et al. (2015) studied the membrane lipid specificity of
GSDMD-N. In their investigation, they discovered that GSDMD-N binds
to numerous membrane lipids, such as cardiolipin, phosphatidylinositol
phosphates (PtdInsP), phosphatidic acid, and phosphatidylserine (PS).
Additionally, the authors discovered that GSDMD-N binds most strongly
to cardiolipin and PtdInsP, especially PtdIns(4)P and PtdIns(4,5)P2,
which are mostly located in the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane. In
the meantime, they found that GSDMD-N binds weakly to phosphatidic
acid and PS, both of which are present on the inner and outer leaflets of
the plasma membrane. Moreover, GSDMD-N exhibited no appreciable
binding to the abundant membrane lipids phosphatidylcholine and
phosphatidylethanolamine. It was further discovered that because
GSDMD-N binds only to the inner leaflet phospholipids, the outside of
the plasma membrane is unaffected (Van Meer et al., 2008; Leventis and
Grinstein, 2010).
The activation of the canonical pathway is initiated by gram-positive
or negative bacteria, whereas the non-canonical pathway is triggered by
LPS present on gram-negative bacteria in the cytosol, resulting in
pyroptosis. Upon binding of GSDMD-N to membrane lipids, pores are
formed, leading to the release of pyroptotic cell factors into the phag-
osome. The release of GSDMD-N and other cytosolic contents into the
extracellular space serves two functions, including limiting bacterial
spread and inhibiting the release of active bacteria from pyroptotic cells.
(Liu et al., 2016; Ding et al., 2016).
Chemokine C-X-C motif ligand 1 (Cxcl1) is a member of the che-
mokine family, which directs the migration of inflammatory cells. Cxcl1
facilitates the recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages to specific
sites within various tissues, hence contributing to the host’s immuno-
logical response against infection or injury(Wu et al., 2021). C-X-C
chemokine receptor 2 (CxCr2) acts as a receptor for Cxcl1 (Marro et al.,
2019). Liu et al. (2021) reported that the absence of GSDMD led to se-
vere abscess formation, extensive skin damage, bacterial spread, and
cellular inflammation in a mouse model of S. aureus skin infection.
Interestingly, the overproduction of Cxcl1-Cxcr2 was identified as the
cause of these symptoms, and not the lack of IL-1β. Cxcl1 is a chemokine
that plays a key role in recruiting neutrophils to sites of infection or
injury, while Cxcr2 is the receptor for Cxcl1. Therefore, the results
suggest that GSDMD is crucial for controlling the production of
Cxcl1-Cxcr2 and limiting excessive neutrophil recruitment during bac-
terial skin infection (Liu et al., 2021).
Although there was a reduction in IL-1β in GSDMD deficiency, this
cytokine was not found to be responsible for the severity of the disease
condition. Instead, the increase in the chemokine Cxcl1 due to GSDMD
deficiency led to an exacerbation of the infection caused by S. aureus.
The statement also notes that inhibiting both Cxcl1 and its receptor
Cxcr2 resulted in a significant reduction in bacterial population,

6.1.3. GSDMD in Burkholderia cenocepacia
Burkholderia cenocepacia (B. cenocepacia) is a member of Bur-
kholderia species (Shropshire et al., 2021). B. cenocepacia was initially
recognized as a plant pathogen responsible for onion rot, but further
studies have revealed that it carries out opportunistic infections in
humans (Shropshire et al., 2021). Certain strains of B. cenocepacia have
been noted for their resistance to virtually all known antibiotics.
B. cenocepacia is commonly known as a pathogen that infects the res-
piratory system in people suffering from cystic fibrosis (CF) (Ganesan
and Sajjan, 2012; Hauser and Orsini, 2015). In addition, B. cenocepacia is
also responsible for causing infection in individuals suffering from
various conditions resulting from the compromise of the immune sys-
tem, such as chronic granulomatous disease. For individuals suffering
from CF, B. cenocepacia infection can lead to a life-threatening condition
referred to as Cepacia syndrome. Cepacia syndrome is marked by
deteriorating respiratory failure, thriving bacteria in the blood known as
bacteremia, as well as necrotizing inflammation of the lungs (Hauser
and Orsini, 2015). GSDMD-N has been shown to disturb the mitochon-
drial membrane of the cell and promote reactive oxygen species (ROS) to
clear B. cenocepacia in phagosomes.
6.1.4. Mechanism of action of GSDMD in B. cenocepacia infection
Inflammatory incidences trigger pattern recognition receptors such
as absent in melanoma 2-like receptors (ALRs), Pyrin, and NLRs. These
are activated by the recognition of PAMPs and DAMPs. NLRP3 (the best-
studied NLR), ASC, and caspase-1 make up the NLRP3 inflammasome.
The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome causes a caspase-1-
dependent GSDMD pyroptosis and the release of cytokines (Samir
et al., 2019; Qiu et al., 2022). Mitochondrial dysfunction can lead to the
disruption of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and ROS gen-
eration. However, the relationship between disrupted MMP and
increased ROS in the context of pyroptosis is not fully clear. (Weinberg
et al., 2015). Mitochondrial ROS promotes an oxidative change in both
hGSDMD and mGSDMD at specific cysteine residues. The modification
has been suggested for ROS-mediated pyroptotic cell death (Wang et al.,
2019).
Previous studies have shown that GSDMD enhances survival of some
bacteria during infections (Estfanous et al., 2021). A study investigating
the role of GSDMD in B. cenocepacia found that GSDMD is involved in the

6.1.5. GSDMD in Streptococcus pneumoniae
Streptococcus pneumoniae is another opportunistic bacterial pathogen
that is known for its contribution to several invasive disease conditions
(Xu et al., 2021; Gou et al., 2022). It gains access to the apex of the throat
from which it spreads to other parts of the body. It is the leading cause of
meningitis and bacterial pneumonia. Other conditions caused by
S. pneumoniae infection include otitis media, bacteremia, sinusitis, and
bronchitis. These infections are predominant in patients presented with
immune deficiency, as well as the elderly and young children (Enghølm
et al., 2017). The bacterial genetic factor is responsible for the vulner-
ability and severity of the infection in its host (Piet et al., 2014). The
mortality and morbidity rate of S. pneumoniae infections is hinged on its
resistance to antibiotics (Ibrahim et al., 2004; Gou et al., 2022). One of
the life-threatening diseases caused by S. pneumoniae is pneumococcal
pneumonia which has been found to increase morbidity and mortality
globally, especially in children less than age 5 (Kim et al., 2015; Gou
et al., 2022). Some strains of S. pneumoniae have been demonstrated to
activate caspase-1, cleave GSDMD, and promote pyroptotic cell death.
6.1.6. Mechanism of action of GSDMD in S. pneumoniae infection
Wang et al. (2022) showed that while GSDMD-induced pyroptosis
plays a beneficial role in eliminating intracellular bacteria such as Sal-
monella typhimurium, its function in pyogenic bacteria is deleterious to
the host cell because it increases tissue inflammation and reduces
pathogen clearance. The absence of GSDMD promotes the killing of
bacteria by macrophages (Miao et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2022). Intra-
cellular bacteria are exposed to additional phagocytes during pyroptotic
cell death of macrophages, thus enhancing the eradication of the path-
ogens. S. pneumoniae infections are characterized by excessive pyrop-
tosis which causes sepsis (Gou et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Miao et al.,
2010). To inhibit the exacerbating effect of GSDMD on the infection,
IL-6, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, plays a protective role during
pneumococcal infection (Yousif et al., 2021; Gou et al., 2022). The
mechanism involved in the defense of the immune system by IL-6 during
S. pneumoniae infection is the inhibition of caspase-1 activated
GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis (Gou et al., 2022).
Pyroptosis in S. pneumoniae infection occurs through the activation
of caspase-1. IL-6 plays a vital role in immune response and
S. pneumoniae infection is characterized by an increase in the level of IL-
6 proteins and messenger RNA (mRNA) in the plasma and lungs of
infected individuals (Yousif et al., 2021; Jwa et al., 2018). The level of
IL-6 may either alleviate or exacerbate the infection (Narazaki and
Kishimoto, 2018).
Jorgensen and Miao (2015) reported that in response to a pathogenic
invasion, there is a release of antimicrobial molecules and infections are
cleared after a pyroptotic response is initiated. However, anomalies may
occur within the cytosol resulting in an aggravated infection because of
improper pyroptosis (Jorgensen and Miao, 2015). GSDMD-mediated
pyroptosis exacerbates S. pneumoniae infection by enhancing the pro-
duction of IL-1β and 1 L-18 resulting in the death of the host cells and the
survival of the pathogen. In order to avert the deleterious effect of
pyroptosis, 	IL-6 	negatively 	inhibits 	the 	caspase-1 	mediated
GSDMD-IL-1β signaling which ultimately reduces the level of injurious
lung inflammation during S. pneumoniae infection (Gou et al., 2022).
6.1.7. GSDMD in Helicobacter pylori
Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori), a significant contributor to the devel-
opment of gastric cancer, is responsible for one of the most widespread
infections in the human population (Camilo et al., 2017). It invades the
gastric mucosa, giving rise to chronic inflammation which results in
peptic ulcer or gastric cancer. It is interesting to note that the vulnera-
bility of the host and the pathogenicity of the bacteria contribute to the
extent of the inflammation and ultimately determine whether an indi-
vidual will develop peptic ulcer or gastric cancer as a result of Heli-
cobacter bacteria infection (Camilo et al., 2017).
The inflammation of the gastric epithelium caused by H. pylori is
initiated by cytotoxin associated gene A (cag A) and vacuolating cyto-
toxin A (Vac A) (Kumar and Dhiman, 2018). Apart from being the main
cause of gastric cancer, infections caused by H. pylori can be responsible
for a decline in cognitive function in older individuals (Tian et al., 2022).
Studies have shown a close relationship between depression and
H. pylori. The proof of this relationship is a reduction in immune effi-
ciency observed in people with a stressful lifestyle that renders them
susceptible to infection (Quraan et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2022). H. pylori
infection leads to improper absorption of nutrients that are important for
proper cognitive function, leading to depression (Takeoka et al., 2017).
H. pylori promotes pyroptosis upon detection through the activation
of NLRP3 inflammasome a,caspase-1, and GSDMD.
6.1.8. Mechanism of action of GSDMD in H. pylori infection
H. pylori infection is characterized by the release of innumerable
mediators of inflammatory responses such as IL-8, growth-regulated
oncogene-α, tumor necrosis factor-α, IL-6, IL-12, interferon-γ as well as
macrophage chemotactic protein-1 spreading to tissues distant from the
site of initial attack (Alvarez-Arellano and Maldonado-Bernal, 2014). A
wide range of proton pump inhibitors and antimicrobial agents such as
clarithromycin, levofloxacin, and amoxicillin have been used to combat
H. pylori infection (Kang et al., 2008). To protect host cells during
inflammation, a multiprotein complex of inflammasomes activates
caspase-1 which in turn causes the maturation of cytokines, thereby
inducing pyroptosis (Franchi et al., 2012). The expression of GSDMD
and NLRP3 intensifies in the tissue of patients diagnosed with gastric
cancer because of H. pylori infection.
In H. pylori infection, the pathogenic infection is sensed by NLRs,
specifically NLRP3, which forms NLRP3 inflammasome activation and
activates caspase-1. Caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD, and pro-inflammatory
cytokines, IL-1β and IL-18 are activated and released. Pore formation
as a result of the adherence of GSDMD to the membrane and the release
of the inflammatory cytokines leads to host cell death which intensifies
inflammation (Xie et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2015; Man et al., 2017).
Rabeprazole is a proton pump inhibitor used in treating acid-related
diseases like gastric ulcers, non-erosive reflux disease, etc. (Ochoa et al.,
2020) It has been found to inhibit pyroptosis by its negative regulation
of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 which are responsible for
the expression of GSDMD. Rabeprazole’s anti-pyroptotic effect proves
promising against H. pylori (Xie et al., 2021).
6.1.9. GSDMD in Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Both developed and developing countries have recorded elevated
tuberculosis (TB) incidence in recent years because of widespread
strains of drug-resistance and HIV infection (Cole et al., 1998; Russell,
2001). The survival of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis) in the
host cell depends extensively on its adaptability to different environ-
ments as a result of its thick cell wall and fatty acid catabolism (Russell,
2001; Richard-Greenblatt and Av-Gay, 2017). It can disrupt the trans-
portation of subcellular macromolecules in infected phagocytes. It in-
hibits the process that enhances the elimination of microorganisms by
macrophages (Russell, 2001).
The advancement of TB from its infectious stage to latency, activa-
tion, spread and immunosuppression is dependent on the inclination of
M. tuberculosis to enter the host phagocyte where it is safely stored in the
autophagosome (Deretic and Fratti, 1999). Latent M. tuberculosis can
reside in the deep tissues and continues for a very long time, extending
to several years before becoming an active tuberculosis infection. Ac-
cording to research, about one-third of the global population is infected
with latent M. tuberculosis. The growing number of latent TB carriers

6.1.10. Mechanism of action of GSDMD in M. tuberculosis infection
After the activation of pattern-recognition receptor on the myeloid
cells, pyroptosis is observed after M. tuberculosis infection (Beckwith
et al., 2020). After the M. tuberculosis pathogen has invaded the host cell
and defeated the immune response by the host, it initiates the canonical
inflammasome activation through a potassium efflux (Beckwith et al.,
2020). Sebastian et al. showed that in M. tuberculosis infection,
NLRP3/ASC/Capase-1-associated inflammasome activation is depen-
dent on the susceptibility of the mitochondrial membrane to perme-
ability. However, this is not a requirement for pyroptotic cell death.
They showed that in the case of M. tuberculosis infection, the activation
of GSDMD is not required before IL-1β is released. Prior to this, GSDMD
was viewed as pivotal to both pyroptosis and the release of cytokines
such as IL-1β and IL-18(Liu et al., 2016).
AIM2 and NLRP3 have been reported to be responsible for the release
of IL-1β during M. tuberculosis infection of the macrophage. A protein
secreted by M. tuberculosis called EST12 triggers NLRP3 or GSDMD-
mediated pyroptotic signal to facilitate the clearance of the pathogen
by interacting with activated C kinase 1 receptors. Previous studies show
that M. tuberculosis survival is observed in the absence of EST12. The
bacteria evade pyroptosis by producing PKnF, a phosphokinase which
enhances the inhibition of NLRP3, Caspase-1 or GSDMD-dependent
pyroptosis. Another evasion mechanism by the pathogen is the inhibi-
tion of AIM2 inflammasome. The bacteria can also promote excessive
pyroptosis of the host cell which allows it to grow and spread causing
lung inflammation. Currently, the mechanism of action of GSDMD-
mediated pyroptosis in TB is unclear (Feng et al., 2022; Beckwith
et al., 2020; Balakrishnan et al., 2018; Rastogi et al., 2021). As opposed
to other intracellular bacteria, research has shown that pyroptosis is a
major necrotic cell death identified in M. tuberculosis as its pathogenic
infection mechanism. The bacteria have a relatively thick and imper-
meable cell wall (Sharma et al., 2021). M. tuberculosis utilizes NLRP3
inflammasome activation in carrying out mitochondrial damage to its
host cells. The damage is induced by the activation of GSDMD, a pore
forming effector protein and the result is the spread of the bacteria,
hyperinflammation, and ultimately, tissue damage (Dorhoi and Kauf-
mann, 2016; Cambier et al., 2014; Theobald et al., 2021).
6.1.11. GSDMD in Francisella novicida
Francisella novicida (F. novicida) is a member of the Francisellacae
family closely related with F. tularensis which is an extremely virulent
pathogen. They are a class of gram-negative, facultative intracellular
organisms responsible for Tularemia, a zoonotic disease that is highly
infectious (Kazemzadeh et al., 2021; Watanabe et al., 2022). The bac-
teria are regarded as a biothreat due to their infectivity and high mor-
tality rate (Kazemzadeh et al., 2021). F. novicida’s activity is weak in
humans when compared to its virulent counterpart F. tularensis, and its
mechanism is not well understood (Kinkead et al., 2017). The bacteria
infect through inhalation, ingestion, or transmission from an infected
animal. They are highly proliferating once they get released into the
cytosol after disrupting the phagosomes (Kazemzadeh et al., 2021). The
infectivity of this pathogen with intracellular parasitic abilities burgeons
as the infected cells are either subjected to pyroptosis or apoptosis
(Kazemzadeh et al., 2021).
GSDMD has been demonstrated to promote F. novicida death in cells.
Upon cleavage by caspase-1, the liberated GSDMD-Nattacks and kills
F. novicida, clearing its infection.
6.1.12. Mechanism of action of GSDMD in Francisella novicida infection
AIM2 plays a vital role in autoimmune diseases and in microbial
infection owing to its ability to identify double stranded DNA from
Francisella, MCMV and vaccinia virus and host cells(Zhu et al., 2018). In
response to cytosolic nucleic acid, macrophages activate cysteine pro-
tease caspase-1 in the inflammasome complex leading to the release of
IL-1β and IL-18 which play vital roles in innate immunity. The lack of
AIM2 makes this process impossible (Jones et al., 2010). AIM2, ASC and
caspase-1 form an inflammasome complex which affects the maturation
of IL-18, IL-1β as well as pyroptosis in F. novicida infection (Jones et al.,
2010; Zhu et al., 2018). The formation of membrane pores in the pres-
ence of GSDMD stimulates the release of IL-1β and IL-18. Although
GSDMD plays a significant role in host defense against F. novicida
infection, it requires activation through the cleavage of its N- terminal
from its C-terminal. The cleavage is facilitated by activated caspase-1,
releasing the active GSDMD-N from GSDMD-C. The presence of phos-
pholipids and cardiolipins in the membrane and mitochondria of the
bacteria makes it susceptible to death through pyroptosis (Chen et al.,
2016; Ding et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2018; X. Liu et al., 2016). The acti-
vated GSDMD-N then attacks F. novicida in the cytosol, causing bacte-
riolysis and the release of the bacteria DNA(Zhu et al., 2018). GSDMD is
further cleaved by the activated AIM2 inflammasome.
Summarily, in vivo investigation revealed that the significance of
GSDMD in clearing F. novicida infection is initiated by the cleavage of
the autoinhibitory form of GSDMD by caspase-1 into its active GSDMD-N
form. The active GSDMD permeates cell membrane and mitochondria of
F. novicida because of the presence of cardiolipins and pores are formed.
Further cleavage of GSDMD is enhanced by AIM2 inflammasome. The
pores formed by GSDMD in the bacterial membrane eventually led to
bacterial clearance through cell death (Zhu et al., 2018; Wang et al.,
2018; Wang et al., 2006; Ding et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2016).
6.1.13. GSDMD in Candida albicans
Candida albicans (C. albicans) is a microbe that can be found in the
human body, and in fact, its population can be larger than that of host
cells. In healthy individuals, C. albicans is usually harmless, but when the
immune system is compromised, it can cause a wide range of infections,
including mucous membrane-related infections or even life-threatening
blood infections. These infections are collectively referred to as candi-
diasis. (Tsui et al., 2016).
C. albicans have resisted antifungal treatments, thereby making them
a major health challenge because of their biofilm-forming ability. Bio-
film formation is a very important survival mechanism utilized by many
microbes and signifies a successful strategy utilized by organisms for
their survival (Soll and Daniels, 2016). They are made on both biotic and
abiotic surfaces and provide resistance to the organism against both the
host cell immunity and drugs that have antimicrobial activities (Atriwal
et al., 2021). The biofilms formed by C. albicans confer virulence on it
which makes it highly invasive in infecting the host cells (Atriwal et al.,
2021). The pathogenic activity of this organism is largely favored by its
commensal relationship in the host cells where it establishes itself
through proliferation in different areas of the human body. Suppression
of the host immunity enhances the deteriorating effect of this opportu-
nistic pathogen (Tsui et al., 2016). It can invade the bloodstream by
adhering to medical implants.
C. albicans infection promotes pyroptotic cell death and detailed
studies is needed to show direct correlation with NLRP3, caspase 1, and
GSDMD.

One of the major health concerns associated with C. albicans is
keratitis which results in visual impairment, and in some cases, blind-
ness (Maharana et al., 2016). Candida species can further complicate
corneal grafts as an opportunistic pathogen (Wang et al., 2020). Upon
infection, inflammatory responses are signaled by PRRs which leads to
further signaling and ultimately NLRP3 inflammasomes are activated,

Generally, the cleavage of GSDMD signifies C. albicans invasion

innate and adaptive immune responses. GSDMD plays a crucial role in

7. Conclusion
GSDMD is a double-edged sword in combating bacterial infection. It
has been reported to play a crucial role in the clearance of pathogens
while posing no harm to host cells. However, excessive, and uncon-
trolled pyroptosis, mediated by GSDMD can have a very deleterious
effect by exacerbating pathogenesis rather than alleviating it. Sum-
marily, the effect of GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis and the presence of
GSDMD in general in some bacterial infections has been summarized
along with its benefits and detriments. Nevertheless, further studies are
needed to elaborate on the potential of GSDMD in other pathogenic
infections which may pave the way for therapeutic advancements in
drug discovery.
