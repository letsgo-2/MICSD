Abstract: Ferroptosis regulates cell death through reactive oxygen
species associated lipid peroxide accumulation, which is expected to
affect the structure and polarity of lipid droplets (LDs), but with no clear
evidence. Herein, we report the first example of LD and nucleus dual-
targeted ratiometric fluorescent probe of CQPP for monitoring polarity
change in cellular microenvironment. Due to the appropriate donor-
acceptor structure of CQPP, it offers ratiometric fluorescence
emission and fluorescence lifetime signals to polarity variations. Using
nucleus imaging as a reference, CQPP has been successfully applied
to report the increase in LD polarity, the homogenization of polarity in
LDs and cytoplasm in the ferroptosis model. This LD and nucleus
dual-targeted fluorescent probe shows great potential of using
fluorescence imaging to study ferroptosis and ferroptosis-related
diseases.
Introduction
The recently discovered pathway of regulating cell death,
ferroptosis, was found to be the nexus between redox biology,
metabolism and human health.[1] Dysregulated ferroptosis is
associated with a variety of physiological and pathological
processes, including degenerative diseases (i.e. Alzheimer), T-
cell immunity, neurodegenerative diseases, and even cancer.[2] In
cancer, ferroptosis has been shown to contribute to the tumor
suppressive function of p53,[3] which indicates that during a
specific period of tumor development, triggering ferroptosis can
be used for cancer treatment. Ferroptosis can also regulate cell
death by deactivation of lipid repair enzyme glutathione
peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and subsequent accumulation of lipid-based
reactive oxygen species (ROS).[2a, 4] Due to the excess lipids exist
in the lymphatic system, cancer cells often preferentially
metastasize through the lymphatic system to resist the excessive
oxidative stress and ferroptosis.[5] The polarity of subcellular
organelles, such as lipid droplets (LDs) therefore reflects the
interaction activity between enzymes and proteins.[6] Excessive
accumulation of lipid hydroperoxides, could trigger rapid response
of LDs, which leads to changes in LD structure and polarity.[7]
Although it is important to understand ferroptosis in order to treat
ferroptosis-related diseases, there is currently no report on the
monitoring of LD polarity change or visualization of how LDs
behave during ferroptosis.
Fluorescence imaging is widely used to track changes in
subcellular behavior and intracellular signaling factors.[8] Recently,
Ma and co-workers reported a fluorescent probe H-V to reveal
that ferroptosis is accompanied by significant •OH generation,
and cytoplasmic viscosity increase was confirmed via
fluorescence imaging.[9] Using brain fluorescent imaging, Qian et
al. revealed that apigenin could relieve the myeloperoxidase-
mediated oxidative stress and inhibit ferroptosis.[10] Among the
fluorescence imaging technology, ratiometric fluorescence
imaging and fluorescence lifetime imaging (FLIM) are some of the
most powerful tools that can be used to quantify the cellular
microenvironment. Ratiometric fluorescence imaging can
accurately quantify the fluctuations of various signals in
organisms and living cells, due to its advantages over traditional
fluorescence imaging.[8c, 11] In addition, FLIM can not only reflect
the changes of microenvironment but also quantify the signal
change in organisms and living cells with high accuracy, since the
fluorescence lifetime of fluorophores is dependent on
environment rather than on concentration.[12] However, designing
single-molecule fluorescent probes that can provide ratiometric
fluorescence response and fluorescence lifetime response of
polarity presents its own challenges, due to the difficulties in
precise regulating the hydrophilicity/lipophilicity and polarity
sensitivity of the probe. Indeed, to the best of our knowledge, no
literature has reported ratiometric fluorescence response and
fluorescence lifetime response of polarity in LDs.
On the other hand, cells that induce ferroptosis often exhibit
unique morphological changes at the cellular and subcellular
levels. Electron microscope imaging confirmed that cells treated
with erastin or RSL3 (ferroptosis inducer) had noticeable changes
in mitochondrial morphology, such as the loss of structural
integrity; in contrast, the structural integrity of the nucleus was
retained, and no nuclear condensation or chromatin margination
was observed following erastin treatment in cancer cells. [1-2, 13]
Although the morphology of some subcellular organelles has
been characterized separately during ferroptosis, the single
subcellular organelle morphological characteristics are not
completely different from other death pathways, especially
apoptosis.[1-2] Therefore, in-situ detection of multiple organelles
(e.g., LDs and nucleus) and in-depth exploration of the sub-
organelle interaction is of great significance to accurately monitor
the occurrence and progress of ferroptosis. Herein, nucleus
imaging is also of interest because ferroptosis does not involve
nucleus change, but apoptosis does.[2c] Unfortunately, materials

Results and Discussion
Optimized molecular structure, balanced
lipophilicity/hydrophilicity and high nucleic acids binding ability are
necessary factors to consider for the design of probes targeting
LDs and nucleus at the same time. On the one hand, the coumarin
unit, similar to Nile Red (a commercial LDs dye) was often used
in the design of LD probes.[14] On the other hand, cationic
quinolinium can interact with nucleic acids via electrostatic
interaction.[15] The integration of both was considered for the
design of dual-targeting probes for LDs and nucleus (Figure1A).
In addition, fluorophores with donor (D)–π–acceptor (A) molecular
structures are susceptible to solvent polarity change, which has a
significant effect on their fluorescence. Increasing the
environment polarity usually results in a red-shift of fluorescence
emission of the D–π–A molecules (Figure1B), which provides an
opportunity for polarity detection. Considering the above, the
coumarin and cationic quinolinium unit were used to design a D-
π-A structure CQPP to simultaneously visualize the LDs and
nucleus in dual channels, and to monitor the microenvironment
polarity changes. A further extended aromatic system with a 1-
(pyridin-4-yl)piperazine group has also been designed to mimic
Hoechst 33342 partially, which may not only improve its ability of
binding to nucleic acids, but also ameliorate its ratiometric
fluorescent properties for sensitive detection of polarity (Figure
1A). By applying its LD/nucleus dual-targeting and sensitive
polarity response properties, the LD polarity increase,
homogenization of polarity in LDs and cytoplasm were observed
in the ferroptosis process model for the first time.

Briefly, as shown in Figure 1C, the quinolinium onium
derivatives (2) can be simply prepared according to the
reference.[16] A mixture of compounds 2 and 3 in 1-butanol was
heated to reflux by nucleophilic substitution for 12 h. Without any
treatment, compound 5 [17] was added and reacted for additional
12 h under reflux. After cooling to room temperature, the mixture
was filtered and washed with ethanol to yield the product of CQPP
in 54% yield. The synthetic details and characterizations of CQPP
are described in the supporting information in Figure S1-3, which
reveal its right structure. The HPLC and ESI-MS spectra collected
under different conditions (Figure S4) proved the high purity and
the good stability of CQPP.
CQPP has two similar absorption peaks (λabs = ~295 nm and
λabs = ~540 nm) in both 1,4-dioxane and DMSO. However, the
fluorescence spectra are quite different (λem = 467 nm for 1,4-
dioxane, and λem = 671 nm for DMSO) under the same excitation
(λex = 405 nm) (Figure 2). What’s more, with the increasing solvent
polarity, the fluorescence of CQPP at ~470 nm is gradually
decreased, which is accompanied by a new fluorescence
emission peak at ~670 nm (Figure S5). The linear relationship of
concentration-dependent absorption in Figure S6 indicates that
CQPP does not undergo self-aggregation even at high
concentrations (80 μM). Comparison between the excitation
spectra of CQPP at the two emission maxima (λem = 470 nm, and
λem = 670 nm) in 1,4-dioxane and DMSO and that of a coumarin
derivative indicated that the short-wavelength excitation band
comes from the coumarin group (Figure S7 and S8). The density
functional theory (DFT) calculations in 1,4-dioxane and DMSO
indicate that the long-wavelength absorption band should be
attributed to the extended π ‐ conjugation induced strong
intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) from the coumarin electronic
donor to the cationic quinolinium acceptor moiety[18] (Figure S9).
The obtained dual fluorescence in different media is due to the
locally excited (LE) state and ICT state in equilibrium, respectively.
Since a polarity-sensitive unit was equipped with CQPP, the
increase in polarity could result in reduced LE emission and
enhanced ICT emission, so that CQPP could present a ratiometric
fluorescence response in media of different polarity (Figure 1B).

The fluorescence response of CQPP to nucleic acids was also
studied in detail. As shown in Figure 3E, compared with the

To explore the application of CQPP in living cell imaging, its
cytotoxicity was firstly testified using MTT assay in LO2, A549,
and HT-1080 cells. As shown in Figure S15, CQPP has negligible
cytotoxicity to the living cells in the concentration range of 0 - 64
μM for 24 h. After that, HT-1080 cells were selected for the LD
and nucleus imaging. As shown in Figure 4A, after 2 h incubation,
the dotted green fluorescence (λem: 470 ± 20 nm) was presented
in the cytoplasm, while bright red fluorescence (λem: 670 ± 20 nm)
was detected in the nucleus. The two fluorescence channels with
different emission of CQPP were not only detected by one-photon
microscopy (OPM) but also two-photon microscopy (TPM)
images (Figure S16), which provided convenience for two-photon
fluorescence lifetime imaging. Further co-localization experiments
using BODIPY 493/503 (a commercial probe for LDs imaging) and
Mito-Tracker Red (mitochondrial probe) demonstrated that the
green emission of CQPP was mainly distributed on LDs in the
cytoplasm with a Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) of 0.92,
rather than mitochondria (Figure 4B and Figure S17). As shown
in Figure S18, upon addition of oleic acid to induce more LD
production in cells,[7c] more dotted green emission could be clearly
observed in the cytoplasm. The fact that more LDs were stained
by green fluorescence further indicated that CQPP could be
selectively enriched on LDs. When switching the fluorescence
channel of CQPP to the red emission (λem: 670 ± 20 nm), the co-
localization with Hoechst 33342 (PCC of 0.91) verified that the red
emission of CQPP was presented in the nucleus (Figure 4C). The
lipophilicity determined by the octanol/water partition coefficient
(Po/w)[25] for the Nile Red, CQPP, and Hoechst 33342 are 1.75, -
1.80, and -3.15, respectively (Figure 4D). Appropriate
lipophilicity/hydrophilicity of CQPP offers its LDs/nucleus dual-
targeting characteristics.[26] It is worth mentioning that because
the large wavelength difference (Δλem ~200 nm) between green
and red emission easily avoids the crosstalk in fluorescence
imaging, which provides conditions for CQPP to simultaneously
offer 3D dual-color imaging of LD and nucleus under the same
excitation conditions (Figure 4E). CQPP also shows a high
photobleaching resistance in buffer solution (Figures S19 and
S20), and excellent chemical stability in biological media (Figure
S21).
To monitor the LD polarity change during ferroptosis process,
the fluorescence characteristics of CQPP were explored by
incubating the ferroptosis-susceptible cell line, HT-1080 cells,
with ferroptosis inducer (Erastin or RSL3) under different
conditions[9, 27], followed by CQPP treatment. Fluorescence
images were simultaneously collected in both green (~470 nm)
and red (~670 nm) channels. Subsequently, the signal of the
green channel was divided by the signal of the red channel to get
the ratiometric signal (Figure 5A). As shown in the ratiometric
signal channel in Figure 5A, compared to the cytoplasm, LDs
displayed significantly lower polarities, with a polarity increasing
gradient from its core (red color) to the surface (blue color). After
treatment with erastin for 6 h, the fluorescence intensity of green
channels (~470 nm) in LDs is gradually decreased, and a slight
increase of red fluorescence (~670 nm) in the cytoplasm is
observed as a result of the polarity increase (λex = 405 nm) (Figure
5B and D). The fluorescence changes in different channels
indicate that CQPP could be applied to monitor the polarity
changes in LDs induced by erastin. Further monitoring of the
nucleus morphology showed that characteristic events such as
chromatin agglutination, nuclear membrane fragmentation, and
DNA degradation observed during apoptosis (Figure S22) were
not found here.[28]
To futher investigate the variations of LD polarity during
ferroptosis, a time response model with ferroptosis inducer RSL3
was also used and the results are shown in Figure 5C and Figure
S23. HT-1080 cells were co-incubated with CQPP (5 μM) and
RSL3 (2 μM). Subsequently, fluorescence images were
simultaneously collected in both green (~470 nm) and red (~670
nm) channels recorded at different time points. With time, the
fluorescence intensity of the green channel gradually decreases,
and the punctate protrusions of LD gradually diffuse into the
cytoplasm accompanied by a slight increase in red fluorescence
in the cytoplasm; the nucleus morphology and red fluorescence
do not change obviously (Figure S23). Integrating the
fluorescence changes from green and red channels yielded
ratiometric fluorescence images shown in Figure 5C and E. As
the progress of ferroptosis continues, the polarity gradient from
the core to the surface becomes less obvious, which is
accompanied by the gradual homogeneity of polarity between
LDs and cytoplasm. The results further confirm the hypothesis
that the increased polarity of LDs is associated with the process
of ferroptosis.

Benefit from its excellent two-photon fluorescence imaging
capability and sensitive response to polarity/nucleic acid, CQPP
(5 μM) was applied to monitor the polarity changes of LDs and
nucleus in living cells under different conditions by FLIM. As
shown by untreated cells in Figure 6A, FLIM images are fitted,
and the lifetime is shown as pseudo ‐color. By counting, the
lifetime distribution in HT-1080 cells shows two distinct regions:
one at 0.5 – 1.5 ns, distributed in the cytoplasm, corresponding to
the fluorescence lifetime of CQPP in LDs; the other at 1.5 – 2.5
ns, distributed in the nucleus, corresponding to the fluorescence
lifetime of CQPP binding with DNA. Fit the fluorescence lifetime
spectra of region “a” and “b” in the living cells (“a” represent LDs
and “b” represent nucleic acids) yielded fluorescence lifetime
values of 1.46 ns and 2.11 ns, respectively. After the HT-1080
cells treated with erastin for 6 h, a new lifetime peak at 0.79 ns
appeared in the short lifetime region, while the fluorescence
lifetime in the 1.5 – 2.5 ns region did not change obviously (Figure
6B). However, the changes are particularly obvious at regions “a”
(0.77 ns). These results further confirmed that the polarity of LDs
increased significantly during the process of ferroptosis.
Compared with the ferroptosis process, we can clearly observe
from Figure 6C that when cells are in the apoptotic state (the cells
treated with cisplatin for 12 h), the fluorescence lifetime and
morphology of nucleus have shown obvious changes, due to the
nuclear condensation and chromatin margination[28], the
fluorescence lifetime for the nucleus was significantly increased,
but the change was not obvious for LDs. By applying the
fluorescence lifetime of CQPP in the nucleus as a reference, it is
clear that the polarity of LDs is increased gradually during the
ferroptosis process.

Conclusion
In summary, a D-π-A probe CQPP with a lipophilic coumarin
donor group and a cationic quinoline derivative acceptor unit was
precisely designed to monitor cellular microenvironment polarity
changes. Due to the appropriate donor-acceptor structure of
CQPP, it realized a sensitive response to polarity by ratiometric
fluorescence emission and fluorescence lifetime changes. For the
first time, CQPP could be used for LD/nucleus dual-targeting by
fluorescence imaging and fluorescence lifetime imaging. By
applying the ferroptosis process as a biological model, an obvious
increase in LD polarity is observed by both fluorescence and FLIM
imaging. The good nucleic acid binding ability of CQPP also
provides opportunities to distinguish ferroptosis from apoptosis.
The capability of CQPP to monitor LD and nuclear structure and
behaviors will help us more effectively understand ferroptosis and
other biological processes.
