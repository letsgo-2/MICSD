Photodynamic Therapy Hot Paper
Activation of Pyroptosis by Membrane-Anchoring AIE
Photosensitizer Design: New Prospect for Photodynamic Cancer Cell
Ablation
Min Wu+, Xingang Liu+, Huan Chen+, Yukun Duan, Jingjing Liu, Yutong Pan and Bin Liu*
Abstract: Pyroptosis as a lytic and inflammatory form of cell
death is a powerful tool to fight against cancer. However,
pyroptosis is usually activated by chemotherapeutic drugs,
which limits its anti-tumor applications due to drug resistance
and severe side effects. Herein, we demonstrate that membrane
targeting photosensitizers can induce pyroptosis for cancer cell
ablation with noninvasiveness and low side effects. A series of
membrane anchoring photosensitizers (TBD-R PSs) with
aggregation-induced emission (AIE) characteristics were pre-
pared through conjugation of TBD and phenyl ring with
cationic chains. Upon light irradiation, cytotoxic ROS were
produced in situ, resulting in direct membrane damage and
superior cancer cell ablation. Detailed study revealed that
pyroptosis gradually became the dominant cell death pathway
along with the increase of TBD-R PSs membrane anchoring
capability. This study offers a photo-activated pyroptosis-based
intervention strategy for cancer cell ablation.
Introduction
Cancers are complex multifactorial diseases resulting
from genetic variations that lead to normal cells being
transformed into malignant cells with evasion of programmed
cell death (PCD). The imbalance of cell growth and death
determines the rapid increase of cancer cells.[1] Apoptosis, as
a major type of PCD, is widely applied in chemotherapy,
phototherapy, gene therapy and radiotherapy for cancer
treatment.[2] Nevertheless, the effect of apoptosis is some-
times restricted due to the inherent or induced resistance of
carcinoma cells to apoptosis, which often results in unsatisfied
therapeutic outcome.[3] It is highly desirable if alternative cell
death pathways could be utilized for more efficient cancer cell
ablation.
Pyroptosis is a form of lytic programmed cell death
initiated by inflammasomes, which activate caspase-1 to
cleave gasdermin-D (GSDMD). The cleaved GSDMD re-
leases gasdermin-N domains (N-GSDMD) which would
translocate into the cell membrane and form membrane
pores (GSDMD pores) that drive cell swelling, membrane
rupture and cell death.[4] Furthermore, compared with
apoptosis, pyroptosis could be a more prospective pathway
for cancer therapy due to its ability to prime anti-tumor
immune responses by releasing cell contents such as lactate
dehydrogenase (LDH) and inflammatory cytokines including
interleukin-1beta (IL-1b) and interleukin-18 (IL-18).[5] The
newly discovered pyroptosis as another unique form of PCD
has attracted much attention to explore its potency in cancer
therapy.[6] However, pyroptosis is usually induced by chemo-
therapeutic drugs, which restricted its biomedical applications
due to drug resistance and severe side effects.[7] Therefore, it is
highly desirable to develop a more effective and noninvasive
method to induce pyroptosis death pathway for cancer
therapy.
Photodynamic therapy (PDT) emerges as a promising
oncologic intervention option owing to its eximious nature
including tunable photodamages, noninvasiveness and low
side effects. During PDT, photosensitizers (PSs) are activated
upon light irradiation to generate deleterious reactive oxygen
species (ROS) for irreversible ablation of cancer cells.[8]
Based on the fact that chemodrug-induced ROS has been
considered as a driving force in cancer cell pyroptosis,[9] we
envision that PDT should also be able to induce pyroptosis by
ROS robust generation, which has the advantages of over-
coming drug resistance and reducing the side effects in
treatment. However, the current commercial PSs, such as
TMPyP and Toluidine blue O, have been used to kill cancer
cells with moderate effectiveness owing to their aggregation-
caused fluorescence quenching with reduced ROS genera-
tion.[10] Furthermore, owing to the intrinsic drawbacks of ROS
including the instantaneous life span (half-life: 0.03–0.18 ms)
and a small diffusion region (0.01–0.02 mm), photo-damages
of PDT are usually spatially confined within the region with
abundant PSs.[11]
To address aggregation-caused reduction in photosensiti-
zation, PSs with aggregation-induced emission characteristics
(AIE PSs) were developed, which could emit bright fluores-
cence with effective ROS generation in aggregate states.[12]
The second dilemma could be resolved by designing PSs
which can accurately localize at specific cellular organelles
with a high concentration to promote PDT effect, since it
targets the primary site of photo-damage and correlates to the
exact cell-death mechanism.[13] Among cellular organelles,
cell membrane has been classified as the most important

Herein, we report the design of membrane-anchoring
AIE PSs, which simultaneously overcome the drawbacks of
conventional PSs to offer strong photosensitization and
effective activation of pyroptosis for cancer treatment.
Motivated by the fact that cationic pendant groups could
promote the solubility of PSs in aqueous media and the
amount of cationic pendant groups would affect the lipid
membrane anchorage capability,[17] we synthesized a series of
AIE PSs: TBD-1C, TBD-2C and TBD-3C by adjusting the
number of cationic chains attached to the same aromatic
structure to investigate their membrane anchorage and
pyroptosis activation capability (Scheme 1). 1,1,2,2-tetraphe-
nylethene-benzo[c][1,2,5] thiadiazole-2-(diphenyl methylene)
malononitrile (TPE-BT-DC, TBD) was chosen as the core to
support the fluorescence turn-on feature for imaging
and photosensitization for PDT. The cationic chains endow
the probes with membrane anchoring capability. Along
with the increased number of cationic chains, their
membrane anchorage capability was significantly enhanced.
TBD-3C was identified as the optimized membrane anchor-
ing PSs with the highest ablation efficiency of cancer cells
under light irradiation. As expected, pyroptosis and apoptosis
have been observed to co-exist during membrane-targeting
PDT with the designed AIE PSs, and pyroptosis gradually
became the dominant cell death pathway for TBD-3C.
Furthermore, pyroptosis was found to promote the immu-
nostimulatory release of cell contents and inflammatory
cytokines, which has great potentials to activate anti-tumor
immune responses.
Results and Discussion
Synthesis and Characterization of TBD-R
The molecular design of TBD-1C, TBD-2C, and TBD-3C
was presented in Scheme 1. As D (donor)-A’ (acceptor)-p-A
structure type PSs, TBD was comprised by methoxy-substi-
tuted tetraphenylethylene (D), benzothiadiazole (A’), phenyl
(p), and dicyanovinyl (A), which was further decorated with
one, two, and three cationic groups to yield TBD-1C, TBD-2C
and TBD-3C, respectively. The synthetic routes to TBD-1C,
TBD-2C and TBD-3C were shown in Scheme S1 and S2 in the
Supporting Information, respectively. The structures of TBD-
1C, TBD-2C, TBD-3C and their intermediates were charac-
terized with NMR and mass spectrometry as shown in the
Supporting Information, Figures S1–S16. All three molecules
displayed a broad absorption ranging from 300 to 500 nm
(Figure 1 A) with a weak red emission centered at 650 nm
(Figure 1 B). As illustrated in Figure 1 C and the Supporting
Information, Figure S17, the TBD-R molecules displayed
a typical AIE characteristic with weak emission in the
molecular states and strong red emission in the aggregate
states. The ROS generation capability of TBD-R was further
investigated with 9,10-anthracenediyl-bis (methylene)dima-
lonic acid (ABDA) as the ROS indicator using Rose Bengal
(RB) as a reference. As shown in the Supporting Information,
Figure S18, the absorbance of ABDA treated with TBD-1C,
TBD-2C, and TBD-3C quickly decreased under white light
irradiation (30 mW cm@2, 400–700 nm). Besides, TBD-1C,
TBD-2C, and TBD-3C showed similar ABDA decomposition
rate (Figure 1 D) of 13.5 nmol, 14.3 nmol, and 15.1 nmol of
ABDA per minute, which were higher than that of RB
(10.8 nmol of ABDA per minute) under the same molar
concentration of PSs. These results unveiled the efficient ROS
generation capability of TBD-1C, TBD-2C, and TBD-3C, as
well as the insignificant distinction in ROS generating ability
owing to the different amount of charges for each molecule.

Membrane Localized Photodynamic Ablation of Cancer Cells
The successful localization of PSs and ROS on cell
membrane drove the subsequent hypothesis that direct
destruction of cell membrane could achieve effective cancer
cell ablation.[14d, 18] To verify this hypothesis, the cytotoxicity of
the membrane-anchoring AIE PSs was firstly explored via
a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8 kit) assay. 4T1, HeLa and C6
cells were incubated with TBD-1C, TBD-2C, and TBD-3C at
various concentrations under white light irradiation
(40 mW cm@2) to achieve light-activated cell ablation. Cell
viability data in Figure 4 A showed that the IC50 values of
TBD-3C on 4T1, HeLa and C6 upon irradiation were,
respectively 7.1, 7.1, and 7.9 mM, less than that of TBD-1C
(20.3, 24.0, and 33.4 mM) and TBD-2C (10.9, 11.7, and
13.4 mM), demonstrating that TBD-3C has the highest
efficiency in killing cancer cells among the three PSs. The
stronger cell ablation effect of TBD-3C could be due to its
superior cell membrane-anchoring capacity. Moreover, negli-
gible dark cytotoxicity of TBD-1C, TBD-2C and TBD-3C was
observed (Supporting Information, Figure S21).

changes in cell morphology.[19] To better understand the
membrane targeting photodynamic cytocidal action, real-
time observation of cell morphological changes was conduct-
ed by CLSM. After being cultured with TBD-1C, TBD-2C,

can effectively accumulate TBD-3C on the cancer cell
membrane, which is beneficial to generate more ROS in situ

damage promotes pyroptosis pathway of cancer cells.
Membrane destruction caused by ROS can trigger the
release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) from dead cells,
which is an indication of pyroptotic cell cytotoxicity.[9b] To
confirm the pyroptosis pathway induced by the membrane-
anchoring PDT, the release of LDH from the TBD-R PSs
treated cancer cells was further detected (Figure 4 C). As
anticipated, the LDH release level of the TBD-R PSs treated
4T1 cells upon light irradiation followed the trend of TBD-1C
(22.1 %) < TBD-2C (59.7 %) < TBD-3C (78.7 %), and the
LDH release from the TBD-R PSs under dark was negligible
using the PBS treated group as a control. Furthermore,
similar LDH release trend has been observed with HeLa and
C6 cells. These results jointly suggest that the membrane
anchoring PDT has induced cell death by pyroptosis pathway.

According to previous literature, PDT could lead to cell
death via apoptosis pathway.[23] Therefore, we further inves-
tigated whether the apoptosis pathway also exists in cancer
cells treated by the TBD-R PSs. Prior to the experiments, the
relationship between pyroptosis-associated proteins
(GSDMD, caspase-1, IL-1b and IL-18) and apoptosis-asso-
ciated proteins (bax, bcl-2 and caspase-3) were firstly
analyzed by STRING v10 database. As shown in the
Supporting Information, Figure S25A, there was a close
relationship between the two types of proteins, which
indicated that pyroptosis and apoptosis pathways may exist
simultaneously during cell death. The increased bax/bcl-2
ratio up-regulates cleaved-caspase-3 and directly induces
cancer cell apoptosis.[24] Then, we studied the typical apop-
tosis proteins in the cells after various treatments. From the
western blot results (Supporting Information, Fig-
ure S25B,C), the ratios of bax to bcl-2 were apparently
increased by photosensitization with TBD-1C (2.1- fold to
control), but only slightly increased with TBD-2C (1.4-fold to
control) and TBD-3C (1.2-fold to control). Further, the
increase of bax to bcl-2 ratio induced by photosensitization
resulted in enhanced expression of cleaved caspase-3 (Sup-
porting Information, Figure S25D). Interestingly, the apopto-
sis level of the cells treated with TBD-1C was much higher
than that of TBD-2C and TBD-3C upon light irradiation,
which was opposite to the trend of pyroptosis. The nature of
cell death treated with TBD-R was further investigated by
flow cytometry with annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI)
staining. As shown in the Supporting Information, Figure S26,
the percentage of double-positive cells (pyroptotic cells)
treated with TBD-R PSs upon light irradiation followed the
trend of TBD-1C (4.00) < TBD-2C (34.3 %) < TBD-3C
(52.5 %), and the proportions of annexin V single-positive
cells (apoptotic cell) followed the reverse trend of TBD-1C
(54.4 %) > TBD-2C (8.95 %) > TBD-3C (7.71 %). These
results collectively imply that pyroptosis and apoptosis were
co-existing in membrane-targeting PDT with these AIE PSs,
and pyroptosis gradually played a more dominant role along
with the increased number of cationic chains. As cancer cells
are often resistant to apoptosis, but pyroptosis is a type of
immunogenic cell death,[25] the photo-activated pyroptosis
induction may represent an alternative therapeutic strategy
against cancer.
Conclusion
Three AIE PSs TBD-R with different numbers of cationic
chains were developed for membrane anchoring to realize
photo-activated cancer cell pyroptosis. TBD-R PSs were
prepared by the conjugation of TBD and phenyl ring with
cationic chains, where TBD supported the dual features of
fluorescence turn-on imaging and the photosensitization for
PDT, and the cationic chains enabled the insertion of the
probes into cell membrane. With the increase of the number
of cationic chains, the membrane anchoring capacity was
improved, and the TBD with three cationic chains (TBD-3C)
showed the strongest membrane anchoring capacity as
compared with one cationic chain (TBD-1C) and two cationic
chains (TBD-2C). The strong membrane anchoring ability of
TBD-3C enabled in situ production of ROS, direct membrane
damage, and the consequent cancer cell death. Through this
study, we demonstrate that pyroptosis pathway could also
play an important role in photodynamic cancer cell ablation,
which is largely ignored in the literature. This study also
demonstrate the promising potential of membrane anchoring
AIE PSs for cancer treatment by activated pyroptosis death
pathway.
