A B S T R A C T
Photodynamic therapy (PDT) uses a photosensitizer (PS) and visible light to induce cancer cell death. Pyroptosis
is a new type of programmed cell death that is associated with the gasdermin protein family. However, the
precise mechanism of pyroptosis in PDT-induced suppression of esophageal cancer remains unknown. We
demonstrate that PDT can induce gasdermin E (GSDME)-mediated pyroptosis, which is characterized by the
formation of pyroptotic blebs in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), which burst and release intra-
cellular contents and pro-inflammatory mediators. Mechanistically, PDT may inhibit pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2)
and consequently, activate caspase-8 and caspase-3, which ultimately releases N-GSDME and triggers pyroptosis
in ESCC. Moreover, PDT decreased the efficiency of pyroptosis in the presence of a glycolytic inhibitor. Overall,
our results show that PDT induces pyroptosis in ESCC by targeting the PKM2/caspase-8/caspase-3/GSDME axis.
This is the first in-depth study of the specific mechanism underlying PKM2-mediated pyroptosis under PDT in
ESCC, and potentially has great implications for the clinical application of PDT in ESCC.
1. Introduction
The incidence and mortality of human esophageal squamous cell
carcinoma (ESCC) have increased over the past several decades, espe-
cially in the Asian Esophageal Cancer Belt, making ESCC the eighth most
common cancer and sixth most common cause of death worldwide [1,2].
Currently, the most common treatments for ESCC include endoscopic
therapies, surgery, and chemoradiotherapy. Despite improvements in
therapies and the introduction of multimodal treatments, ESCC can
progress aggressively, resulting in poor survival rates; therefore, the
identification of new therapeutic targets and elucidation of mechanisms
of ESCC is warranted [3,4].
Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is an approved and effective treatment
method for many cancer types. PDT uses a photosensitizer (PS) drug and
visible light of a specific wavelength to generate reactive oxygen species
(ROS), which can induce cancer cell death [5]. PDT has several advan-
tages over other common cancer therapies such as chemotherapy or
radiotherapy, including minimal normal tissue toxicity, spatiotemporal
selectivity, negligible systemic effects, a noninvasive nature, and
markedly reduced long-term morbidity [6–9]. Therefore, PDT has been
applied in clinical esophageal cancer therapy with low systemic toxicity
and no damage to organ function [10,11]. Although the anti-tumor ef-
fect of PDT in esophageal cancer cells is well established, the mechanism
underlying PDT-mediated cancer cell death remains elusive.

Pyroptosis plays an essential role in immune defense and inflam-
matory diseases [12–20]. The gasdermin family, which comprises six
tumor suppressor proteins including gasdermin E (GSDME), has rede-
fined 	pyroptosis 	as 	gasdermin-mediated 	programmed 	necrosis.
Following stimulation, GSDME is cleaved by caspase-3 activation and
releases the N-terminal effector domain, which forms pores in the cell
membrane, triggering rapid plasma membrane rupture. Subsequently,
the cells release intracellular contents and pro-inflammatory mediators,
such as interleukin (IL)-18 and IL-1β, which in turn recruit immune cells
and further promote inflammation [21]. Pyroptosis can be manipulated
to promote cancer cell death owing to its anticancer properties [12,
22–29].
Pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) is overexpressed in most tumor cells and
is essential for the Warburg effect, indicating that cancer cells rely on
glycolysis to produce energy [30–32]. We previously showed that PDT
can induce caspase-3 expression and drive the Warburg effect by
inhibiting PKM2 expression. The inhibitory activity of PDT decreases
with time, possibly due to the tolerance and recurrence of PDT [5]. Liu
et al. also showed that PKM2-dependent glycolysis can activate the
NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to pyroptosis in muscle cells [33].
However, to date, the role of PKM2 in PDT-mediated inhibition of ESCC
remains unknown.
Here, we investigated whether GSDME was upregulated in ESCC
cells and explored the mechanism of pyroptosis in the antitumor effects
of PDT.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Patient specimens
Normal esophageal and cancer tissue samples of patients (two males;
two females) with histopathologically-confirmed ESCC who were hos-
pitalized from November 2019 to June 2020 were provided by Harbin
Medical University Cancer Hospital. No patient underwent adjuvant
chemotherapy or radiation treatment before surgery. The study protocol
was described in detail to the patients, and written informed consent
was obtained. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee.
2.2. Cell culture
Human esophageal cancer cell lines, Eca109 and Ec9706, were
procured from Harbin Medical University. Cells were cultured in RPMI
1640 supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10%) and penicillin/
streptomycin (1%) at 37 ◦C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere.
2.3. Establishment of stable PKM2 overexpression and GSDME silencing
Stable PKM2 overexpression (OE-PKM2) and GSDME silencing (Si-
GSDME) were achieved using lentiviral constructs, including one PKM2-
Lenti-OE™ miRNA (42428-2, GeneChem, Shanghai, China) and DFNA5-
RNAi (80218-1 GeneChem, Shanghai, China). The RNAi oligos targeting
PKM2 and GSDME genes were 5′- GCCCGAGGCTTCTTCAAGAAG-3′ and
5′-ATGATGAATGACCTGACTT-3′, respectively. Targeted cells were
transfected and incubated with 1 μg/mL puromycin (ST551 Beyotime
Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 2 weeks and then transferred to a
culture bottle.
2.4. Chemicals and reagents
RPMI 1640 (SH30809.01B) was obtained from Hyclone (GE
Healthcare Life Sciences, Buckinghamshire, England), FBS (900-108)
from Gemini (Woodland, USA), penicillin/streptomycin (C0222) and
trypsin (C0202) from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), and
dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) from Sigma-Aldrich. Prior to usage, 5-ami-
nolevulinic acid (5-ALA; A7793, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Ac-DEVD-CHO,
a specific caspase-3 inhibitor, was purchased from MedChemExpress
(New Jersey, USA). Z-IETD-FMK, a specific caspase-8 inhibitor, was
purchased from MedChemExpress (New Jersey, USA). The inhibitor
stock solution was then diluted to 10 mmol/L. Apoptosis and necrosis
assay kits were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai,
China). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay reagent was obtained from
Dojindo (Kumamoto, Japan). CytoTox 96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity
Assay kit was purchased from Promega (Madison, WI, USA). Enzyme-
linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (SEA133Hu, SEA071Hu,
SEA563Hu, SEA079Hu, SEA049Hu) were obtained from Wuhan USCN
Sciences Corporation, China.
2.5. PDT treatment
Cells in the logarithmic phase were seeded onto target plates over-
night. Then, cells were cultivated respectively with 5-ALA (0.5 mmol/L)
for 4 h, protoporphyrin IX dimethyl ester (20 μg/mL) for 3 h, and chlorin
e6 (20 μg/mL) for 4 h in a dark room. Cells were then restored with 5 mL
of fresh RPMI 1640. Red light irradiation was carried out at 630 nm
wavelength (LWRL630-1.5W-FRL18101711, Beijing Laserwave Opto-
Electronics Technology Co. Ltd) with an irradiation intensity of 32 mW/
cm2 and fluence rates of 25 J/cm2 in Eca109 cells and 20 J/cm2 in
Ec9706 cells. Spent RPMI 1640 was then restored with fresh RPMI 1640
containing 10% FBS, followed by incubation for 4, 12, or 24 h in the
dark.
2.6. Microscopic imaging
To observe cell morphology during pyroptosis, cells (1.0 × 105 cells/
well) were seeded onto six-well plates. Static bright-field images were
captured using a LEICA Camera microscope (Leica, Mannheim, Ger-
many) at 26 ◦C and processed using Adobe Photoshop.
2.7. Hoechst 33342/PI staining
Hoechst 33342/PI staining assay was performed according to the
manufacturer’s instructions. Cells (1.0 × 105 cells/well) were seeded
onto six-well plates and subjected to the indicated treatments. 24 h after
irradiation, cells were then stained with Hoechst 33342 (5 μg/mL) and
PI (2 μg/mL) for 30 min at 4 ◦C in a dark room. Images were captured
using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica, Mannheim, Germany)
at 26 ◦C and processed using Adobe Photoshop.
2.8. Subcellular localization of 5-ALA-induced protoporphyrin IX
Cells (1.0 × 105 cells/well) were seeded onto six-well plates and
cultivated with 5-ALA. After 4 h, the fluorescence patterns of 5-ALA-
PpIX (red) were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope
(Leica, Mannheim, Germany) at 26 ◦C. Images were processed using
Adobe Photoshop.
2.9. Transmission electron microscopy
To examine the morphology of pyroptotic cells, cells were seeded as
monolayers overnight. After specific treatment, cells were collected
using trypsin-EDTA solution, fixed with ice-cold 2.5% glutaraldehyde
for 24 h at 4 ◦C, and observed under a transmission electron microscope
(Hitachi 7500, Japan).
2.10. CCK-8 assay
CCK-8 assay is a test for the activity of mitochondrial de-
hydrogenases and the results can be defined as measuring toxicity. Cells
were seeded onto 96-well culture plates (1.0 × 104 cells/well) overnight.
After the specified treatment, 10 μL CCK-8 reagent was added and the
optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm after 1 or 2 h. All exper-
iments were performed in triplicates and repeated at least thrice. Data

2.11. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay

2.12. ELISA
After the designated treatment, circulating tumor necrosis factor
alpha (TNF-α), IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, and interferon gamma (IFN-γ) con-
centrations were determined by ELISA using a commercial kit according
to the recommended protocol in the manufacturer’s instructions
(Wuhan USCN Sciences Corporation, China). The standard curves were
plotted using purified cytokine standards. Each sample was tested in
duplicate, and their average value was obtained.
2.13. Western blotting

natants were quantified using 10–15% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-
polyacrylamide gels in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.
Proteins were electrophoresed in 10% or 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel
electrophoresis and transferred to a nitrocellulose filter membrane (Bio-

thrice.
2.14. Reverse transcription quantitative PCR
Total RNA was extracted from xenografts and cells using TRIzol re-
agent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). RNA samples were reverse-transcribed into
cDNA. The SYBR ® Green Real-time PCR Master Mix was used to mea-

this assay were PKM2 forward: 5′-GCCA TAA TCGTCCTCACCAAGT-3′,

GAGAGCCAGTCTTCATTT-3′, 	reverse: 	5′-AGCACAGGGTTTCTCA-
GATTTA-3′; and β-actin forward: 5′-ACCGAGCGTGGCTACAGCTTC
ACC-3′, reverse: 5′-AGCACCCGTGGCCATCTCTTTCTCG-3′. Each assay
was repeated three times, and the gene expression level was computed
using 2  ΔΔCT method following the manufacturer’s instructions.
2.15. Tumor xenografts
The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved animal
use in this study. Logarithmic-phase cells (5 × 106) were injected into
the left flanks of 4-week-old female nude mice. Six days after cell in-
jection, the length (a) and width (b) of the xenografts were measured
using calipers. The tumor volume (V) was calculated as V = ab2/2. Nude
mice were injected with 5-ALA and light irradiation was carried out at
630 nm wavelength. Nude mice were treated with PDT or other treat-
ments when tumors reached volumes greater than 200 mm3. The nom-
inal power was set to 400mw. Irradiation was continued for 4 min. After
24 h, mice were euthanized and xenograft tumor and serum were
obtained.
2.16. Immunohistochemical staining
Immunohistochemical staining was carried out using the Vectastain
EliteDAB Kit (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). Xenograft
tumors were cut into 4-μm sections. Slides were deparaffinized, hy-
drated, and permeabilized with PBST for 10 min. The sections were then
incubated with anti-PKM2 (1:200) and anti-cleaved caspase-3 (1:2000)
primary antibodies. Next, the samples were incubated with anti-Rabbit
for 1 h at 26 ◦C.
2.17. Statistical analysis
The data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation of at least
three independent experiments. Graphs showing the results were
generated using PRISM 5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) and the
statistical significance was analyzed using one-way analysis of variance.
In all statistical comparisons, p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
3. Results
3.1. GSDME is overexpressed in esophageal cancer tissue
We first evaluated the DNA copy number of GSDME in different types
of tumors in the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA)
database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), and found that the level of
mRNA expression of GSDME in tumor tissues was higher than that in the
normal tissues of the esophagus (Fig. 1A). Moreover, the copy numbers
of GSDME in tumors were higher than those in other tissues in the
Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (p = < 0.001; Fig. 1B). Subse-
quently, we investigated the mRNA copy number of GSDME in the
ONCOMINE database (www.oncomine.org). We observed that the
expression level of GSDME in tumors was higher than that in their paired
adjacent tissues in human ESCC (p < 0.001, Fig. 1C). Consistent with
these observations, western blot analysis and PCR showed that GSDME
expression was significantly higher in tumor than in normal (Fig. 1D and
E).
3.2. PDT induces pyroptosis in ESCC
To obtain a clearer view of the Eca109 and Ec9706 cells after
exposure to PDT, we observed the cells using microscopy and found that
most cells were swollen peripherally and had large bubbles emanating
from the cell membrane after 4 h, 12 h, and 24 h of PDT. There were no
obvious changes between cells in the negative control (NC) and the
photosensitizer or light irradiation groups (Fig. 2A and B). Therefore, we
used the Hoechst 33342/PI staining assay to further elucidate the
characteristics of PDT-mediated cell death. We found that the propor-
tion of PDT-mediated pyroptotic cell death (PI-positive cells) was
significantly higher than NC groups. The PI-positive cells exhibited a
round cell morphotype and had large, red-stained bubbles (Fig. 3B). In
addition, fluorescence microscopy showed that PpIX localized in mito-
chondria after 4 h treatment with ALA (Supplementary Fig. S2B).
Transmission electron micrographs showed nuclear pyknosis, cyto-
plasmic swelling, and multiple pore formation in the membranes of PDT-
treated Eca109 and Ec9706 cells (Fig. 3A). To evaluate this effect, we
measured the levels of LDH released by treated and untreated cells. We
found that the release of LDH in the PDT group was significantly higher
than that in the control group (Fig. 3C). However, this effect was not
time-dependent. To further validate the role of pyroptosis in PDT, we
quantified the levels of TNF-α, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IFN-γ, using ELISA.
We found that these proinflammatory factors were significantly elevated
in the PDT group, indicating plasma membrane rupture and leakage
(Fig. 2C–G). In addition, we tested other photosensitizer classes such as
protoporphyrin IX dimethyl ester (PDT1) and chlorin e6 (PDT2) and
found that the two photosensitizers also elevated LDH and N-GSDME
(Supplementary Figs. S3B and C) expression levels. Furthermore, light

3.6. 2-DG combined with PDT inhibited pyroptosis in PDT
Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed a relationship between
the mRNA level of GSDME and the glycolytic pathway (PID_G-
LYCOLYSIS_PATHWAY) in patients with ESCC (GSE23400, n = 53)
(Fig. 10B). Therefore, we explored the anti-tumor efficacy of 2-Deoxy-D-
arabino-hexose (2-DG) in combination with PDT. Phase-contrast mi-
croscopy showed that the pyroptotic morphology was abrogated in
Eca109 and Ec9706 cells in the 2-DG/PDT combination group
(Fig. 10A). In addition, CCK-8 was slightly decreased in the 2-DG/PDT
group compared with that in the other groups (Fig. 10C). However,
the liberation of LDH, IL-1β, and N-GSDME was inhibited in the com-
bined treatment group (Fig. 10D–F). Moreover, western blotting anal-
ysis showed no obvious effects of 2-DG and PDT on the level of PKM2
expression compared with treatment with PDT alone (Fig. 10F).
3.7. PDT induces ESCC pyroptosis by targeting the PKM2/GSDME axis in
animal models
To further study the role of pyroptosis in PDT-mediated tumor sup-
pression, we injected Si-GSDME, OE-PKM2, and control cells into the
subcutaneous tissue of nude mice (Fig. 11A). After 3 weeks, the photo-
sensitizer was injected intraperitoneally and the mice were then treated
with visible light of the appropriate wavelength. Xenografted tumors
and serum were harvested at the end of the experiment (after 24 h)
(Fig. 11B). LDH and ELISA analysis of serum showed that the liberation
of LDH and IL-1β in all PDT groups was increased compared with that in
the control group. In contrast, the liberation of LDH and IL-1β in Si-
GSDME, 2-DG, and OE-PKM2 groups treated with PDT were less than
those in the PDT-only group (Fig. 11D and E). Immunohistochemical
analysis showed increased cleaved caspase-3 and decreased PKM2 levels
in all PDT-treated groups. However, the expression level of activated
caspase-3 was lower in the 2-DG, Si-GSDME, and OE-PKM2 groups
treated with PDT than in the PDT-only group. In addition, the expression
level of PKM2 in the 2-DG/PDT combined treatment group was similar
to that in the PDT-only group (Fig. 11C). Western blotting analysis
revealed that the level of N-GSDME expression in the Si-GSDME, 2-DG,
and OE-PKM2 groups with PDT was less than that in the PDT-only group
(Fig. 11F and Supplementary Fig. S4).
4. Discussion
This study investigated the specific mechanism of GSDME-mediated
pyroptosis in PDT in ESCC. Previously, Zhu et al. showed that sono-
photodynamic therapy (SPDT) induces pyroptosis and apoptosis in
HepG2 liver cancer cells [34]. Wu et al. suggested that PDT might induce
pyroptosis through the production of cytotoxic ROS [35]. In our study,
we demonstrated that PDT could downregulate PKM2 expression, which
consequently activates caspase-8 and caspase-3, and eventually releases
N-GSDME and triggers pyroptosis in ESCC.
Traditionally, pyroptosis has been referred to as GSDMD-induced cell
death. However, the discovery that caspase-3/GSDME-induced pyrop-
tosis indicated that the pathways of pyroptosis are likely more diverse
than previously reported [27]. In 2019, Wu et al. showed that
co-treatment with BI2536 and cisplatin (DDP) could induce pyroptosis
by targeting the caspase-3/GSDME pathway in ESCC [36]. Recently,
Zhang 	et 	al. 	found 	that 	alpinumisoflavone 	(AIF) 	promoted
GSDME-mediated pyroptotic cell death by activating caspase-3 in ESCC
[37]. In our previous work, we have provided evidence indicating that
PDT may have an extraordinary antitumor effect in ESCC and that it
could activate caspase-3 in ESCC [38].In this study, we found that PDT
increased N-GSDME and cleaved-caspase-3 levels in PDT-treated cells
but did not influence GSDMD and caspase-1 levels, suggesting that PDT
can induce pyroptosis in ESCC. Furthermore, specific caspase-3 in-
hibitors and GSDME downregulation completely eliminated pyroptosis
in PDT-treated cells. However, PDT-treatment in Si-GSDME cells did not
influence the activation of caspase-3 and further indicated GSDME
located in the downstream of caspase-3 in PDT-induced pyroptosis.
Overall, our findings suggest that GSDME is activated by caspase-3 in
PDT-mediated pyroptosis in ESCC.
Caspase-8 was originally defined as an extrinsic coactivator of
apoptosis, which can activate caspase-3 [39]. It has been shown that
caspase-8 induces the cleavage of GSDMD to trigger pyroptosis during
Yersinia infection [39]. Fritsch et al. also found that caspase-8 is the
molecular switch that controls apoptosis and pyroptosis, indicating that
pyroptosis and apoptosis might share many similar signal transduction
pathways [40]. Other studies have shown that pyroptosis can be driven
by the N-terminal domain of GSDME through the activation of caspase-3
or caspase-8 [39,41,42]. We found that the blockade of caspase-3 and
caspase-8 	activity 	suppressed 	the 	cleavage 	of 	caspase-3 	and
PDT-induced pyroptosis. However, the specific caspase-3 inhibitor used
in this study did not prevent the cleavage of caspase-8. These findings
imply that caspase-8 might be involved in the activation of caspase-3
and might participate in PDT-induced pyroptosis in ESCC. Although
P-value of less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance, the effects of
DEVD, IETD, and 2-DG on CCK-8 and LDH release are rather minimal.
We speculated that the pathways of antitumor effect in PDT were
complex and variable and these specific inhibitors only blocked
caspase-3, caspase-8 and Warburg effect, but there might be other
pathways participating the process of CCK-8 and LDH release.
In our previous studies, we have argued that PDT can stimulate
caspase-3 expression and drive the Warburg effect by controlling the
expression of PKM2 in a time-dependent manner [38]. In addition, other
studies have shown that PKM2 is important for cancer cell proliferation
and tumor xenograft growth [30,43–45]. Interestingly, Wu et al.
revealed that PKM2-dependent glycolysis can activate NLRP3 and
trigger pyroptosis through the caspase-1/GSDMD pathway in muscle
tissue lesions [33]. In this study, we demonstrated that PDT inhibited
PKM2, which could lead to caspase-3 activation, GSDME cleavage, and
pyroptosis induction. Furthermore, we found that the overexpression of
PKM2 reversed pyroptosis in PDT treatment. Therefore, we speculated
that 2-DG, a well-known glycolysis inhibitor, might significantly
improve PDT-induced pyroptosis compared with either 2-DG or PDT
monotherapy. However, we found that 2-DG enhanced PDT cytotoxicity
but inhibited PDT-mediated pyroptosis. Further analysis revealed that
2-DG/PDT combined treatment had no obvious effect on the level of
PKM2 expression compared with the PDT-only group. We believe that
this outcome might be due to the fact that pyroptosis is an
energy-intensive process, and as such, might drive the Warburg effect. In
our previous study, we showed that PDT drives Warburg effect in a
time-dependent manner through regulating PKM2 [5]. Therefore, 2-DG
might disturb energy supply by inhibiting the Warburg effect. However,
pretreatment with 2-DG did not influence the expression of PKM2 and
might have little effect on the PKM2/caspase-3/GSDME pyroptosis
pathway. This notion is consistent with the findings of Hu et al. that
treatment with 2-DG had no obvious effect on the total level of PKM2
expression [46]. Overall, we have shown the effect of 2-DG on PKM2 and
