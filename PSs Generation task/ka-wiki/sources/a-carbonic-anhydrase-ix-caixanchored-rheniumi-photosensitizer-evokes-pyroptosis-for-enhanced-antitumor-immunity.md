Metals in Medicine Very Important Paper
A Carbonic Anhydrase IX (CAIX)-Anchored Rhenium(I)
Photosensitizer Evokes Pyroptosis for Enhanced Anti-Tumor
Immunity
Xuxian Su, Wen-Jin Wang, Qian Cao,* Hang Zhang, Bin Liu, Yuyi Ling, Xiaotong Zhou and
Zong-Wan Mao*
Abstract: An ideal cancer treatment should not only destroy
primary tumors but also improve the immunogenicity of the
tumor microenvironment to achieve a satisfactory anti-tumor
immune effect. We designed a carbonic anhydrase IX (CAIX)-
anchored rhenium(I) photosensitizer, named CA-Re, that not
only performs type-I and type-II photodynamic therapy (PDT)
with high efficiency under hypoxia (nanomolar-level photo-
toxicity), but also evokes gasdermin D (GSDMD) mediated
pyroptotic cell death to effectively stimulate tumor immuno-
genicity. CA-Re could disrupt and self-report the loss of
membrane integrity simultaneously. This promoted the matu-
ration and antigen-presenting ability of dendritic cells (DCs),
and fully activated T cells dependent adaptive immune
response in vivo, eventually eliminating distant tumors at the
same time as destroying primary tumors. To the best of our
knowledge, CA-Re is the first metal complex-based pyroptosis
inducer.
Introduction
With the clinical success of immune checkpoint blockade
(ICB) in some advanced cancer, immunotherapy has attract-
ed extensive attention.[1] However, its therapeutic effect on
malignant tumors, such as triple-negative breast cancer
(mTNBC) and colorectal carcinoma, is severely restricted
by the non-immunogenic tumor microenviroment.[2] Recent-
ly, there is much evidence showing that immune stimulation
occurs after photodynamic therapy (PDT), suggesting that it
is a potential immunogenic therapeutic procedure.[3]
PDT is a non-invasive clinical procedure that uses light
and non-toxic photosensitizers to produce singlet oxygen
(1O2) via oxygen-dependent pathway (type-II) or other
reactive oxygen species (ROS) via oxygen-independent path-
way (type-I), thus destroying tumor tissues with temporal and
spatial selectivity.[4] In some cases, PDT could induce
extracellular release damage-associated molecular patterns
(DAMPs), thus enhancing the immunogenicity of cancer cells
and activating the systemic adaptive immune response.[5]
However, the robust anti-tumor immune response induced
by PDT is still the exception rather than the rule.[6] Moreover,
most photosensitizers must be combined with ICB (e.g. anti-
CTAL4, anti-PD-L1) to achieve satisfactory anti-tumor
immune effect than used alone, which brings high cost and
serious side effects.[7] The reasons for this lack of universality
and validity mainly lie in: i) tumor hypoxia seriously limits the
PDT efficiency and promote the immunosuppression and
ii) PDT usually induce cell apoptosis which is poorly immu-
nogenic owing to the slow or even no release of DAMPs.[8]
Therefore, to achieve effective antitumor immunity, novel
photosensitizers capable of displaying high PDT efficiency
under hypoxia and inducing cell death patterns with high
immunogenicity rather than apoptosis are urgently needed.
Pyroptosis is a unique form of programmed cell death that
is essentially proinflammatory and critical for immunity.[9]
The cleavage of gasdermin D (GSDMD) by inflammatory
caspases is considered as a critical event to mediate pyrop-
tosis, which releases its N-terminal domain (GSDMD-N) to
bind with membrane phospholipids and drill pores on cell
membranes.[10] As a result, the osmotic potential of membrane
is disrupted to drive cell swelling, cell membrane rupture and
fast release of inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-1b, IL-18).[11] It
has been found that inflammation induced by pyroptosis is
able to trigger strong anti-tumor immunity and enhance the
efficacy of ICB.[12] More interestingly, inducing less than 15 %
of tumor cells to pyroptosis is sufficient to clear the entire
breast tumor graft.[13] Therefore, pyroptosis induction pro-
vides a new direction for the design of highly immunogenic
photosensitizers. However, the current pyroptosis inducers
are mostly chemodrugs, while the photosensitizers capable of
inducing pyroptosis are rarely reported.[14] On the other hand,
PDT capable of increasing cell membrane permeability and
inducing membrane rupture has also been proposed as an
effective strategy to promote antitumor immunity as it could
cause the fast release of cellular content including DAMPs.[15]
Inspired by these, we have designed a carbonic anhydrase
IX (CAIX) anchored rhenium(I)-based photosensitizer,
named as CA-Re (Scheme 1). CAIX is a transmembrane
protein over-expressed exclusively in tumors rather than
normal tissues, and its transcription is hypoxia-dependent
regulated by hypoxia inducible factor (HIF-1a).[16] CAIX-
targeted chemotherapeutic agents as well as rare examples of
photosensitizers have been reported to overcome the hypoxia
limitation in cancer therapy.[17] Our group also reported two
novel platinum(IV) prodrugs against hypoxic tumors via

Results and Discussion
As shown in Scheme 1, a pyridine-based ligand containing
benzene sulfonamide tail was designed, which was a common-
ly used moiety for binding the zinc active center of CAIX.
Then the novel photosensitizer CA-Re was synthesized by
reacting this monodentate ligand with the dechlorinated
Re(CO)3(N N)Cl precursor in THF, followed by column
chromatography (CH2Cl2/CH3OH) for purification. The
structure was characterized by ESI-MS, HRMS, 1H and
13C NMR (Figure S1–S4). HPLC investigation showed that
CA-Re had good stability in PBS/acetonitrile (v/v, 3:7)
solvent at least for 1 week no matter light irradiation
(425 nm for 10 min or 525 nm for 1 h) was present. Moreover,
in PBS/acetonitrile containing ascorbic acid (20 mM) which
was a reducing substance that existed naturally in living
organisms, the HPLC signals of CA-Re still remained
constant in at least 24 h after light irradiation (Figure S5).
These results demonstrated that CA-Re had excellent (photo-
)stability without releasing the pyridine ligand even after
irradiation.
The electronic absorption spectrum of CA-Re in PBS was
dominated by high-energy intraligand (IL) transition at ca.
280–320 nm and low-energy metal-to-ligand charge-transfer
(MLCT), which was a much broader band even reaching
600 nm with the maximum absorption center at ca. 425 nm
(Figure S6A). Upon 425 nm excitation, CA-Re was lumines-
cent with the emission maximum at ca. 685 nm (Figure S6B),
and the emission quantum yields were determined to be 0.01–
0.04 % in various solvents by using [Ru(bpy)3]2+ as a standard,
which was similar to that of TLD1433.[4a] The photophysical
properties of CA-Re was collected in Table S1. We also
conducted the nanosecond transient absorption (TA) spectra
after a 355 nm laser pulse excitation, which generally
displayed signatures of either 3pp* (ILCT) or 3MLCT states,
so as to further investigate the excited states of CA-Re. As
shown in Figure S7, the 3MLCT signature could be charac-
terized by the bleach below 490 nm, corresponding to the loss
of the ground-state 1MLCT absorption, and the signature of
the 3ILCT state were also observed as indicated by the new
broad absorptions at 500–700 nm with a long-lived lifetime
(ca. 1.2 ms). This result was consistent with computational
studies using DFT and TDDFT calculations. As indicated by
the calculated T1 (triplet) energies and the frontier orbital
calculations, both the donor and acceptor orbitals of the
lowest-energy T1 were localized mainly on the bidentate IP-
TT ligand and predominantly 3ILCT in nature, the energy of
which was as low as 1.90 eV (Figure S8 and Table S2). We
reasoned that this 3ILCT state could participate in intermo-
lecular charge transfer reaction with oxygen and biomolecules
to cause various ROS.
The capability of CA-Re to generate ROS under light
irradiation was also studied. PDT generally produces 1O2
responsible for the cancer cell death, however, the 1O2
quantum yield (FD) of CA-Re was determined to be only
0.16 and 0.06 in aerated acetonitrile and PBS, respectively
(Figure S9), which was even lower than the reference com-
pound [Ru(bpy)3]Cl2. This was probably due to that the
lowest-lying T1 energy of CA-Re approximated to the energy
required for sensitizing 1O2 (Table S2). To characterize the
generation of other ROS, a commercial COH indicator
hydroxyphenyl fluorescein (HPF) was used, which was non-
fluorescent in PBS in the presence of CA-Re under dark but
strongly emissive upon 425 nm irradiation, indicating the COH
production (Figure S10). Moreover, electron paramagnetic
resonance (EPR) spectroscopy using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrro-
line N-oxide (DMPO) as a spin-trap showed that pure DMPO
solution did not show EPR signals under 425 nm irradiation
(20 mW cm@2, 120 sec), whereas a characteristic EPR signal in
good accordance with superoxide radial anion (CO2@) was
observed in the presence of CA-Re under the same condition,

The distribution of CA-Re in living cells could be easily
monitored by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).
Figure 1 A shows that after incubating MDA-MB-231 cells for
3 h or even as long as 24 h, CA-Re was always distributed on
cell membrane rather than in cytoplasm. Co-staining experi-
ment showed a good colocalization between the red emission
from CA-Re and the green fluorescence from a commercial
membrane dye Dil, further verifying the membrane-local-
ization of CA-Re (Figure 1 B). Based on the molecular
design, the membrane-localization of CA-Re was expected
due to its CAIX binding capacity. To verify this, molecular
docking was initially performed, showing that the benzene
sulfonamide moiety of CA-Re could enter the pocket of
CAIX (PDB code: 3IAI), coordinating with the Zn2+ active
center and hydrogen bonding with THR199, THR200, and
HIS94 residues, while the terthiophene moiety was stuck
outside the pocket (Figure 1 C). The binding energy of
@7.7 kcal mol@1 was comparable to acetazolamide (AAZ,
a FDA approved carbonic anhydrase inhibitor), indicating
good CAIX binding ability.
Then the inhibition effects of CA-Re on CAIX were
studied both in in vitro kit test and at cellular level. As shown
in kit test using human CAIX as the enzyme source and 4-
NPA as substrate (Figure S12), CA-Re could effectively
inhibit CAIX activity with inhibitory IC50 value of ca.
3.85 mM. It was worth mentioning that although CA-Re
exhibited similar inhibitory effect on the activity of other CA
isoforms such as CAII and CAVB in kit test, CA-Re had none
opportunity to interact with other intracellular CAs because it
would meet and interact with trans-membrane CAIX first,
thus realizing the selectivity to CAIX in living cells. Mean-
while, western blot showed that CA-Re treatment could
significantly inhibit the expression level of CAIX and HIF-1a
in hypoxic MDA-MB-231 cells in a dose-dependent manner
(Figure 1 E).
Moreover, we evaluated the effect of CAIX expression
level on CA-Re uptake was studied by ICP-MS. After the
same CA-Re treatment (10 mM, 3–6 h), the ReI content in
MDA-MB-231 cells incubated under hypoxia was higher than
that under normoxia (Figure 1 D), which was consistent with
the fact that CAIX expression level was higher in hypoxic
than normoxic cancer cell. Under the same condition, once
the hypoxic cells were pre-treated with AAZ (400 mM, 6 h),
the intracellular ReI content was substantially reduced,
further verifying that CAIX was the binding target of CA-
Re. These results indicated that CA-Re possessed the CAIX
binding and intervention capacity, endowing it to anchor on
the cell membrane and alleviate tumor hypoxia to a certain
extent, which would be conductive to the in situ production of
ROS and improvement of PDT efficiency.
The (photo)cytotoxicity of CA-Re against breast cancer
(MDA-MB-231) and normal (MCF-10A) cell lines were
investigated using 48 h MTT assay (Table 1). Under dark
condition, CA-Re was nearly non-toxic (darkIC50 > 100 mM)
towards all tested cell lines, probably due to the membrane-
localization avoiding the high toxicity in cytoplasm. Upon
425 nm irradiation (20 mW cm@2, 10 min) under nomorxia
(20 % O2), CA-Re exhibited substantially increased cytotox-
icity against cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231) with nanomolar
lightIC50 values (7.3 nM), thus giving very high phototoxicity
index (PI) values of 13 700, which was about 2 times higher
than that of TLD1433. By comparison, the phototoxicity of
CA-Re against MCF-10A cells was ca. 830 times lower,
indicating that CA-Re had a certain selectivity (SI value of
16.4) to kill cancer cells while the cancer selectivity of
TLD1433 was 3 times lower. Considering the limitation of
tumor hypoxia on PDT efficiency, the phototoxicity of CA-Re
was also evaluated under hypoxia (1 % O2). In the case of
hypoxic MDA-MB-231 cell lines, the lightIC50 values were still
in nanomolar range (20 nM), giving PI values as high as 5000.
This verified the extremely high PDT efficiency of CA-Re
even under hypoxia.

We also studied the phototoxicity of CA-Re under 525 nm
irradiation (15 mW cm@2, 60 min) because this wavelength
was already used by TLD1433 in clinical trials. Under this
irradiation, CA-Re still exhibited nanomolar-level photo-
toxicity against MDA-MB-231 cells, and the lightIC50 values
were determined to be 76 nM and 190 nM in normoxia and
hypoxia, respectively, which were similar to that of TLD1433
under the same condition. By comparison, the phototoxicity
against MCF-10A cells under the same irradiation was much
smaller (lightIC50 value of 390 nM ), which still gave a certain
selectivity (SI value of 5.1) to kill cancer cells. It was worth
mentioning that although the phototoxicity of CA-Re under
525 nm was ca.10-times lower than that under 425 nm
irradiation, the lightIC50 values were still at nanomolar level
and the PI values were as high as ca. 500–1300 under both
normoxia and hypoxia. Wavelength of 633 nm (20 mW cm@2,
60 min) was also used to irradiate CA-Re treated cancerous
and normal cells but none phototoxicity was observed (Fig-
ure S13). This indicated that more biocompatible 525 nm
irradiation could be used instead of 425 nm for the in vivo
investigation.
To understand the antitumor mechanism of CA-Re, we
characterized the specific ROS produced in MDA-MB-231
cells during PDT. Firstly, the total ROS level in CA-Re
treated normoxic cells were found negligible in the dark but
substantially increased in a concentration-dependent manner
upon 425 nm irradiation (20 mW cm@2, 10 min), as indicated
by the gradually enhanced fluorescence from 2’,7’-dichloro-
fluorescein (DCF), a commercial ROS indicator (Figure 2 A
and S14). Pre-incubation of cells with ROS inhibitors usually
decreases the PDT induced ROS production, but the decline
caused by different inhibitors was found very different. As
shown in flow cytometry (Figure 2 B,C and S15), take
normoxic cells treated with CA-Re (100 nM) plus irradiation
as the control, pre-incubating cells with NaN3 and d-mannitol
(1O2 and COH specific scavenger, respectively) only caused
a slight decrease in the fluorescence intensity of DCF,
indicating that 1O2 and COH accounted for only a small part
of the total ROS induced by CA-Re. In contrast, pre-
incubation with tiron (CO2@ specific
scavenger), ebselen and Trolox
(OONO@ and ROOC specific scav-
enger) could effectively quench the
DCF fluorescence, indicating that
CO2@ and lipid peroxidation ac-
counted for the vast majority of
CA-Re PDT induced ROS.
To ascertain this, CLSM was
performed using a commercial CO2@
indicator (dihydroethidium, DHE)
to stain CA-Re treated cells, which
showed that the indicators fluo-
rescence was negligible in the dark
but substantially increased upon
irradiation (Figure S16). The intra-
cellular lipid peroxidation was also
verified by measuring the intracel-
lular concentration of malondial-
dehyde (MDA), a lipid peroxida-
tion indicator. Thiobarbituric acid (TBA) assay showed that
the MDA level was negligible in control cells but substantially
increased in a dose dependent manner after CA-Re +
irradiation treatment (Figure 2 D). These results demonstrat-
ed the capability of CA-Re to produce CO2@ and lipid
peroxidation as the cytotoxic species responsible for the
PDT induced cancer cell death. As mentioned earlier, CA-Re
was always anchored on the cell membrane mainly composed
of phospholipids, it was highly possible that lipid peroxidation
occurred due to the oxidation damage of membrane compo-
nents, which was caused by the attack of nearby CO2@ or the
direct electron transfer from the excited state of Re complex.
Then CA-Re PDT mediated cell death and a series of
subsequent domino effects were studied in detail using MDA-
MB-231 cells. PDT usually induced cell apoptosis, however,
CLSM observation in real-time showed none of apoptotic
characteristics such as cell shrinkage, nuclear fragmentation
or formation of apoptotic bodies during CA-Re PDT. Instead,
the dying cells blew out large bubbles from the plasma
membrane and the whole cell was typically swollen, whereas
the cell nucleus remained intact (Figure 2 E and CLSM video
in SI). Meanwhile, most dead cells were observed not to float
like apoptotic cells, but still tightly attached to the culture
plate during CA-Re PDT. Scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) showed that the cell corpses looked like fried eggs
with flattening cytoplasm and intact nucleus located on the
main plane of the cell body, which was quite different from
the control cells and the apoptotic cells induced by dimethyl
sulfoxide (DMSO) (Figure 2 F). These morphology features
exactly resembled pyroptosis, suggesting that CA-Re PDT
induced pyroptotic cell death. To ascertain this death pattern,
western blot was performed to measure the GSDMD-N
activation. As shown in Figure 2 G, the expression level of
GSDMD-N and cleaved caspase-1 in CA-Re treated cells was
significantly increased upon 425 nm irradiation, indicating the
efficient cleavage of GSDMD and caspase-1 activation after
CA-Re PDT. As glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a lipid
peroxidation defense enzyme, was recently reported with
a negative regulatory effect on GSDMD cleavage and

Based on the membrane localization and imaging ability,
CA-Re could self-report the PDT induced membrane rupture
due to its membrane imaging ability. As shown in Figure 3 A,
most CA-Re still remained on the cell membrane rather than
entering the cytoplasm even when the cell membrane was
destroyed after CA-Re PDT treatment, thus clearly tracking
the gradual loss of membrane integrity. Moreover, the
expression level of CAIX in CA-Re treated cells was also
decreased in a light-dose dependent manner accompanied by
the down-regulation of HIF-1a. The inactivation of mem-
brane anchored protein (CAIX) provided further evidence
proving the membrane rupture (Figure 3 B). These results
suggested that CAIX anchored CA-Re could induce cell
membrane rupture and GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, which
might synergistically promote tumor immunogenicity.

The in vivo PDT efficacy and systemic immune responses
were studied on an immunocompetent mice model bearing
homogenotypic 4T1 tumors instead of human MDA-MB-231
tumors. Moreover, more biocompatible 525 nm irradiation
(15 mW cm@2, 1 h) was used instead of 425 nm for in vivo
investigation because this irradiation was already used by
TLD1433 in clinical trials. MTT assay showed that the
phototoxicity of CA-Re against hypoxic 4T1 cells under
525 nm irradiation was determined to be 220 nM with a high
PI value of 455, which was comparable to that against MDA-
MB-231 cells (lightIC50 = 190 nM, PI = 526) under the same
treatment (Table S3). Before we started in vivo investigation

To evaluate the anti-tumor immune responses, we profiled
infiltrating immune cells in vivo. First, the maturation of
dendritic cell (DC) in tumor draining lymph nodes was
assessed, which was essential for initiating adaptive anti-
tumor immunity. As shown in flow cytometry (Figure 4 E), the
percentage of CD80+CD86+ DCs in CA-Re + light group was
significantly increased from 16.0 % to 28.4 % compared with
the groups treated with light or CA-Re only, indicating the
increased level of DC maturation after CA-Re PDT treat-
ment. The antigen-presenting capacity of DCs was also
evaluated by measuring the serum level of tumor associated
antigen (TAA). Compared with control group, tumor necrosis
factor a (TNF-a), interleukin 6 and 12 (IL-6 and IL-12p70)
were substantially increased accompanied by the decrease of
immunosuppressive cytokine interleukin 10 (IL-10) in the
CA-Re + light group, indicating the enhanced antigen-
presenting capacity of DCs after CA-Re PDT treatment
(Figure S19). Next, we quantified the cytotoxic T cells (CD8+)
and helper T cells (CD4+) in distant tumors as indicators of
adaptive immune response. As shown in Figure 4 F,G, the
proportion of tumor-infiltrating CD3+CD8+ and CD3+CD4+
T lymphocytes in CA-Re + light group was ca. 28.3 % and
32.3 %, respectively, both of which were 2–3 folds higher than
that in the light only group (10.1–14.1 %) or CA-Re only
group (15.9–17.0 %). These results indicated that CA-Re PDT
could recruit cytotoxic T lymphocytes to activate adaptive
immune response. Immunohistochemistry analysis was also
performed on the primary and distant tumor tissues, both of
which showed enhanced tumor-infiltrating CD8 in CA-Re +
light group compared with control groups, further indicative
of CA-Re PDT-elicited adaptive immunity (Figure 4 H).
Besides, the level of pyroptosis-related inflammasome IL-18
and IL-1b was also found increased, suggesting the occur-
rence of pyroptotic cell death (Figure S20). These in vivo
results jointly indicated that CA-Re PDT could cause
pyroptosis of cancer cells to enhance the tumor immunoge-
nicity, which promoted the activation of DC and triggered T
cell-dependent immune responses, thus not only possessing
excellent anti-tumor efficacy but also provoking adaptive
anti-tumor immunity in high-efficiency.
The side effect of CA-Re + Light was also evaluated
throughout the whole in vivo experimental period. We found
that the body weights of BALB/c mice bearing 4T1 tumors
remained stable before and after CA-Re treatment regardless
of irradiation (Figure S21). Histochemical analysis by H&E
staining exhibited no obvious pathological abnormalities in
the main organs (liver, heart, lung, kidney, and spleen) of CA-
Re PDT treated mice (Figure S22). These results indicated
that no systemic toxicity was caused in the process of CA-Re
PDT.
Conclusion
We designed a novel CAIX-anchored ReI photosensitizer
CA-Re, which was the first reported metal complex-based
pyroptosis inducer, capable of effectively activating the anti-
tumor immune responses in vivo. With the low dark toxicity
(darkIC50 > 100 mM) and nanomolar-level phototoxicity
(lightIC50 = 20 nM) under hypoxia, CA-Re stood out as one
of the most hypoxia-active photosensitizers. Based on CAIX
anchoring characteristics, CA-Re was always fixed on the cell
membrane to ensure the in situ production of ROS for
membrane rupture and self-report the loss of membrane
integrity simultaneously. More interestingly, CA-Re could
evoke GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis for enhanced tumor
immunogenicity, eventually promoting the maturation of DC
cells and activating T cells dependent adaptive immune
responses to eliminate distant tumors at the same time as
destroying primary tumors. This work suggests that design of
novel photosensitizer capable of overcoming the hypoxic and
non-immunogenic tumor environment is very promising for
photodynamic immunotherapy.
