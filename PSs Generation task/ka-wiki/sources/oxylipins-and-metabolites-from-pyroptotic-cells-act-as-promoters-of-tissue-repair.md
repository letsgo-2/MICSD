Pyroptosis is a lytic cell death mode that helps limit the spread of infections and is
also linked to pathology in sterile inflammatory diseases and autoimmune diseases1–4
.
During pyroptosis, inflammasome activation and the engagement of caspase-1 lead
to cell death, along with the maturation and secretion of the inflammatory cytokine
interleukin-1β (IL-1β). The dominant effect of IL-1β in promoting tissue inflammation
has clouded the potential influence of other factors released from pyroptotic cells.
Here, using a system in which macrophages are induced to undergo pyroptosis
without IL-1β or IL-1α release (denoted Pyro−1
), we identify unexpected beneficial
effects of the Pyro−1 secretome. First, we noted that the Pyro−1 supernatants upregulated
gene signatures linked to migration, cellular proliferation and wound healing.
Consistent with this gene signature, Pyro−1 supernatants boosted migration of primary
fibroblasts and macrophages, and promoted faster wound closure in vitro and
improved tissue repair in vivo. In mechanistic studies, lipidomics and metabolomics
of the Pyro−1 supernatants identified the presence of both oxylipins and metabolites,
linking them to pro-wound-healing effects. Focusing specifically on the oxylipin
prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), we find that its synthesis is induced de novo during pyroptosis,
downstream of caspase-1 activation and cyclooxygenase-2 activity; further, PGE2
synthesis occurs late in pyroptosis, with its release dependent on gasdermin D pores
opened during pyroptosis. As for the pyroptotic metabolites, they link to immune cell
infiltration into the wounds, and polarization to CD301+ macrophages. Collectively,
these data advance the concept that the pyroptotic secretome possesses oxylipins
and metabolites with tissue repair properties that may be harnessed therapeutically.
Pyroptosis is a form of lytic cell death due to assembly of large mem-
brane pores composed of gasdermin family proteins. Gasdermin D
(GSDMD) is the primary executor of pyroptosis induced by cytosolic
protein complexes called inflammasomes 1–4
. Once assembled, the
inflammasomes lead to caspase-1-mediated cleavage and release of
inflammatory cytokines such as the interleukins IL-18, IL-1β and IL-1α
through the GSDMD cell membrane pores1–4
. Induction of canonical
inflammasome assembly involves two signals: signal 1 is often pro-
vided by a Toll-like receptor (TLR) ligand leading to NF-κB-mediated
transcriptional upregulation of inflammasome components and
pro-inflammatory cytokines such as pro-IL-1β; signal 2 can ensue
from a wide variety of triggers (such as ATP, microbial toxins and
crystals that cause lysosomal damage) enabling inflammasome
assembly, recruitment and activation of caspase-1, and ultimately
cellular lysis 5,6
.
Pyroptosis is observed during infections to limit the infectious
spread 7–9
, and in sterile inflammatory pathologies such as autoim-
mune diseases, cancer and atherosclerosis. Inhibiting pyroptosis
also provides benefits in certain non-infectious settings 10–12 . How-
ever, the influence of pyroptosis and GSDMD activation cannot be
solely diminished by neutralizing the cytokines such as IL-1β and
IL-18 13,14 , suggesting the presence of additional factors. Besides
these cytokines, pyroptotic death can also release other pro-
teins induced during pyroptosis (‘inducible’ damage-associated
molecular patterns) or constitutively expressed (constitutive
damage-associated molecular patterns)15
. Further, inflammasome
activation can occur without IL-1β or IL-1α release 16
. However, which
other small molecules and metabolites may also be released dur-
ing pyroptosis, and their influence on the tissue neighbourhood,
is unclear.

To identify the effects of the pyroptotic secretome that are inde-
pendent of IL-1β, we utilized the ability of the NLRP1B inflammasome
to induce pyroptosis without TLR priming 17,18 . In this model, the
anthrax lethal toxin (LeTox) induces pyroptotic cell death through
the NLRP1B inflammasome in bone marrow-derived macrophages
(BMDMs) without eliciting IL-1β or IL-1α release17,18
. Using this approach,
we uncover an unexpected beneficial effect of both oxylipins and
metabolites in the pyroptotic secretome capable of promoting
tissue repair.
Gene signatures induced by Pyro−1 supernatants
BMDMs from C57BL/6 mice are resistant to LeTox (‘control’). However,
BMDMs from Nlrp1b-transgenic C57BL/6 mice (Nlrp1b-Tg) undergo
pyroptotic death when incubated with LeTox. Most relevant for this
work, this LeTox-induced pyroptosis occurs without IL-1β or IL-1α
release 17,18
. For ease, we refer to this pyroptosis as Pyro −1 , to denote
the absence of signal 1 triggers and the lack of IL-1β or IL-1α release
(Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). Furthermore, when the Nlrp1b-Tg mac-
rophages are engineered to lack the genes encoding caspase-1 and
caspase-11 (Casp1/11-KO), the same LeTox trigger fails to trigger pyrop-
tosis, and instead induces apoptosis17,18 (‘Apop’; Extended Data Fig. 1a).
This allows live, pyroptotic and apoptotic secretome comparison after
the same cell death trigger.
To address how the Pyro−1 secretome may communicate with other
cells, we added supernatants of LeTox-treated BMDMs from control and
Nlrp1b-Tg mice (Fig. 1a) to naive BMDMs from C57BL/6 mice (‘M0 target
macrophages’) for 4 h and carried out RNA sequencing (RNA-seq).
Pyro −1 supernatants induced notable gene expression changes in M0
target macrophages compared to control supernatants (Fig. 1a); how-
ever, supernatants of live cells from these genotypes did not induce
similar gene expression changes (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Analysing
the upregulated and downregulated genes, we made several notable
observations. First, Pyro −1 supernatants neither induced genes for
inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 or TNF, nor induced the release of
these cytokines from M0 target macrophages (Extended Data Fig. 1d).
Next, we noted a marked enrichment of genes associated with cellular
migration, proliferation and immune response by Pyro−1 supernatants
(Fig. 1b, Extended Data Fig. 1e and Supplementary Table 1). Further
analysis identified a number of genes previously linked to wound
healing and tissue repair, such as those encoding thrombospondin 1
(Thbs1) 19
, vascular endothelial growth factor (Veg f ) 20
, nuclear recep-
tor 4a1 (Nr4a1) 21 , sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 1 (S1pr1) 22 and
transglutaminase 2 (Tgm2); by contrast, lipid metabolism genes were
downmodulated by Pyro−1 supernatants in the M0 target macrophages
(Extended Data Fig. 1f–h). Notably, the apoptotic supernatants after the
same LeTox trigger did not induce genes expression linked to wound
healing (Fig. 1c).
In contrast to pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-1α that require NF-κB-dependent
transcriptional upregulation by TLR ligands, IL-18 is constitutively
expressed in macrophages, and released during pyroptosis18
. As Pyro−1
supernatants did contain IL-18 (Fig. 1c), we tested the contribution of
IL-18 to the Pyro−1 gene signature. We crossed the Nlrp1b-Tg mice with
IL-18-null mice to obtain Nlrp1-Tg Il18-knockout (KO) mice. BMDMs
from the Nlrp1b-Tg Il18-KO mice underwent comparable pyroptosis
following LeTox trigger (Pyro−1-18 ), and the Pyro−1-18 supernatants still
induced the Nr4a1, Tgm2, Thbs1 and Vegf genes in target macrophages
(Fig. 1c). Thus, gene signatures induced by Pyro−1 supernatants were
largely IL-18 independent.
To better understand factor(s) that may contribute to the Pyro −1
gene signature, we first tested the contribution of proteins present
in the supernatants (as proteins are released during pyroptosis 15
). We
treated control, Pyro −1
, Pyro−1-18 and Apop supernatants with proteinase
K (ProtK) followed by heat inactivation (95 °C; Fig. 1d, top). Although
proteinase K effectively degraded proteins in Pyro −1 supernatants
(Extended Data Fig. 1i), the ProtK-treated supernatants still induced
the expression of Thbs1, Vegf, Nr4a1 and Tgm2 (Fig. 1d) in M0 target
macrophages; of note, there was a decrease in Cx3cl1 expression levels
after ProtK-treatment, suggesting protein-dependent induction of
Cx3cl1 (Extended Data Fig. 1j).
Metabolic mediators in the Pyro−1 secretome
We next investigated whether small-molecule mediators in the Pyro −1
supernatants may play a role using two types of bioinformatics
approach. First, when we carried out NicheNet analysis, which helps
assign specific gene signatures to possible ligand–receptor pairs 23
, no
obvious pattern emerged for Pyro−1 supernatants; after discussion with
the original authors of this algorithm, we discovered that NicheNet is
more suitable for protein ligands than metabolites or lipid mediators.
Second, to identify metabolic hubs that might be enriched in the M0
target macrophages owing to cellular signalling initiated by unique
molecules in the Pyro−1 supernatants, we compiled a Pyro−1 gene signa-
ture comprising 103 upregulated genes (Supplementary Table 1). When
we compared them against a compendium of 24,970 public mouse
gene expression datasets24 using the GESECA algorithm25 (Methods,
Extended Data Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 1), this pointed to
upstream lipid-mediated signalling, in particular, PGE2 -dependent
signalling.
On the basis of this insight, we next carried out lipidomics of the
Pyro−1 supernatants, focusing on prostaglandins and oxylipins that led
to several notable observations. First, lipidomics showed enrichment
of oxylipins, including PGE2, in Pyro−1 supernatants that were absent in
control supernatants. Notably, these oxylipins were absent in the apop-
totic supernatants from Nlrp1b-Tg Casp1/11-KO macrophages treated
with the same LeTox trigger (Fig. 2a). To assess what fraction of the
Pyro−1 gene signature could be attributable to PGE 2, we used a publicly
available RNA-seq dataset in which PGE 2 was added directly onto M0
mouse BMDMs (similar to the case for our target macrophages; Gene
Expression Omnibus (GEO) accession GSE119509)26
; notably, half of the
genes induced by Pyro−1 supernatants overlapped (Fig. 2b), including
many of the highly upregulated genes induced by Pyro−1 supernatants
(Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 1f,g). This suggested that PGE2 may be a
major component of Pyro −1 supernatants; this also implied that other
factors in the Pyro −1 supernatants probably contribute to the rest of
the Pyro −1 gene signature.
As the Pyro−1 gene signature includes cell proliferation and migration
genes, and PGE2 is linked to wound healing (Fig. 1b), we initially tested
the Pyro−1 supernatants in wound closure assays in vitro. When added
to wounds created on primary mouse fibroblast monolayers, Pyro−1
supernatants increased wound closure compared to live or apoptotic
supernatants (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 2b); of note, Pyro−1-18 super-
natants showed comparable closure in these conditions, suggesting
that IL-18 was dispensable in this context (Fig. 2c). As cell migration
affects wound closure in these assays, we tested whether Pyro −1 super-
natants could induce migration of primary mouse ear fibroblasts and
BMDMs in a Transwell migration assay. Pyro −1 supernatants induced
greater migration of fibroblasts and macrophages, and this effect was
again independent of IL-18 (Fig. 2d).
We next investigated whether this effect with mouse macrophages
also occurs with human myeloid cells. We triggered pyroptosis in
human CD14+ blood monocytes with the NLRC4 inflammasome ago-
nist NeedleTox. These pyroptotic supernatants also induced a sharp
increase in the rate of fibroblast wound closure (Fig. 2e). Although
the pyroptotic human monocytes released small amounts of IL-1β in
these conditions (about fivefold less compared to mouse macrophages
triggered with lipopolysaccharide (LPS); Extended Data Fig. 2c), this
did not interfere with wound closure under these conditions. When
we assayed the release of PGE 2 , we could readily detect PGE 2 release in
the human pyroptotic supernatants (Fig. 2e).

After the pyroptotic trigger, inflammasome activation leads to
caspase-1 activity that is critical for induction of pyroptosis. To test
whether caspase-1 activity contributed to PGE2 release, we engineered
human THP-1 monocytic cells expressing an inducible version of
caspase-1 (using an FKBP-based dimerization approach; Methods and
Extended Data Fig. 3c). Pyroptosis induction through direct activation
of caspase-1 also led to a significant level of PGE2 release (Fig. 3e). This
supports the concept that de novo prostaglandin synthesis during
pyroptosis occurs downstream of caspase-1 activation.
We next tested whether the GSDMD pore opening, which occurs
downstream of caspase-1 activation, contributes to PGE 2 release, using
genetic and pharmacological approaches. For the genetic approach,
we generated HoxB8 macrophages using progenitors from Nlrp1b-Tg
Gsdmd-KO mice (Methods). Pyroptosis induction in Gsdmd-KO mac-
rophages does not lead to PGE2 release in the supernatants; by contrast,
control HoxB8 macrophages from Nlrp1b-Tg mice secreted signifi-
cant amounts of PGE 2 . Pharmacological treatment of Nlrp1b-Tg mac-
rophages with disulfiram, an inhibitor of GSDMD pores 29
, completely
inhibited PGE2 release from Nlrp1b-Tg macrophages (Fig. 3f). Thus,
PGE 2 release from pyroptotic cells requires GSDMD pore formation
during pyroptosis.
To test whether the PGE 2 released into the Pyro −1 supernatants is
functional in vivo, we used a colonic punch biopsy model of wound
healing in which multiple wounds are surgically introduced in different
parts of the distal colon per mouse30–32 , and the tissue repair is abso-
lutely dependent on the localized presence of bioactive PGE 2 . When
the colonic wounding is carried out with systemic administration of
the COX2 inhibitor NS-398, this substantially delayed the wound heal-
ing, leading to fibrin clots over the wounded areas31
. We administered
control or Pyro−1 supernatants locally (through enema), and the area of
wound closure was assessed on day 4 at the injury sites. The Pyro−1 super-
natants strongly promoted colonic wound repair in this model (Fig. 3g).
Consistent with wound-associated epithelial cells playing a critical role
in intestinal barrier re-establishment, and claudin-4 being a specific
marker induced by PGE2 on wound-associated epithelial cells33,34
, our
data showed that claudin-4-positive epithelial cells stretched across the
wound bed (Fig. 3h and Extended Data Fig. 3d). These data confirmed
the bioactivity of PGE 2 in Pyro −1 supernatants.
Pyroptotic secretome in wound repair
Next we tested the Pyro −1 supernatants in a model of full-thickness
skin wounding in mice (schematic in Fig. 4a). This model only partially
relies on PGE 2 for tissue recovery, allowing us to address the contri-
bution of PGE 2 as well as other components in Pyro−1 supernatants.
When Pyro −1 supernatants were administered intradermally on days
0, 1 and 2 post-wounding, there was a positive effect on wound closure
(Fig. 4a). Notably, the maximal beneficial effect was noted on day 2
and day 4 post-wounding, during the inflammatory phase of repair35
.
When we took skin sections and stained them for Ki67 and ITGA5 to
monitor proliferation and migration in the wound epithelium along
with anti-cytokeratin-1436 (for keratinocytes), Pyro −1 did not cause
significant changes in the migration or proliferation of keratinocytes
in the wound epithelial tongue (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b). However, in
haematoxylin and eosin staining on the entire wounds, a significant
decrease in granulation tissue thickness was seen in Pyro −1 -treated
conditions (Extended Data Fig. 4c). In the wound bed, Ki67+ staining
revealed an increase in the level of cell proliferation (Extended Data
Fig. 4d, top). Further, immune staining in wound sections collected at
day 4 post-wounding with antibodies against α-SMA and CD31 showed
increased levels of revascularization and fibroblast repopulation in the
Pyro−1
-treated wound beds (Extended Data Fig. 4d, bottom).
We next assessed the relative contribution of PGE 2 (and other pros-
taglandins) by administering supernatants from aspirin-treated Pyro −1
cells. Pyro−1(Asp) supernatants showed a partial pro-wound healing effect
(P = 0.23, control versus Pyro −1(Asp) ; Fig. 4b), relatable to roughly half of
the gene expression signature being attributable to PGE 2 (see above;
Fig. 2b). This suggested that components other than oxylipins in the
Pyro −1 supernatants probably contribute to improved tissue repair.
Although apoptotic cell supernatants do not contain oxylipins (Figs. 2a
and 4c), the apoptotic secretome is known to contain metabolites capa -
ble of promoting an anti-inflammatory and tissue repair signature37
.
Apoptotic cell supernatants had smaller, yet significant improvement
in wound closure (Fig. 4d and Extended Data Fig. 4e). This prompted
us to examine metabolites in the Pyro −1 supernatants that may work
with oxylipins.
We performed untargeted metabolomics of >3,000 compounds or
features in Pyro−1 supernatants at 90 and 120 min after LeTox trigger
(these time points were chosen because the 7-AAD dye uptake occurs
already by 90 min after LeTox trigger, and the release of the larger LDH
is best seen at 120 min; Fig. 3a,b). As a comparison, we also performed
untargeted metabolomics of supernatants of primary macrophages
from control mice (that do not undergo cell death) and Nlrp1b-Tg
Casp1/11-KO mice that undergo apoptosis instead of pyroptosis. We
made three notable observations. First, the Pyro −1 secretome contained
a number of ‘unique’ metabolites as well as metabolites that were also
present in the apoptotic cell secretome (‘shared’), although greater
levels of the ‘shared’ metabolites were seen in the Pyro−1 conditions
(Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 5a). Quantitative metabolic analysis on
select compounds confirmed the specific metabolites in Pyro−1 super-
natants (Extended Data Fig. 5b). Second, even at 90 min after pyroptosis
induction (before LDH release; Fig. 3b), many released metabolites
were detectable, and these were enhanced at 120 min (Extended Data
Fig. 5c). Third, although some metabolites were enriched only in 120 min
supernatants, this was not due to increasing GSDMD pore size, as some
smaller molecular weight metabolites were seen only at 120 min (for
example, ornithine, xanthine and thioproline); this may reflect contin-
ued changes in the metabolic composition of the pyroptotic cells and/
or their release properties during the course of pyroptosis (Extended
Data Fig. 5c). Notably, some of the metabolites in the Pyro−1 secretome
contained signalling metabolites previously linked to tissue repair and
anti-inflammatory signalling (for example, spermidine37
, putrescine38
,
taurine, methyladenosine 39 and fumarate11
; Extended Data Fig. 5c). Thus,
metabolites in the pyroptotic secretome probably contribute to the
beneficial wound healing seen with Pyro−1 supernatants.

matory phase, is accompanied by rapid infiltration of macrophages
and neutrophils to the injury site that help prevent infections while
promoting wound repair. To better understand mechanistically the
contribution of metabolites in the Pyro−1 supernatants towards modu-
lating immune cell migration and/or polarization at the wound site
in vivo, the full-thickness skin wounds (day 3) were collected, and the
immune cell infiltration was analysed (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Wounds
treated with Pyro−1 supernatants showed an increase in the number of

Discussion
Although pyroptosis is generally thought to be tissue damaging, the
multiple complementary pieces of data presented here advance a new
concept that the pyroptotic secretome lacking IL-1β can be a potent
promoter of tissue repair. First, the Pyro −1 secretome contains both
oxylipins and metabolites that coordinately promote wound closure
in vivo. The observations correlate with the counter-intuitive findings
that in some cases, necrotic cells can also release PGE 2 , which sup-
presses inflammation, and promote repair44
. Mechanistically, this cor-
relates with the induction of gene signatures in naive macrophages
that are linked to proliferation and cell migration, promoting tissue
repair. Second, we find that the production of oxylipins such as PGE 2
occurs de novo, downstream of caspase-1 activation, with PGE2 release
dependent on GASDMD pores; notably, the activity of cyclooxygenases,
which induce the prostaglandins, seems to occur during the final stages
of pyroptosis, suggesting a temporal regulation that remains to be
deciphered. Third, from a bioinformatics analysis, PGE2 contributed
to about 50% of the gene signature induced by Pyro−1 supernatants,
and consistent with this, oxylipins provided a partial benefit towards
healing in the full-thickness wound model, with the remaining effect
attributable to metabolites in the Pyro−1 supernatants. Although lethal
eicosanoid storms have been observed after systemic injection of
inflammasome-activating toxins45
, our data suggest that localized
(and perhaps lower level) generation of eicosanoids such as PGE2 at
the site of injury by pyroptotic death could have beneficial effects.
Fourth, the metabolites in the Pyro−1 supernatants promote wound site
CD45+
F4/80+
CD301+
IL-27+ macrophages, a subpopulation that has been
linked to wound healing41,42,46
. It is possible that pyroptotic supernatants
influence efferocytic capacity, and/or intercellular communication 47,48
at the wound site to trigger closure. Members of the gasdermin family
of proteins have been linked to tissue repair and central nervous system
homeostasis, although whether these effects are mediated through
the pyroptotic secretome remains to be defined 14,49
. Collectively, these
data advance the concept that pyroptotic cell death probably balances
tissue injury with tissue regeneration, and that harnessing the power
of the pyroptotic secretome may be beneficial in promoting wound
repair. This matches well with the beneficial effects of blocking IL-1β in
improving wound repair in diabetic conditions43,50
, in which the contin-
ued presence of oxylipins and metabolites in the pyroptotic secretome
could continue to provide a therapeutic benefit.

Methods
Mice
C57BL/6 female mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory at 6
weeks of age. Nlrp1b-Tg, Nlrp1b-Tg Casp1/11-KO and Il18-KO mice have
been described previously17,18
. Nlrp1b-Tg Il18-KO mice were generated
by crossing Nlrp1b-Tg mice with Il18-KO mice to obtain homozygous
knockout for Il18 and heterozygous knockout for the Nlrp1b-Tg allele.
Rosa26-TdT Tg; Sox2-cre mice were a gift from P. Vandenabeele. Mice
were housed under 14 h/10 h (light/dark) light cycles, at 21 °C and 60%
humidity. These conditions are checked and maintained by vivarium
staff daily. All animal work was approved by the VIB-UGent Center for
Inflammation Research Ethical Committee and the University of Ghent
Animal Ethics Committee.
Primary cells and cell lines
All reported healthy donors provided written informed consent for
participation in the study, in accordance with International Council
for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for
Human Use Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The research protocol
was approved by the local ethics committee of Ghent University Hospi-
tal, and approval for the use of blood products was obtained from the
Red Cross (G20221107). Mouse BMDMs were generated by culturing
mouse bone marrow cells in Gibco RPMI 1640, supplemented with
50 ng ml −1 of mouse MCSF, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% peni-
cillin–streptomycin for 6 days in a humidified atmosphere containing
5% CO2 at 37 °C. BMDMs were then detached from culture plates using
Accutase cell detachment reagent (423201; BioLegend), counted and
re-seeded in either 6-cm culture dishes or 12-well plates and allowed
to rest overnight before use in further experiments. For generating
primary mouse ear fibroblasts, mouse ears from female Rosa26-TdT Tg;
Sox2-cre were cut and placed in 70% ethanol for 5–10 min, and then the
tissue was allowed to completely air dry. The piece was cut into small
pieces (about 3 mm in size) and incubated with 0.5 ml of 0.2 μm sterile
filtered collagenase A (Type XI 1,500 U ml −1 in 1× Hank’s balanced salt
solution (HBSS)) at 37 °C for 90 min with occasional vortexing. The
tube was centrifuged at 1,000g for 5 min at room temperature, and the
pellet was washed with 1 ml HBSS. A 0.5 ml volume of 0.25% trypsin–
EDTA was added to the tube and mixed thoroughly, and the tissue was
incubated at 37 °C for 20 min with occasional vortexing followed by
centrifugation at 1,000g for 5 min at room temperature. The pellet
was again washed once with HBSS. The pellet was then resuspended in
2.5 ml DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% penicillin–streptomycin and transferred
to a suspension in a 6-well tissue culture plate containing 2 ml medium.
The cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 3–4 days. Cells were used
within 14 days of isolation. J2-3T3 mouse fibroblasts were obtained from
ATCC and cultured as instructed by the supplier. HoxB8-ER progenitors
from C57BL/6, Nlrp1b-Tg, Nlrp1b-Tg Il18-KO, Nlrp1b-Tg Casp1/11-KO and
Nlrp1b-Tg Gsdmd-KO mice were generated as described previously 51 .
Supernatant generation for in vitro and in vivo assays
Mouse BMDMs. Bone marrow progenitors or ER-HoxB8 progenitors

, Quadratech). The cells were incubated for up to 3 h at 37 °C

Primary human monocytes. PBMCs were isolated from buffy coats

130-096-537, Miltenyi Biotec). After isolation, 5 × 106 monocytes were

at 1,700g for 5 min at 4 °C and clarified supernatants were snap-frozen
in liquid nitrogen. Aliquots were stored at −80 °C until further analysis.
Generation of THP-1-iC1
Cloning of pLEX307_imCasp1deltaCARD. pLEX307 was obtained
from Addgene (number 41392) and linearized with NheI-HF and SpeI-HF
(NEB). The vector backbone was gel-purified following separation
through electrophoresis. The sequence encoding for mouse caspase-1
lacking the CARD domain was preceded by a sequence encoding for the
dimerization domain FKBP12(F36V), with a GSG-linker separating the
protein domains. Further, a Kozak-sequence was included before the
start codon and 50-base-pair overlaps were added to the 3′ and 5′ end
to allow recombination into the pLEX307 backbone. This sequence was
ordered as a double-stranded DNA fragment (IDT) and cloned into the
linearized pLEX307 vector using Gibson assembly. The integrity of the
vector was verified by next-generation sequencing (Plasmidsaurus).
Virus generation. Lentiviral supernatants were generated by trans-
fecting HEK293T cells (ATCC) with pLEX307_imCasp1deltaCARD,
pCMV-VSV-G (Addgene, number 8454) and psPAX2 (Addgene, num-
ber 12260). The lentiviral supernatant was collected after 72 h and
centrifuged for 10 min at 2,000g. The supernatant was further filtered
through a 0.45-μm PVDF filter (Millipore) and stored at −80 °C until use.
Generation of THP-1 cells expressing inducible caspase-1. THP-1

out for 7 days and the population was further expanded afterwards.

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug treatment regimens
Continuous drug treatment. For COX1 and COX2 inhibition, on day

Discontinuous drug treatment. For COX1 and COX2 inhibition, on day

1640, supplemented with 50 ng ml −1 of murine MCSF, 10% FBS and 1%

warm serum-free, phenol red-free RPMI medium to remove the drugs.
This was followed by LeTox trigger supernatant collection at 120 min as
described above and PGE2 levels were determined using ELISA.
Kinetic drug treatment. For COX1 and COX2 inhibition, on day 6 after
differentiation, BMDMs from Nlrp1b-Tg or control mice were seeded
onto 6-cm dishes at a density of 5 million per dish in Gibco RPMI
1640, supplemented with 50 ng ml −1 of murine MCSF, 10% FBS and 1%

acid (aspirin) was added to the wells exposed to LeTox. Supernatants
were collected at 120 min post trigger as described above and the levels
of PGE2 were determined using ELISA.
Proteinase K treatment
Supernatants were treated with 1 mg ml−1 proteinase K (Bioline; BIO-

RNA-seq
Day-7 primary mouse BMDMs (M0 target macrophages) were incu-

instructions. After cluster generation, the library preparations were
sequenced on an Illumina platform, and paired-end reads were gener-
ated (Novogene).

Pyro −1 RNA-seq data analysis and GO analysis. Aligned reads (from

Metabolite measurements. The compounds were measured in the
cation and anion modes of capillary electrophoresis time-of-flight
(CE–TOF)-MS)-based metabolome analysis in the following conditions
Cationic metabolites (cation mode) were measured on the Agilent
CE–TOF-MS system (Agilent Technologies) using a fused silica capillary
i.d. 50 μm × 80 cm. Anionic metabolites (anion mode) were measured
on the Agilent CE–TOF-MS system (Agilent Technologies) using a fused
silica capillary i.d. 50 μm × 80 cm.
Data processing and analysis. Peaks detected in the CE–TOF-MS analy-
sis were extracted using automatic integration software (MasterHands
v.2.19.0.2 developed at Keio University) to obtain peak information,
which includes m/z, migration time (MT) and peak area. The peak area
was then converted to relative peak area. The peak detection limit
was determined on the basis of signal–noise ratio; S/N = 3. Putative
metabolites were then assigned from HMT’s standard library on the
basis of m/z and MT. The tolerance was ±0.5 min in MT and ±10 ppm
in m/z.
Absolute quantification was carried out on 110 targeted metabo-
lites. Metabolite concentrations were calculated by normalizing the
peak area of each metabolite with respect to the area of the inter-
nal standard and by using standard curves, which were obtained by
single-point (100 μM) calibrations. Hierarchical cluster analysis and
principal component analysis were carried out using statistical analysis
software developed at HMT. The profile of peaks with putative metab-
olites was represented on metabolic pathway maps using VANTED
(Visualization and Analysis of Networks containing Experimental Data)
software.

Transwell assay
Transwell migration assays were carried out by seeding 100 μl of M0
target macrophages in their culture medium at a density of 0.5 × 106

to the lower chamber as the chemoattractant of a 24-well plate with a

followed by 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining. Images of
the migrated cells were captured on the Nikon AZ1000M macroscope
at ×2 zoom, allowing a full field view of the membrane. Migrated cells
were counted using ImageJ.
