Intramolecular Charge Transfer-Based Conjugated Oligomer with
Fluorescence, Efficient Photodynamics, and Photothermal Activities
Rui Peng, Yufeng Luo, Chuang Yao, Qianling Cui,* Qing Wu, and Lidong Li*
Cite This: https://doi.org/10.1021/acsabm.1c00719 	Read Online

ABSTRACT: To develop efficient photoactive agents with
satisfactory fluorescence, photodynamic, and photothermal effects
is crucial for a phototherapeutic strategy to combat cancer diseases
and pathogenic microbes. Herein, a water-soluble donor−accept-
or−donor (D−A−D) structured conjugated oligomer was
designed and synthesized, consisting of two cyclopenta-dithio-
phene (CDT) units as the electron donor and boron
dipyrromethene (BODIPY) as the electron acceptor. Upon
excitation, dual emission was observed for CDT-BODIPY with
blue and red fluorescence peaks at 463 nm and at 730 nm, respectively, which was ascribed to intramolecular charge transfer (ICT).
Due to the ICT effect, the singlet-to-triplet intersystem crossing rate of CDT-BODIPY was also enhanced, leading to an outstanding
photodynamic behavior to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS). Meanwhile, its low bandgap also enabled it a moderate
photothermal capability with a conversion efficiency of 33.1%. Taking advantage of its phototriggered activities, this conjugated
oligomer exhibited an effective inhibition behavior on the pathogenic growth of Escherichia coli (E. coli), Staphylococcus aureus (S.
aureus), and Candida albicans (C. albicans), which can be guided by dual-wavelength fluorescence imaging. This D−A−D type
conjugated oligomer with balanced photophysical characteristics provides a promising strategy to imaging-guided photoactive
therapy.
KEYWORDS: conjugated oligomer, fluorescence, antibacterial, photodynamic therapy, photothermal therapy
■ INTRODUCTION
Microbial pathogens, which can induce infections and diseases,
pose a great threat to public health worldwide, and the growing
emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains as a result of
abuse of conventional antibiotics, making them more difficult
than before to combat. 1,2 In recent years, the fast developing
phototherapeutic strategy has been regarded as an alternative
solution, owing to its superior advantages including high
spatiotemporal selectivity, minimal invasion, and broad-
spectrum biocidal activity.3−6 This phototherapeutic strategy
can be mainly classified into photodynamic therapy (PDT)
and photothermal therapy (PTT) based on different working
mechanisms. PDT usually employs photosensitizers that
absorb light, sensitize molecular oxygen, and generate reactive
oxygen species (ROS) including singlet oxygen (1
O 2) and
various radicals, which are toxic to the surrounding organisms
and induce cancer cell or bacterial death by disrupting the
membrane structure and inhibiting the enzyme activity. 7
However, the depleting of local oxygen in the PDT process
is an important obstacle that impedes further generation of
ROS and weaken PDT efficiency in clinical application.8
However, PTT involves the use of photothermal agents which
are able to convert light to heat, elevate local temperature, and
destroy nearby microorganisms through hyperthermia ef-
fects.9−12 Principally, it requires PTT to rise to a temperature
of 50 °C to kill microbes, as higher than that causes cell
apoptosis (45 °C), which will cause thermal damage to
neighboring tissues and limit its practical use. These limitations
motivate the need for developing photoactive materials with
combinational capabilities of PDT and PTT for efficient
sterilization, especially those guided by fluorescence imaging,
which can precisely localize the cells and reduce side effects.
To integrate fluorescence, PDT, and PTT effects, one
commonly used method is to assemble each functional block
into a hybrid nanocomposite. For example, organic photo-
sensitizers have been loaded or conjugated with a photo-
thermal agent, like gold nanoparticles, to acquire light-triggered
synergistic PDT/PTT treatments.13,14 However, the complex-
ity of the fabrication process and the possible risk of biosafety
impede their further practical application. As an alternative
method, to develop a single material with multifunctional
photophysical properties including fluorescence, PDT, and
PTT effects is more attractive and demanding.

Conjugated polymers/oligomers have emerged as a
competitive class of photoactive agents for phototriggered
inactivation of cancer cells or microorganisms. 15−23 Due to its
large π-conjugated structure, conjugated polymers/oligomers
possess strong light-harvesting abilities which can motivate a
cascade of photochemical reactions. Through radiative
transition from singlet excited state, they display favorable
fluorescence properties with high quantum yields, which makes
them excellent probes for biological imaging and diagno-
sis. 24−26 Meanwhile, the excited triplet state formed by
intersystem crossing can interact with the surrounding oxygen
and other substrates to produce toxic ROS. 27−29 Moreover,
their capacity to serve as photothermal agents has also been
explored, which mainly focus on nonfluorescent polydopamine
(PDA), polyaniline (PANI), polypyrrole (PPy), and poly(3,4-
ethyl-enedioxythiophene) (PEDOT), as well as some con-
jugated polymers/oligomers with a narrow bandgap that
shifted their absorption into the NIR region. 9,30−34 In these
cases, photon energy has been transferred into heat through
nonradiative transition, and the radiative channel and ISC
process are inhibited. Up to now, only a few examples of
conjugated polymers/oligomers have been developed as
photoactive agents integrating both PDT and PTT effects.35,36
Since the radiative transition, singlet-to-triplet intersystem
crossing, and nonradiative transition are the three main decay
processes of a photoexcited fluorophore and are competitive
with one another, how to balance these three processes and
acquire a multifunctional photoactive agent is still a big
challenge.
Herein, a D−A−D type conjugated oligomer was synthe-
sized consisting of two electron-rich cyclopenta-dithiophene
(CDT) units as the donor and electron-deficient boron
dipyrromethene (BODIPY) as the acceptor. Due to its
intramolecular charge transfer and suitable bandgap, CDT-
BODIPY showed a balanced behavior on emission properties,
ROS generation, and photothermal conversion. Dual-wave-
length fluorescence was found for CDT-BODIPY with two
emissions at 463 and 730 nm, respectively, providing easy
imaging-guided therapy. Upon 660 nm laser irradiation, CDT-
BODIPY can efficiently generate ROS, while a moderate
photothermal capability with a conversion efficiency of 33.1%
was observed. Finally, CDT-BODIPY showed a broad-
spectrum biocidal activity toward three kinds of micro-
organisms through a single-wavelength light-triggered combi-
national effect of PDT and PTT.

■ RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The chemical structure of conjugated oligomer is shown in
Figure 1. BODIPY is one popular and attractive fluorophore
because of its outstanding photophysical properties such as
high molar absorption coefficient, excellent photostability,
ROS generation ability, as well as easy structural variation. 40,41
It has also become one popular building block to construct
various photofunctional conjugated polymer-based materi-
als. 42−44 In order to tune the emission of BODIPY from
visible to NIR region, donor−acceptor−donor (D−A−D)
strategy was adopted to narrow the bandgap, where two CDT
units serve as electron donor and BODIPY core acts as
acceptor. CDT segment possesses electron-rich structures due
to the presence of thiophene rings, and it has been widely used
as electron donor to build up various D−A or D−A−D type
fluorophores. 45−48 Besides, easy modification on its C-5

appended at 3 and 5 positions of BODIPY by connecting
through a vinyl π-bridging bond. Upon photoexcitation,
intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) would occur from CDT

an enhanced spin−orbit coupling mechanism, which could
subsequently increase the ROS generation efficiency.53−56
Furthermore, the introduction of quaternary ammonium onto
side chains of CDT was aimed to improve its solubility in an
aqueous environment and provide its binding affinity with
negatively charged membrane of pathogenic bacteria.
Synthesis of CDT-BODIPY. The synthetic route of CDT-
BODIPY is outlined in Scheme 1. BODIPY was conventionally
synthesized via a base-catalyzed condensation reaction using
2,4-dimethylpyrrole as the starting material. Then, its 2,6-
positions were substituted with iodine atoms by iodination
reaction to give rise to BODIPY-I. Extension of the π-
conjugation was accomplished through Knoevenagel con-
densation between the methyl groups at the 3,5-positions of
BODIPY-I and aromatic aldehyde groups of CDT-CHO.57
The chemical structures of the obtained compounds were fully
characterized by 1
H NMR technique and mass spectrometry
(Figures S1−S10 of the Supporting Information, SI). Due to
the presence of quaternary ammonium moieties, CDT-
BODIPY can be readily dissolved in polar solvents like
DMSO and H 2O, leaving a transparent solution with green
color. Owing to its hydrophobic conjugated backbone and
hydrophilic side chains, the amphiphilic CDT-BODIPY likely
formed micelles or aggregates in water, which was proven by
an obvious Tyndall effect in its aqueous solution in Figure S11.
Photophysical Characterization. The systematic photo-
physical characterization of CDT-BODIPY was investigated in
DMSO and H 2O, respectively, as shown in Figure 2, and the
corresponding photophysical data were summarized in Table
1. Since CDT-BODIPY had a less extensive aggregation in
DMSO, studying its DMSO solution would help us better
understand its photophysical properties with lower affinity
from aggregation. For comparison, absorption and emission
spectra of intermediates in the synthesis process were also
measured in dichloromethane and displayed in Figure S12.
After substitution with iodine atoms, the characteristic cyanine-
type absorption peak of BODIPY shifted from 523 to 562 nm,
which can be assigned to the S0-S 1 transition. 58 CDT-BODIPY
showed similar absorption curves in DMSO and H2 O with
three main absorption bands. The former absorption peak
around 329/327 nm was ascribed to CDT units, while the
absorption band in the range of 370−530 nm was attributed to
the π−π* transition of BODIPY. 43 Besides, the broad
absorption covering 540−800 nm with a peak around 640
nm was caused by the ICT band and characteristic absorption
of BODIPY, which showed a significant bathochromic shift of
∼78 nm after conjugation of CDT donors compared with the
parenting BODIPY-I.
Upon excitation with a different wavelength, CDT-BODIPY

to 463 and 730 nm in H 2 O, respectively. The emission peak of
the precursors including CDT-CHO and BODIPY-I in
dichloromethane were located at 435 and 588 nm (Figure
S12). We assumed the blue and red emissions of CDT-

Photodynamic Properties. In order to study the ROS
generation ability of CDT-BODIPY, DCFH was used as a
fluorescent probe for total ROS. Nonfluorescent DCFH could
be oxidized by ROS to fluorescent 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein
(DCF), whose emission intensity at 525 nm shows a
proportional dependency on ROS concentration. 59 Upon
exposure to a 660 nm laser, the fluorescence intensity of
DCF in the presence of CDT-BODIPY ascended quickly and
continuously with irradiation time, displaying a dependency on
both concentration and laser power density (Figure 3a and
3b). L -Ascorbic acid (AA), a commonly used quencher for all
kinds of ROS, was added to confirm if the fluorescence
increment was caused by ROS generation.35,60 As expected, the
addition of AA led to a dramatic reduction in fluorescence
intensity of DCF (Figure 3c), proving the successful
generation of ROS by CDT-BODIPY triggered by light.

Photothermal Conversion Behaviors. To verify the
photothermal conversion ability of CDT-BODIPY, its aqueous
solution with different concentrations were irradiated with a
660 nm laser at a power density of 1.5 W/cm2
. From Figure 4a,
it can be seen that the control water sample showed almost no
temperature elevation (ΔT) during the 12 min irradiation. By
contrast, the temperature of CDT-BODIPY at concentrations
of 0.1 and 0.3 mg/mL increased 10 °C and 18 °C after 12 min
irradiation, respectively, indicating its good photothermal
conversion capability. According to the previous findings, the
obtained temperature elevation is sufficient to kill bacteria.62
Moreover, Figure 4b shows that the temperature increment of
CDT-BODIPY had a close dependency on the laser power
density. By measuring one heating/cooling cycle (Figure 4c),
the photothermal conversion efficiency of CDT-BODIPY was
calculated to be 33.1%. This value was comparable to other
photothermal agents like conjugated polymers, 35,36,63 but
higher than some organic fluorophores like cyanine dyes
having a photothermal conversion efficiency of 26.6%. 35 In
addition, the photothermal stability of the CDT-BODIPY
solution was determined and displayed in Figure S15.

property of CDT-BODIPY, the fluorescence signals could be
collected from blue channel (430 nm ∼530 nm) and red

respectively. Figure 6 showed that both two channels showed
strong fluorescence signals, which were well overlapped with
the microorganisms as in the merged photos. These results
proved that CDT-BODIPY was capable of binding with these
microorganisms efficiently. Due to the positively charged
quaternary ammonium terminated side chains, CDT-BODIPY
could readily bind with the negatively charged cell wall of
microorganisms through electrostatic attractions and hydro-
phobic interactions.63 This close binding was helpful to
enhance the light-activated antibacterial property because it
would promote the efficient diffusion of ROS and heat
generated by CDT-BODIPY into the cell membrane. 27

enhanced ICT of CDT-BODIPY after binding with bio-
macromolecules, which provided a less polar environment than
water. Consequently, its bright blue/red fluorescence provides
a facile way to precisely locate the position of microorganisms,
which would be helpful for fluorescence imaging-guided
treatment.

a moderate dark toxicity toward E. coli and S. aureus, leading to
a bacterial death of less than 50%. This dark toxicity was
mainly caused by quaternary ammonium moieties, which have
intrinsic antibacterial activity through electrostatic binding

inactivated biomacromolecules. Therefore, the microorganisms
were effectively killed by a combinational effect of the
quaternary ammonium, elevated local temperature, and highly
toxic ROS generated by CDT-BODIPY upon light irradiation.
■ CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, a water-soluble BODIPY-based conjugated
oligomer (CDT-BODIPY) with D−A−D structure was
synthesized and applied as an antimicrobial, taking advantage
of its single wavelength-triggered synchronous PDT and PTT
effects. Due to its D−A−D feature, it displayed dual emission
peaks at 463 and 730 nm, respectively, providing a possibility
for fluorescence imaging-guided therapy. Under 660 nm laser
irradiation, CDT-BODIPY absorbed the light and transferred
light to heat with a moderate photothermal conversion
efficiency of 33.1%, meanwhile generating ROS mainly
consisting of singlet oxygen and superoxide anion radical.
Owing to its abundant quaternary ammonium moieties, CDT-
BODIPY can well bind with the three microorganisms
including E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans through electrostatic
interactions. Upon laser treatment, the growth of the three
microorganisms was effectively suppressed by CDT-BODIPY
through a synchronous effect of PDT/PTT, proving its broad-
spectrum biocidal activity. In addition, we believe this D−A−D
type conjugated oligomer is also a potential candidate for a
fluorescence-guided phototherapeutic strategy to treat cancers.
