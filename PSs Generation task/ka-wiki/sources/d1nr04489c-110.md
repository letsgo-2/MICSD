Gadolinium–porphyrin based polymer
nanotheranostics for fluorescence/magnetic
resonance imaging guided photodynamic therapy†
Wandi Chen,‡a Junkai Zhao,‡a Mengfei Hou, a Mo Yang b and Changqing Yi 	*a,c
Nanotheranostics for fluorescence/magnetic resonance (FL/MR) dual-modal imaging guided photo-
dynamic therapy (PDT) are highly desirable in precision and personalized medicine. In this study, a facile
non-covalent electrostatic interaction induced self-assembly strategy is developed to effectively encapsu-
late gadolinium porphyrin (Gd-TCPP) into homogeneous supramolecular nanoparticles (referred to as
Gd-PNPs). Gd-PNPs exhibit the following advantages: (1) excellent FL imaging property, high longitudinal
relaxivity (16.157 mM−1 s−1
), and good singlet oxygen ( 1
O 2) production property; (2) excellent long-term
colloidal stability, dispersity and biocompatibility; and (3) enhanced in vivo FL/MR imaging guided tumor
growth inhibition efficiency for CT 26 tumor-bearing mice. This study provides a new strategy to design
and synthesize metalloporphyrin-based nanotheranostics for imaging-guided cancer therapy with
enhanced theranostic properties.
1. 	Introduction
Cancer remains the leading disease threatening human life
and health. Although great progress has been made, frequently
used cancer therapies in clinical practice, such as surgery,
radiotherapy and chemotherapy, still suffer from relatively low
therapeutic 	efficacy 	and 	serious 	adverse 	effects. 1–5 	For
example, it is still challenging to accurately determine the
resection site, resulting in 90% of cancer recurrences occur-
ring near the resection site.6 In addition, both radiotherapy
and chemotherapy still suffer from the predicament of obser-
vable side effects. 7–9 Among a variety of rapidly developing
treatment modalities, photodynamic therapy (PDT) has proven
to be a safe and effective treatment in preclinical and clinical
studies.10–13 In the PDT process, a photosensitizer (PS) trans-
fers light energy to the surrounding molecular oxygen to gene-
rate toxic radicals, especially reactive oxygen species (ROS,
1 O 2 ), which irreversibly destruct the structure of the cell mem-
brane and result in subsequent cell death.14–16 Thanks to its
features such as minimal invasiveness and high excellent
spatial/temporal controllability, PDT is extensively exploited
for the treatment of cancer, rheumatoid arthritis, etc.12,17–19
Although localized PDT could substantially reduce side
effects, it is still challenging to precisely guide an external
light source to irradiate tumor lesion sites and determine
therapeutic time windows. Therefore, theranostic platforms
that integrate therapies under imaging guidance are becoming
a hot spot in cancer diagnosis and therapy as they can be
designed to realize the precise detection of tumor lesion sites,
effective monitoring of treatment outcomes, and accurate
evaluation of pharmacokinetic effects simultaneously. 20–22
Currently, various clinical imaging methods, such as ultra-
sound (US), magnetic resonance (MR), fluorescence (FL), X-ray
computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography
(PET), and single photon emission CT (SPECT), have been
extensively exploited to visualize physiological and structural
lesion changes. Due to the inherent deficiency of each individ-
ual imaging technique, a difficulty still exists in the collection
of complete information for accurate diagnosis when relying
on a single imaging technique. 23–25 Among all the reported
multimodal imaging techniques, FL/MR dual-modal imaging
has already been successfully implemented in clinical practice
as it can provide complementary information for cancer diag-
nosis and high soft tissue resolution for tumor boundary
detection. 26–32 Therefore, theranostic platforms that integrate
PDT 	under 	FL/MR 	dual-modal 	imaging 	guidance 	have
attracted dramatically growing research interest in recent
years. 33–38 It is indisputable that developing versatile and

As a class of highly efficient PSs, metalloporphyrin mole-

functional theranostic agents as they can serve as not only PSs

translation of metalloporphyrins.
Although it has been well-established that nanomaterials
can not only exhibit the enhanced permeability and retention
(EPR) effect but also transport the metalloporphyrins by
neglecting their intrinsic solubility, 48–52 there is still a lack of
an efficient way to integrate Gd 3+–porphyrin into nanothera-
nostic platforms for FL/MR imaging guided PDT. Self-assembly
is a ubiquitous process in nature and central to the bottom-up
nanotechnology. 33,53–56 It has been reported that porphyrin
building blocks such as Bi–TPPS 4 can be transformed into
ordered nanoplatforms through coordination co-assembly.57
Herein, in this study, a simple but efficient synthetic route is
successfully illustrated to prepare Gd3+–porphyrin based supra-
molecular nanotheranostics (referred to as Gd–PNPs) and the
capabilities of Gd–PNPs are demonstrated for FL/MR imaging
guided PDT using CT 26 tumor-bearing mice (Scheme 1).

2.4. 	Biocompatibility
To evaluate the biocompatibility of Gd–PNPs in vitro, a stan-
dard MTT assay using HeLa cells and CT 26 cells and a hemo-
lysis assay using human blood cells were used to determine
the cytotoxicity and blood compatibility of Gd–PNPs, respect-
ively. In addition, the cellular uptake and in vitro FL imaging
capacities of Gd–PNPs were evaluated using CT 26 cells. In
brief, after being plated in a 35 mm dish with 14 mm bottom
wells (1 × 105 cells per well) and allowed for 12 h of adhesion,
CT 26 cells were incubated with 300 μg mL−1 Gd–PNPs for
different time periods (0, 0.5, 1, 3 and 6 h). Then, the cells
were washed with PBS 3 times, followed by fixation with 4%
paraformaldehyde and consequent 30 min’ staining with DAPI
for cell nuclei. Finally, the cells were washed with cold PBS
and were subsequently subjected to confocal laser scanning
microscopy (CLSM, Olympus FV3000, Japan) for taking FL
images under excitation at 405 nm (for DAPI) and 488 nm (for
TCPP).
Zebrafish and Balb/c mice were used as animal models for
evaluating the biotoxicity of Gd–PNPs in vivo. All experiments
were 	approved 	by 	the 	Animal 	Experimentation 	Ethics
Committee of Sun Yat-Sen University and conducted in accord-
ance with guidelines for human care. Internalization of Gd–
PNPs by zebra fish was evaluated by using a fluorescence inver-
sion microscope system (Olympus IX71, Japan). All the experi-
mental procedures are described in detail elsewhere31,58 and
also in the ESI.†
2.5. 	FL/MR dual-modal imaging
CT 26 tumor-bearing mice were used as animal models for
demonstrating the capabilities of Gd–PNPs for FL/MR dual-
modal imaging. For in vivo FL imaging, CT 26 tumor-bearing
mice were injected with 200 μL of Gd–PNPs (800 μg mL−1 ) or
equivalent TCPP via the tail vein. The FL images were taken at
1, 6, 12 and 24 h after injection using an IVIS Lumina XR
imaging system (PerkinElmer Inc.). The mice were sacrificed at
24 h after injection and the major organs such as heart, lungs,
liver, spleen, and kidneys and the tumors were exteriorized
and then the same instrument was used for recording their
ex vivo FL images. For in vivo MR imaging, the CT 26 tumor-
bearing 	mice 	were 	injected 	with 	200 	μL 	of 	Gd–PNPs
(0.05 mmol Gd3+ kg−1 ) or Gadobutrol (0.05 mmol Gd 3+ kg−1 )
via the tail vein. Then, the MR images were taken at 1, 3, 6, 9
and 12 h after injection using a 1.5 T clinical MR instrument
(SIEMENS).

added to 2 mL of different concentrations of Gd–PNP solution

spectrometer (Beckman) to record their absorbance at 440 nm.

cells were irradiated for 10 min with a 635 nm laser at a power
density of 25 mW cm−2 and the cells without laser irradiation
were used as the control group. Subsequently, after another
12 h of incubation in the dark, the cell viabilities were
measured using a standard MTT assay.

3. 	Results and discussion
3.1. 	Synthesis and characterization
In order to efficiently integrate diagnosis and therapy, simple
and versatile synthetic routes for the preparation of nanothera-
nostic platforms with enhanced performance are still highly
desirable. In this study, a supramolecular nanotheranostic
platform was facilely synthesized for FL/MR imaging guided
PDT. Scheme 1 shows the synthetic route of Gd–PNPs. TCPP, a
typical porphyrin molecule, is selected as the initial building
block for the synthesis of a multifunctional nanotheranostic
platform in which TCPP serves a crucial function as the chelat-
ing agent for Gd3+ . To this end, Gd 3+ , which serves as a T1 -MR
contrast agent, was chelated to TCPP to prepare Gd–TCPP
firstly. TCPP is an excellent chelator for many metal ions such
as Cu 2+ , 39 Gd3+ , 41 and Mn 2+60 because of its tetrapyrrole struc-
ture. Fig. S2A† shows the chemical structures of TCPP and Gd–
TCPP. It has been reported that Gd3+ -chelated porphyrins have
an out-of-plane structure as a relatively large ionic radius.61
The successful preparation of Gd–TCPP could be validated by
its UV-visible spectra. As shown in Fig. S2B,† TCPP exhibited
four Q bands (absorption peaks at 516, 551, 590 and 645 nm),
while Gd–TCPP exhibited only two Q bands (absorption peaks
at 559 and 596 nm). Gouterman’s four orbital model can be
used to explain the decrease in the number of Q bands after
the formation of Gd–TCPP. 45 The formation of the out-of-plane
structure in Gd–TCPP resulted in a slight red-shift of the Soret
band, as compared with TCPP. 62 In addition, the 1 H NMR
spectra also suggested the successful synthesis of the key
building block, Gd–TCPP (Fig. S1A†).
Then, the negatively charged Gd–TCPP was mixed with the
positively charged supermolecule PEI to form supramolecular
nanotheranostics by non-covalent electrostatic interaction-
induced self-assembly. The mass ratios of PEI/TCPP and reac-
tion time were optimized at first (Fig. S2C and S2D†). Finally,
in order to minimize the possible surface charge-induced bio-
toxicity, the negatively charged mPEG–PGA was electrostatically
adsorbed onto the surface of the as-prepared supramolecular
nanotheranostics to prepare the final product, Gd–PNPs. As
designed, after the electrostatic adsorption with mPEG–PGA,
the surface potential of Gd–PNPs substantially dropped from
+50.3 	mV 	to 	−0.1 	mV. 	The 	feed 	mass 	ratio 	of
Gd : TCPP : PEI : mPEG–PGA was optimized to 8 : 4 : 2 : 1.6 for
the synthesis of Gd–PNPs.
The TEM images showed the well dispersed spheres of Gd–
PNPs with an average diameter of ∼200 nm (Fig. 1A and B),
which was consistent with the DLS measurement (inset of
Fig. 1A). The XPS spectra validated the successful synthesis of
Gd–PNPs by presenting characteristic Gd peaks including Gd
4d5/2 (167.9 eV), Gd 4d 3/2 (153.3 eV) (Fig. 1C and D) and Gd
3d5/2 (1226.4 eV) (Fig. 1C and E). The coexistence of both the
Gd 4d and Gd 3d peaks confirmed the presence of Gd3+ on the
surface of Gd–PNPs, thus verifying its capability as a T1 -MR

3.2. 	FL and MR properties
The maximum excitation/emission peaks of Gd–TCPP and Gd–
PNPs were ∼430/650 nm and ∼430/666 nm (Fig. 2A), respect-
ively. The Gd–TCPP and Gd–PNPs exhibited almost the same
excitation and emission curves, also validating the successful
encapsulation of Gd–TCPP in Gd–PNPs. In addition, the emis-
sion of Gd–PNPs exhibited an obvious redshift of ∼16 nm
versus Gd–TCPP, which might be attributed to the stronger
polarity of PEI than H 2 O. 31,63,64 Notably, after being encapsu-
lated with PEI, the FL lifetime of Gd–TCPP significantly
increased from 0.71 to 8.88 ns (Fig. S4†). The severely
restricted molecular motion of Gd–TCPP after encapsulation
and its substantially weakened stretching and bonding
vibrations might contribute to its significant FL lifetime
increase. In addition, the FL quantum yields (QYs) of TCPP,
Gd–TCPP and Gd–PNPs were determined to be 7.84%, 8.05%
and 7.25%, respectively. This result suggested that not only the
chelation of Gd3+ onto TCPP but also the encapsulation of Gd–
TCPP into Gd–PNPs did not cause obvious FL quenching.
The presence of Gd3+ on the surface of Gd–PNPs endowed
them with MR imaging capability, as evidenced by the obvious
concentration-dependent 	positive 	contrast 	enhancement
exhibited in the gray-scale T1 -weighted MR images of Gd–PNP
dispersions (Fig. 2B). From the linear regression curve between
the longitudinal relaxivity and equivalent Gd concentration of
Gd–PNPs, the r1 value was calculated to be 16.157 mM−1 s−1 .
The Gd–PNPs are believed to be T1 -contrast agents because the
ratio of r2 /r1 was calculated to be 1.16.65 Notably, Gd–PNPs
exhibited significantly higher r1 values than most commer-
cially available T1 -weighted MR contrast agents including
Gadobutrol (2.5 mM−1 s−1 ), Gadovist 	(4.34 mM−1 s−1 ),
Magnevists (3.2 mM−1 s−1 ), Omniscans (3.3 mM−1 s−1 ), and
ProHance (4.3 mM−1 s−1 ). The higher r1 value means less
dosage of contrast agents and thus fewer side effects.
3.3. 	Biocompatibility and biotoxicity
The results from the Gd 3+ leakage assay in which xylenol
orange was used as a free Gd3+ indicator, revealed that only a
negligible amount of free Gd3+ was released from Gd–PNPs for
up to 48 h incubation (Fig. S5A†). This result confirmed the
good chemical stability of Gd–PNPs and also suggested good
biocompatibility. As anticipated, the MTT assay confirmed that
the viabilities of both HeLa cells and CT 26 cells were over
88% even upon treatments with 400 μg mL−1 Gd–PNPs
(Fig. 3A). The hemolytic potential of Gd–PNPs was evaluated
using human red blood cells and the results revealed excellent
blood compatibility as evidenced by the only ∼5.6% hemolytic
efficiency of Gd–PNPs at a concentration of up to 400 μg mL−1
(Fig. 3B). In addition, the micrograph also confirmed that the
red blood cells remained intact even upon treatment with
400 μg mL−1 Gd–PNPs (Fig. S5B†). It is also worthy mentioning
that Gd–PNPs exhibited good colloidal stability in FBS as evi-
denced by no obvious size change and no observable precipi-
tation for up to 48 h (Fig. S6†).
As an animal with a short generation time, fast develop-
ment, low-cost maintenance and similarities with humans,
zebrafish is an excellent animal model for biotoxicity evalu-
ation.66 As shown in Fig. 3C, the development of zebrafish was
not affected by Gd–PNPs as the zebrafish exhibited similar
morphologies at different stages of development no matter
whether they were treated with Gd–PNPs or not. In addition,
no lesion or teratogenesis was observed in Gd–PNP-treated
zebrafish. The calculated survival rate and hatching rate also
validated the low biotoxicity of Gd–PNPs, as evidenced by the
over 90% survival and hatching rates even upon treatments
with 400 μg mL−1 Gd–PNPs (Fig. S7A and S7B†). More impor-
tantly, an H&E staining assay confirmed that all major organs
of Gd–PNP-treated mice, such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs
and kidneys, exhibited no observable organ damage or inflam-
mation (Fig. 3D). All the above results confirmed that Gd–
PNPs caused no obvious side effects to the examined animals,

3.4. 	Dual-modal imaging
The bright FL and high r1 value (Fig. 2) make Gd–PNPs an
effective probe for FL/MR dual-modal imaging which might
provide complementary functional and anatomical infor-
mation for precise diagnosis and therapy of tumors. As
expected, Gd–PNPs efficiently accumulated not only in the cell
cytoplasm but also in the nucleus (Fig. 4A), possibly due to the
surface amine group-aroused endosomolytic effects. 67 Fig. 4A
clearly shows that the cellular uptake process was time depen-
dent, as evidenced by the increasing intracellular red fluo-
rescence intensity along with time. In addition, the intact cell
morphology also validated the good biocompatibility of Gd–
PNPs.
Fig. 4B clearly shows that bright red fluorescence could be
observed at the yolk sac of zebrafish, demonstrating the suc-
cessful internalization of Gd–PNPs by zebrafish without indu-
cing noticeable lesions. This result correlated well with a pre-
vious report that nanoparticles can enter into zebrafish
through swallowing behavior and/or the yolk membrane. The
in vivo FL imaging capability of Gd–PNPs was also demon-
strated using CT 26 tumor-bearing mice as the model. The
time-dependent biodistribution of Gd–TCPP and Gd–PNPs in
mice was measured at pre-designed time points (1, 6, 12, and
24 h) after intravenous injection. As shown in Fig. 4C and D,
time-dependent accumulation of Gd–PNPs in tumors was
clearly observed, where the strongest FL signal was detected
after 12 h of intravenous injection and remained detectable
after 24 h. In comparison, Gd–TCPP largely accumulated in
the tumor 1 h after injection and gradually diminished with
time until 12 h. Compared to Gd–TCPP-treated mice, ∼1.6×
stronger FL signals were observed by Gd–PNP-treated mice. In
addition, the ex vivo FL images of excised tumors and organs
at 24 h after injection revealed that the strongest FL signal was
observed from the tumors of Gd–PNP treated mice, rather than
the metabolic organs, such as the kidneys, spleen and liver. In
comparison, the liver of Gd–TCPP treated mice had the stron-
gest FL signal.
In vivo FL imaging revealed the high tumor uptake of Gd–
PNPs; hence, it is reasonable to expect that Gd–PNPs would
also have outstanding T1 -MR imaging capability due to their
high r1 value. As shown in Fig. 4E, a time-dependent positive
contrast enhancement of the MR signal at tumor sites could
be observed with bare eyes for both Gd–PNP-treated mice and
Gadobutrol-treated 	mice. 	Specifically, 	for 	Gd–PNP-treated
mice, the strongest MR signal with a sharp border to delineate
the clear outline of the tumor was observed after 6 h of intrave-
nous injection, while Gadobutrol-treated mice exhibited the
strongest MR signal after 1 h of intravenous injection. This
observation is different from FL imaging in which the stron-
gest FL signal could only be observed at 12 h after injection.
The poorer tissue penetration capability of FL imaging than
MR imaging should be responsible for this difference; there-
fore, it might take a longer time to accumulate enough Gd–
PNPs in tumors to generate strong enough FL signals. In
addition, different tissue penetration properties of MR and FL
imaging resulted in their different enhancement effects at the
same time point. Since the FL signal is partially attenuated
during the process of tissue penetration, the collected FL
signal intensity should be lower than the real value. Therefore,
a substantially stronger enhancement effect was observed for
the MR signal than the FL signal in vivo (Fig. 4D and F). The

correlated well with the bare eye observation. Notably, Gd–

4. 	Conclusions
In summary, a simple and robust synthetic route is success-
fully 	demonstrated 	to 	prepare 	metalloporphyrin-based
nanotheranostics for FL and T1 -MR imaging guided PDT. The
multifunctional nanotheranostics Gd–PNPs are assembled
from Gd–TCPP, PEI, and PGA-g-mPEG layer by layer. Notably,
Gd–TCPP serves triple functions in Gd–PNPs as an FL imaging
probe, MR contrast agent, and PS for PDT. This facile non-
covalent electrostatic interaction-induced self-assembly strat-
egy simplifies nanotheranostic synthesis and improves col-
loidal stability and dispersity. Cellular PDT experiments con-
firmed that Gd–PNPs exhibit good tumor cell ablation efficacy.
The in vivo study further demonstrated that Gd–PNPs showed
excellent biocompatibility, efficient tumor accumulation and
long retention time at tumor sites, thus facilitating FL/MR
imaging guided PDT in CT 26 tumor-bearing mice with
enhanced theranostic properties. These metalloporphyrin-
based nanotheranostics might provide a new stimulus to
design other multifunctional nanoplatforms for more precise
and effective cancer theranostics.
