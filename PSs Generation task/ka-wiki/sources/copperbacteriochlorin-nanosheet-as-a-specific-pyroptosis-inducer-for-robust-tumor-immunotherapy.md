Copper-Bacteriochlorin Nanosheet as a Specific Pyroptosis
Inducer for Robust Tumor Immunotherapy
Yunxiu Zhang, Qingyan Jia,* Jian Li, Jian Wang, Ke Liang, Xiaokuang Xue, Tiejin Chen,
Lin Kong, Haohui Ren, Weimin Liu, Pengfei Wang,* and Jiechao Ge*
Pyroptosis is increasingly considered a new weathervane in cancer immune
therapy. However, triggering specific pyroptotic tumor cell death while
preserving normal cells still remains a major challenge. Herein, a brand-new
pyroptosis inducer, copper-bacteriochlorin nanosheet (Cu-TBB), is designed.
The synthesized Cu-TBB can be activated to an “on” state in the tumor
microenvironment with glutathione (GSH) overexpression, leading to the
release of Cu+ and TBB, respectively. Intriguingly, the released Cu+ can drive
cascade reactions to produce O 2−• and highly toxic ·OH in cells. Additionally,
the released TBB can also generate O2−• and 1 O2 upon 750 nm laser
irradiation. Encouragingly, both Cu+-driven cascade reactions and
photodynamic therapy pathways result in potent pyroptosis along with
dendritic cell maturation and T cell priming, thus simultaneously eliminating
the primary tumors and inhibiting the distant tumor growth and metastases.
Conclusively, the well-designed Cu-TBB nanosheet is shown to trigger specific
pyroptosis in vitro and in vivo, leading to enhanced tumor immunogenicity
and antitumor efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects.

1. Introduction
Pyroptosis, a new type of immunogenic cell
death (ICD), is characterized by the for-
mation of transmembrane pores, cellular
swelling, and lysis, which can subsequently
lead to the release of inflammatory factors
and intracellular contents. [1] Pyroptosis has
been increasingly considered a new weath-
ervane in cancer immune therapy because
it can trigger inflammatory response via ac-
tivating intracellular proinflammatory fac-
tors (e.g., interleukin-1𝛽 (IL-1𝛽), IL-18).[2]
Currently, chemotherapeutic drugs and in-
organic nanoparticles have been reported
to promote pyroptosis.[3] However, the non-
specific activation of pyroptotic cell death
caused by these therapeutic strategies may
lead to adverse effects within the body.
Therefore, it is urgent to develop new pyrop-
tosis inducers that can selectively eliminate
tumor cells while sparing normal cells.[4]
Spatiotemporal selective photodynamic
therapy (PDT) is a clinically approved
antitumor treatment with precise controllability, minimal inva-
siveness, and no drug resistance. It utilizes light and photosen-
sitizers (PSs) to induce the production of harmful reactive oxy-
gen species (ROS) via either the electron transfer process (type
I PDT) or the energy transfer process (type II PDT), resulting
in tumor cell death.[5] Previous studies have demonstrated that
ROS generated by PDT can stimulate an antitumor immune re-
sponse. However, PDT primarily relies on the apoptosis pathway,
which has limited immunogenicity.[6] Currently, combining PDT
with immune checkpoint blockade agents such as programmed
cell death protein ligand-1 and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated
antigen 4 is a conventional approach to achieve robust antitumor
immunity; however, this inevitably results in high costs and seri-
ous side effects. [3e,7] To overcome these limitations, membrane-
anchored aggregation-induced emission PSs have recently been
successfully designed for the activation of pyroptosis.[8] Sub-
sequently, a carbonic anhydrase IX anchored rhenium (I) PS
named CA-Re was reported as a metal complex-based pyroptosis
inducer to enhance antitumor immunity. [9] Moreover, traditional
PSs used in PDT are indiscriminately distributed in both tumor
and normal tissues, leading to undesired photosensitive dam-
age to normal tissues due to their constant activation. Recently,
Wang et al. made significant progress in cancer therapy by de-
veloping an acid-activatable nanoPS library that enables tunable

Metallic copper (Cu), as the cofactor of redox-active copper-
dependent enzymes, plays a crucial role in various biological
processes.[10] It is increasingly evident that glutathione (GSH)
could mediate the reduction of Cu2+ to produce the redox-active
Cu+ species, which in turn catalyzes the conversion of molecu-
lar oxygen (O2 ) into the superoxide anion (O2−•).[11] Moreover,
the depletion of GSH induced by Cu2+ has been demonstrated
to exacerbate oxidative stress in cancer cells, thereby augment-
ing its antitumor effect. [12] Based on the aforementioned fact, we
postulate that Cu 2+/PS nanoaggregates with high stability may
be formed under normal physiological conditions by the coor-
dination of Cu 2+ with appropriate PSs. Due to the Forster reso-
nance energy transfer between Cu2+ and PSs, such nanoaggre-
gates would exhibit quenched fluorescence of PS and incapacity
for ROS production upon light irradiation. However, in a tumor
microenvironment (TME) where GSH is overexpressed at con-
centrations of 1–10 mm,[13] the Cu-based nanoassembly would
undergo disassembly due to the reaction between Cu 2+ and GSH,
leading to the specific release of Cu+ and PSs within tumors
rather than normal tissues. Consequently, upon light exposure,
the coexistence of Cu+ catalysis and PDT may trigger ROS pro-
duction, leading to selective pyroptosis of cancer cells. Therefore,
it is highly desirable to develop such nanoassembly with satisfac-
tory specificity toward pyroptosis outcomes.
In this study, we successfully designed a bacteriochlorin-based
nanosheet named Cu-TBB, consisting of Cu2+ and 5,10,15,20-
tetra(p-benzoato) bacteriochlorin (TBB), which is a natural pho-
tosensitizer derivative (Scheme 1a). The Cu-TBB was at an “off”
state (inactivated) in normal cells. However, in tumors with high
levels of GSH, it could be activated to the “on” state due to the
disassembly of Cu-TBB, resulting in the specific release of Cu+
and TBB. Subsequently, the released Cu+ could catalyze the con-
version of endogenous O 2 to O2−•. Meanwhile, the formed O2−•
could further undergo superoxide dismutase (SOD) dispropor-
tionation and Haber–Weiss reaction to produce O2 for recycling
and highly toxic ·OH. Beyond that, the “switching-on” TBB could
generate both O2−• and 1 O2 upon 750 nm light irradiation. Dur-

2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Synthesis and Characterization
5,10,15,20-Tetra (p-benzoato)-porphyrin (TCPP), a traditional PS,
is restricted in the current PDT study due to its limited absorp-
tion wavelength at ≈300–450 nm. For the treatment of deep-
seated tumors, PSs with longer wavelengths are required. In
this study, p-toluenesulfonyl hydrazide was employed to reduce
TCPP to form TBB (Figure S1, Supporting Information). [14] The
UV–vis spectrum (Figure S2, Supporting Information) showed
that the obtained TBB exhibited a distinct absorption peak at
≈750 nm, which is an optimal wavelength for tissue penetra-
tion. Subsequently, Cu-TBB was synthesized via solvothermal re-
action in DMF at 70 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere using the
synthesized TBB as the precursor. The physico-chemical prop-
erties of Cu-TBB were then analyzed. The prepared Cu-TBB ex-
hibited a typical 2D nanosheet morphology with a thickness of
≈1.0 nm, as revealed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
(Figure 1a) and atomic force microscopy (Figure S3, Support-
ing Information) images. The elemental mapping revealed that
Cu, N, and C elements were homogeneously distributed in the
Cu-TBB nanosheets (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Power
X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirmed that Cu-TBB was
highly crystalline due to its sharp diffraction peaks (Figure 1b). [15]
The wide range of scans of X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS) demonstrated the successful introduction of Cu into TBB,
as evidenced by Figure S5 (Supporting Information). The bind-
ing energy and satellite structure of Cu2p spectra revealed the
presence of Cu2+ (Figure 1c).[16] Besides, the surface O element
of Cu-TBB exhibited a significantly higher content of C═O com-
pared to that of C─O/OOH due to the successful coordination be-
tween Cu 2+ with the ─COOH group in TBB (Figure S6, Support-
ing Information). The FTIR analysis further confirmed this coor-
dination by revealing the typical stretching mode of O=C─O─Cu
at 1401 and 1610 cm−1 (Figure 1d). [17] Additionally, the peak ob-
served at 1000 cm−1 was assigned to Cu─N stretching of the Cu-
TBB, indicating that Cu2+ is coordinated in the center of TBB. [18]
Compared to TBB, Cu-TBB exhibited a slight redshift in its UV–
vis absorption (Figure 1e), and its fluorescence (Figure S7, Sup-
porting Information) was completely quenched due to the Forster
resonance energy transfer resulting from the overlay between the
broad spectral absorption of Cu2+ (Figure S8, Supporting Infor-
mation) and emission of TBB. [19] In addition, the dynamic light
scattering analysis of Cu-TBB revealed an average size of 193 nm
and a zeta potential of −24.7 mV, which are beneficial to the en-
docytosis of Cu-TBB by tumor cells (Figures S9,S10, Supporting
Information). Furtherly, the size of Cu-TBB remained unchanged
for one month, indicating excellent stability (Figure S9, Support-
ing Information).
Given that the co-occurrence of Cu 2+ and GSH can enhance
the pro-oxidant effect through metal-reducing action, we inves-
tigated the interaction between Cu-TBB and GSH.[11] First, we
examined the UV–vis absorption spectrum of Cu-TBB in the
presence of GSH. As shown in Figure 1f, the steady redshift
of absorption from 375 to 385 nm was observed due to the S-
Cu 2+ charge transfer, indicating the reaction between Cu-TBB
and GSH, resulting in the formation of Cu+. Additionally, af-
ter a reaction time of 30 min, the characteristic absorption of
glutathione disulfide (GSSG) at 254 nm was clearly observed as
shown in Figure 1f insert, suggesting the formation of GSSG
(Reaction 1). [11c] The ROS generation capacity of Cu-TBB was
subsequently assessed using electron spin resonance (ESR) spec-
troscopy. Figure 1g shows no detectable signal when only Cu-TBB
is present, while the clear 1:1:1:1 quaternate signal of O 2−• was
observed after incubating Cu-TBB with GSH for 30 min, indi-
cating the formation of O 2−• through the reaction between Cu+
and O2 (Reaction 2). These findings revealed that Cu-TBB acti-
vated by GSH can stimulate O 2−• production through Cu catal-
ysis, suggesting its potential for targeted elimination of tumor
cells in response to the high levels of GSH present in the TME.

Next, we evaluated the impact of GSH on fluorescence and
ROS generation of Cu-TBB upon laser irradiation. As shown in
Figure 1h, the quenched fluorescence of TBB obviously recovered
after reacting with GSH and gradually increased as the concen-
tration of GSH rose from 0 to 10 mm. We subsequently mea-
sured the ROS production capability of Cu-TBB by using 1,3-
diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF), a ROS trapping agent. As shown
in Figure 1i, without GSH present, DPBF’s absorbance barely
changed even upon laser irradiation, indicating the incapability
of Cu-TBB to generate ROS. However, under 750 nm laser irra-
diation for 5 minutes, the generation of ROS increased as GSH
concentration rose from 0.5 to 2 mm, possibly due to the en-
hanced release of TBB. Notably, complete degradation of DPBF
occurred at a GSH concentration of 2 mm. In contrast, at a GSH
concentration of 5 mm, excess ROS was scavenged by GSH re-
sulting in decreased degradation of DPBF. Previous studies have
demonstrated that TBB with both type I and II photodynamic
capabilities can generate 1 O2 and O2−•.[20] In this study, specific
agents including 9,10-anthracenediyl-bis(methylene) dimalonic
acid (ABDA) and dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR123) were utilized
to detect the generation of 1 O2 and O2−•, respectively. The excel-
lent ability of GSH-activated Cu-TBB to generate 1 O2 and O2−•
was confirmed by the rapid degradation of ABDA (Figure S11,
Supporting Information) and the increasing fluorescence inten-
sity of DHR123 (Figure S12, Supporting Information). TEM anal-
ysis revealed that the gradual destruction and disassembly of the
Cu-TBB after incubation with GSH (Figure 1j). These findings
demonstrate that the presence of GSH can activate Cu-TBB from
“off” states to “on” states, as indicated by the restored fluores-
cence and enhanced ROS production induced by Cu catalysis and
PDT.

2.2. In Vitro ROS Production
To analyze the ROS production by GSH-activated Cu-TBB in
vitro, we first used 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid), a GSH
detection agent, to quantify the GSH concentrations in HeLa,
HepG2, and 4T1 cells.[12] Our findings revealed that the lev-
els of intracellular GSH were not significantly different among
HeLa, HepG2, and 4T1 cells with only 10 μm, which was lower
than those reported in cancer cells (1–10 mm) (Figure S13, Sup-
porting Information). To mimic GSH overexpression in TME,
GSH was supplemented to the cell culture medium in subse-
quent experiments. In the absence of GSH, Cu-TBB fluores-
cence signals were not detected in HeLa cells, as depicted in
Figure 2a (upper panel). However, with increasing concentra-
tions of GSH from 1 to 10 mm, red fluorescence gradually in-
tensified, and strong red fluorescence was observed at 10 mm
of GSH. This indicates that Cu-TBB fluorescence was activated
by GSH. These findings were consistent with the results ob-
tained in the solution and further confirmed in HepG2 and 4T1
cells (Figures S14 and S15, Supporting Information). Notably,
co-incubation of Cu-TBB and GSH induced significant changes
in cell morphology, which became more pronounced with in-
creasing GSH concentration (Figure 2a, lower panel). However,
no discernible alteration in cell morphology was observed when
treated with GSH alone (Figure S16, Supporting Information).
The results suggest that co-incubation of Cu-TBB with GSH
could induce cellular damage. Subsequently, the methyl thiazolyl
tetrazolium (MTT) assay was conducted to quantitatively assess
cell viability. As depicted in Figure 2b, GSH alone did not ex-
hibit any toxicity toward cells even at a concentration of 10 mm.
However, when combined with Cu-TBB, cell viability decreased
significantly.
To verify the O2−• generation in vitro, we conducted a dihy-
droethidium (DHE) staining assay. As shown in Figure 2c, PBS,
GSH, and Cu-TBB groups exhibited weak intensities of red flu-
orescence due to the presence of endogenous O 2−•; however,
strong red fluorescence was observed in the Cu-TBB + GSH
group, indicating the production of O 2−•. The production of O2−•
in HeLa cells was also investigated under hypoxia conditions
(Figure 2d). Surprisingly, clear DHE fluorescence and cell mor-
phology damage were observed, indicating that Cu-TBB + GSH
could induce the generation of O2−• in a hypoxia environment.
This may be due to the fact that the generated O2−• could be fur-
ther catalyzed by endogenous SOD disproportionation (Reaction
(3)) and Haber–Weiss reaction (Reaction (4)) to produce O2 , thus
relieving hypoxia. [4b,21]

It is known that intracellular redox balance is essential for
maintaining normal cell metabolism. Initially, to verify the H 2 O2
generation in HeLa cells after treatment with GSH, we monitored
the intracellular H2 O2 changes using H 2 O2 -specific probes. As
shown in Figure 2e, clear green fluorescence was observed at a
concentration of 10 mm GSH, indicating the production of H2 O2
within HeLa cells to maintain their redox homeostasis. There-
fore, following GSH stimulation, the production of H 2 O2 not
only enabled Haber–Weiss reaction (Reaction 4) proceed to allevi-
ate cancer cell hypoxia, but also would trigger the Fenton-like re-
action (Reaction 5) to produce highly toxic ·OH. Subsequently, we
employed hydroxyphenyl fluorescein (HPF), a specific probe for
·OH, to detect its level in cells. As shown in Figure 2f, the bright
green fluorescence was exclusively observed in Cu-TBB + GSH
group, indicating that ·OH generation resulted from both Haber–
Weiss and Fenton-like reactions. Finally, we evaluated intracellu-
lar ROS levels by measuring malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker
of oxidative stress. [22] The results demonstrated a significant in-
crease in MDA content with increasing GSH concentration from
1 to 10 mm (Figure 2g), further confirming substantial ROS pro-
duction.
In brief, as shown in Figure 2h, upon entering cancer cells,
Cu-TBB underwent disassembly through the initial reaction of
Cu 2+ and GSH to form Cu+ (path 1). Subsequently, the pro-
duction of O 2−• and Cu 2+ was induced by the redox reac-
tion between Cu+ and intracellular O2 (path 2), while simul-
taneously inducing Fenton-like reactions that generate Cu2+
and ·OH (path 3). Finally, the produced O2−• was converted
to O2 through the endogenous SOD disproportionation, while
also undergoing Haber–Weiss reaction with H 2 O2 to gener-
ate ·OH and O2 (paths 4 and 5). Therefore, Cu 2+-driven cas-
cade reactions in cancer cells can produce a large amount of
ROS.
Given the generation of O 2−• and 1 O2 by Cu-TBB in solution
upon laser irradiation, the PDT capacity was further explored
in vitro using DHE and singlet oxygen sensor green (SOSG) as
specific fluorescent probes for detecting O 2−•and 1 O2 , respec-
tively. As shown in Figure 2i, without laser irradiation, red flu-
orescence signals were observed due to the generation of O 2−•
via Cu 2+-driven cascade reactions. Under laser irradiation, more
O2−• was generated via TBB leading to stronger red fluorescence
signals. Moreover, following laser irradiation, there was obvious
green fluorescence with SOSG as 1 O2 probe only in the Cu-
TBB + GSH + Laser group (Figure S17, Supporting Informa-
tion), indicating sufficient 1 O2 production of TBB. The aforemen-
tioned results demonstrated that both Cu2+-driven cascade reac-
tions and PDT pathways could induce abundant ROS (1 O2 , O2−•,
and ·OH) generation, exhibiting great potential for the targeted
killing of cancer cells.
2.3. In Vitro ROS Generation Triggered Pyroptosis
The MTT assay was employed to evaluate the cytotoxicity of
Cu-TBB on cancer cells, including HeLa, HepG2, and 4T1 cells.
As shown in Figure 3a–c, there was no apparent cytotoxicity
observed in the only Cu-TBB group, indicating excellent biocom-
patibility of Cu-TBB. However, when cancer cells were treated
with both Cu-TBB and GSH (10 mm), their viabilities were
significantly reduced due to the production of ROS through
Cu 2+-driven cascade reactions. Furthermore, upon 750 nm
laser irradiation, ≈90% mortality was achieved due to increased
ROS generation. The anticancer efficacy in vitro was further
confirmed by the Calcein-AM/PI assay. Consistent with MTT
results, no significant cytotoxicity was observed in the PBS,

(LDH), high mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1), and calreti-

and Cu-TBB + GSH + Laser groups exhibited a significant in-

2.4. Tumor-Specific Fluorescence Imaging and Therapy In Vivo
The in vivo fluorescence imaging of Cu-TBB was investigated us-
ing BALB/c nude mice bearing 4T1 tumors. Cu-TBB was intra-
venously injected and fluorescence imaging was then carried out.
At 3 h post-injection, a detectable fluorescence was observed in
tumor tissues (Figure 4a). The intensity of fluorescence gradually
increased over time and reached its peak at 48 h post-injection.
Meanwhile, the changes in fluorescence intensity were moni-
tored in various organs, including the spleen, liver, lung, pan-
creas, muscle, kidney, heart, skin, caecum, and tumor to ana-
lyze the distribution of Cu-TBB. Because of the very low GSH
concentration (2–40 μm) in blood, the fluorescence signals of
blood were almost invisible. [25] As shown in Figure S20 (Support-
ing Information), the spleen, pancreas, muscle, heart, skin, and
lung showed negligible fluorescence with a tiny organ/blood ra-
tio (OBR). This may be attributed to that the tight interendothe-
lial connections for normal vascular systems could prevent Cu-
TBB nanosheets from leaking into tissues. [26] Liver as the largest
reticuloendothelial phagocytosis system is capable of phagocy-
tose foreign bacteria, dyes, and other nanoparticles from the
bloodstream.[27] Thus, there was always some degree of fluores-
cence observed in the liver due to the partial disassembly of Cu-
TBB. However, no significant differences were observed in liver
function indicators such as alanine aminotransferase, aspartate
transaminase, and alkaline phosphatase compared to the PBS
group (Figure S21, Supporting Information), illustrating mini-
mal hepatotoxicity of Cu-TBB. Meanwhile, the kidney exhibited
weak fluorescence and demonstrated comparable levels of albu-
min and urea nitrogen to those observed in the PBS group. For
the tumor, the OBR was continually increasing due to the ele-
vated GSH concentration and enhanced permeability and reten-
tion effect, demonstrating the remarkable tumor-targeting ability
of Cu-TBB. These findings suggest that the fluorescence of TBB
could be switched from an “off” to “on” state in response to over-
expressed GSH within TME, thereby demonstrating the selective
activation of Cu-TBB in tumor tissues while ensuring low toxicity
to normal tissues and organs.

2.5. Antitumor Immune Responses
The induction of tumor pyroptosis by Cu-TBB in vivo was eval-
uated through the detection of related biomarkers. As shown
in Figure 5a,b, compared with the control groups, the expres-
sions of N-GSDMD and cleaved caspase-1 were upregulated in
the Cu-TBB group and further promoted in the Cu-TBB + Laser
group. Additionally, the expression levels of pyroptosis-associated
cytokines IL-1𝛽 and IL-18 were also upregulated in both Cu-TBB
and Cu-TBB + Laser groups (Figure 5c,d). The results collectively
demonstrate that the in vivo occurrence of GSDMD-mediated py-
roptosis induced by Cu-TBB. To evaluate the activation of ICD
by pyroptosis in vivo, we further examined the expression of
CRT and extracellular release of HMGB1, which are the typical
hallmarks of ICD. [29] The expression of CRT in tumor tissues
was quantified by immunofluorescence. As shown in Figure 5e,
there was a significant increase in CRT expression observed in
the Cu-TBB group; however, this increase was even more pro-
nounced in the Cu-TBB + Laser group. Moreover, the serum level
of HMGB1 was examined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent
assay. The findings indicated that Cu-TBB + Laser group exhib-
ited a higher serum level of HMGB1 compared to the Cu-TBB
group (Figure 5f). These results validated that pyroptosis medi-
ated the antitumor immune effect of Cu-TBB in vivo, which is
consistent with in vitro outcomes.
It is well-established that the expression of CRT, which is
the “eat me” signal, can activate DCs to phagocytize dead tu-
mor cells. Meanwhile, the release of HMGB1 and IL-1𝛽 from
the damaged cells can stimulate DC maturation, and the prim-
ing of T cells.[30] Therefore, we employed flow cytometry to de-
tect changes in DC populations following different treatments.
As shown in Figure 5g,h, the proportions of mature DCs in the
spleen of mice treated in Cu-TBB + Laser and Cu-TBB alone were
≈62.6% and 54.9%, respectively, which were significantly higher
than that observed in PBS-treated mice. Cytotoxic T lymphocytes
(CD8+ T cells) and helper T cells (CD4+ T cells), as key players
in tumor immunity, are crucial for adaptive immune responses.
As shown in Figure 5i–k, the percentages of CD8+ T cells were
≈16.2% and 10.4%, while those of CD4+ T cells were ≈28.3%
and 19.5% in the spleens of mice treated with Cu-TBB + Laser
or Cu-TBB alone, respectively, which was significantly higher
than that observed in control groups. Finally, immunohistochem-
istry (IHC) analysis was performed to assess the infiltration of
CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues. Compared with the PBS
group, the Cu-TBB, and Cu-TBB + Laser groups exhibited more
pronounced infiltration of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells (Figure 5l).
The intricate interplay between pyroptosis and immune re-
sponses is expounded in Figure 5m. A pyroptosis is a crucial
form of ICD that involves the release of DAMPs, such as CRT,
LDH, and HMGB1, as well as intracellular contents, thereby ac-
tivating a potent inflammatory response. These immunogenic
signaling molecules can be captured by DCs for processing and
presentation to T cells. Meanwhile, DCs are capable of express-
ing co-stimulatory signals such as CD80 and CD86, which can
provide secondary signaling for T cell activation. The interactions
between T cells and DCs may lead to the priming and subsequent
activation of T cells. Once activated, these tumor-specific T cells
can migrate to the site of the tumor, and then CD8+ cytotoxic T-
lymphocytes will specifically recognize and destroy the malignant
cells. Collectively, these results suggested that the pytoptosis in-
duced by Cu-TBB could promote the release of proinflammatory
cytokines, the maturation of DCs, and the infiltration of T cells,
and trigger an adaptive immune response in vivo.
The potential in vivo toxicity of Cu-TBB was evaluated, and no
significant changes were observed in the body weights of mice
(Figure S25, Supporting Information). Additionally, H&E stain-
ing results revealed that Cu-TBB did not induce any damage
to normal organs (Figure S26, Supporting Information). These
findings collectively suggest that Cu-TBB has excellent biocom-
patibility as an antitumor agent.

of GSH overexpression, Cu-TBB can be transformed into an
“on” state, thereby inducing fluorescence recovery of TBB and
ROS production through both Cu2+-driven cascade reactions and
