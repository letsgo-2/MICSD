1. Introduction
Pyroptosis has been regarded as a non-apoptotic and inflammatory
manner of cell death, and the characteristics of pyroptosis was defined as
the rupture of the plasma membrane and the release of cell contents, a
process that arisen from an activation of the inflammatory response
(Taabazuing et al., 2017). It was well known that inflammasomes played
an important role in responding to the environmental toxicants received
by 	damage-associated 	molecular 	patterns 	(DAMPs) 	or
pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) (Lamkanfi and Dixit,
2014). As ASC and Caspase-1 were recruited, the wheel-shaped NLRP3
inflammasomes were gradually assembled. Following the enhancement
of cleaved Caspase-1, the N-terminal of Gasdermin D (GSDMD) was
activated and accumulated to drill holes in the phospholipids of the cell
membrane. Withal the IL-1β and IL-18 were activated by cleaved
Caspase-1 and the cellular content released due to the impairment of cell
membrane integrity (Bergsbaken et al., 2009; Shi et al., 2015). Previous
investigations have shown potential correlation between nanoparticles
and pyroptosis. Carbon black nanoparticles have been proved to induce
primary macrophages inflammatory death as defined by pyroptosis. And
the inflammasomes were formed with the growth of the cleaved
Caspase-1, followed by the increased level of IL-1β (Reisetter et al.,
2011). Moreover, the pyroptosis of nerve cell lines stimulated by Stober
silica nanoparticles has been testified. The investigation found that
Stober silica nanoparticles caused production of pyroptosis in N9 cells,
with the hallmarks of the generated GSDMD -NT and the leakage of IL-1β
(Du et al., 2019). Noteworthily, the application of metal oxide nano-
particles was also capable of triggering pyroptosis. The mice exposed to
indium-tin-oxide nanoparticles through intraperitoneal inoculation,
displayed peritoneal macrophages pyroptosis and this death pathway
was NLRP3-ASC-Caspase-1 complex-dependent. Further, IL-1β and
TNF-α secretion were also enhanced (Naji et al., 2016). These findings
provide insights to the relationship between pyroptosis and nanotech-
nology, further confirmed the ability of nanoparticles to mediate the
pyroptosis signal pathway in vivo and in vitro.
Metal or metal oxide modified nanoparticles were the most prolific
nanomaterials in the global industry (Wang and Tang, 2021). Particu-
larly, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) have been widely utilized in
the daily necessities, clinical diagnosis, drug delivery and agricultural
production industries due to their unique functions, such as excellent
catalytic activity, and antibacterial property (Swain et al., 2016).
However, the progressive exposure to ZnO NPs through a variety of
novel pathways may pose a great threat to the ecosystem and public
health (Chang et al., 2012; Vandebriel and De Jong, 2012). Increasing
reports exited regarding the toxicity of ZnO NPs including pulmonary
toxicity, neurotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxi-
city (Li et al., 2020; Singh, 2019; Xiao et al., 2016). According to the
literatures, these consequences were sensitive to generation of oxidative
stress induced by ZnO NPs. Remarkably, despite the lack of direct evi-
dence, pyroptosis was also a possible mechanism of ZnO NPs toxicity. At
the dose of 80 μg/mL, ZnO NPs significantly reduced the survival rate of
BV-2 microglia, accompanied by the occurrence of ROS accumulation
and mitochondrial damage. However, Annexin V/PI did not exhibit any
apoptotic signal (Annexin V+/PI  cells) during the cell death, and the
results of western blot assay denied the high expression of PARP and
Caspase-3. Instead of this, Annexin V+ /PI+ signal accounted for about
68.2 ± 6.7% of the cells, which showed plasma membrane injury,
confirming the absence of apoptosis in cell death (Sruthi et al., 2021).
Surprisingly, this non-apoptotic cell death that involved in ZnO NPs also
has been explained in early experiments. The type II alveolar epithelial
cells (A549) cultured in vitro were stimulated with 20 μg/mL ZnO NPs,
and thus up-regulated the ROS accumulation and IL-1β release, the
expression level of intracellular Caspase-1 was also elevated, along with
the inflammatory death (Song et al., 2013). Even though this indirect
evidence predates the definition of pyroptosis, it was enough to predict
the potential activation of pyroptosis by ZnO NPs, along with the ROS
generation.
Normally, humans and animals were exposed to ZnO NPs in clinical
diagnosis, drug delivery, daily necessities and agricultural supplies (Cao
et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2016). Therefore, dermal, inhalation, injection
and gastrointestinal tract were all regarded as important exposure route
for ZnO NPs (Cao et al., 2016; Liang et al., 2018). With the gradual
understanding on the tissue distribution of ZnO NPs, it was determined
that liver was one of the important target organ of ZnO NPs accumula-
tion (Baek et al., 2012; Kielbik et al., 2017; Yu and Choi, 2021). In a
recent study, 5-week-old SD rats were given 20 nM ZnO NPs orally for 90
days, the different doses were set at 500, 250 and 125 mg/kg, respec-
tively. The results indicated that concentration of Zn in liver increased in
a dose-dependent manner (Park et al., 2014). Another pharmacokinetic
study confirmed that the liver of Wistar rat was the site where the ZnO
NPs was initially accumulated, reaching the highest level at 30 min after
treatment. While for continuous administration, the peak accumulation
was observed on day 7 (Watson et al., 2015). In addition to gathering in
the liver, ZnO NPs could contribute to the cytotoxicity and tissue injury
mainly through oxidative stress and apoptosis. ZnO NPs have been
demonstrated to significantly increase Malondialdehyde (MDA)
expression, down-regulate antioxidant index Catalase (CAT) and
Glutathione (GSH) in liver, further strengthen damage parameters in
DNA comet assay (Kermanizadeh et al., 2012, 2014). Furthermore, after
treatment with ZnO NPs (15 μg/mL) in HepG2 cells for 4 h, The ROS
production was enhanced, the cell viability was reduced and the
p53-mediated apoptosis was promoted. In the treatment group, the
mRNA expression of Bax and enzyme activity of caspase-3 were both
up-regulated, while the Bcl-2 expression was down-regulated (Akhtar
et al., 2012). Moreover, the liver inflammation response was also acti-
vated by ZnO NPs significantly. Consequently, the JNK/p38 and STAT-3
signal pathways were shown to be involved in regulating ZnO NPs
toxicity (Aboulhoda et al., 2020). In addition, the secretion of IL-8 by
C3A hepatocytes was significantly increased (Kermanizadeh et al.,
2012). The excessive ZnO NPs could exceed the payloads range of liver
cells, which further initiated the death process, depending on the
recognition function of toll-like receptors (TLRs) and the outbreak of
inflammation. It was also found that liver macrophages interacted with
2.5 μg/mL ZnO NPs for 24 h promoted the up-regulation of activation
markers including CD1D and MHC-II, TLR6/MyD88 and MAPK path-
ways, which as a result, more IL-1β and TNF-α were secreted into the
supernatant (Roy et al., 2014). Therefore, the oxidative stress and cell
inflammatory death played crucial roles in the hepatotoxicity related to
ZnO NPs. However, to date, whether pyroptosis was involved in the
toxicity of ZnO NPs in animals or cells remains unclear, and the effect of

In our study, SD rats and HepG2 cell lines were used for confirming
the ZnO NPs-induced hepatocyte oxidative stress and pyroptosis. For
pyroptosis, the variation in inflammasomes, pyroptosis related proteins
and membrane perforation were all measured. Other auxiliary in-

induced by pyroptosis were also all observed to verify the development
of cell pyroptosis comprehensively. We selected a specific inhibitor of
caspase-1 to determine the dependence of ZnO NPs-induced hepatocyte
pyroptosis on the Caspase-1 classical cell pyroptosis route. Furthermore,
we focused on reversing the ZnO NPs-induced oxidative stress damage
and pyroptosis through the antioxidant effect of N-Acetyl-L-Cysteine
(NAC). To summarize, ZnO NPs accelerated caspase-1 activation by
assembling inflammasomes, which led to the aggregation of cleaved
GSDMD and the completion of its perforation action. This pyroptosis
process was alleviate by universal antioxidant NAC. These new findings
have revealed a new form of hepatocyte death induced by ZnO NPs in
liver and indicated a potential protection strategy. The clues in this
paper refresh the cognition related to the toxicity mechanism of ZnO
NPs, and thus provided data support for their safety monitoring and
rational application.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Chemicals and reagents
The Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (high glucose)
and Fetal bovine serum were acquired from Gibco (Grand Island, NY,
USA), other cell culture reagents were provided by Solarbio Science &
Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was
purchased from AMRESCO Inc. (Solon, OH, USA). Phenylmethylsulfonyl
fluoride (PMSF), TBST solution, Sodium dodecyl sulfonate (SDS)-PAGE
solution system, QuickBlock™ Blocking Buffer and Enhanced chem-
iluminescence (ECL) system were acquired from Beyotime Institute of
Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Haimen, China). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), 4′,
6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) were obtained from AMRESCO Inc.
(Solon, OH, USA). Formalin Fix Solution (10%) and Gluta Special fixing
solution for electron microscopy were purchased from Solarbio Science
& Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Sodium carboxymethylcellu-
lose (SCMC) was provided by Aladdin Company (Shanghai, China).
Antibody of NF-κB, HO-1, Nrf2, HMGB1, NLRP3, ASC, Caspase-1,
GSDMD, IL-1β and IL-18 were obtained from Cell Signaling Technol-
ogy (Beverly, MA, USA). Other chemical reagents used in this research
were of analytical grade.
2.2. Particles characterization and zinc content measurement
The powder of ZnO NPs (CAS NO. 1314–13–2, purity > 97%) were
purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Saint Louis, MO, USA), the specific
surface area of ZnO NPs sample was 41.632 (m2/g). Transmission
electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi-7500, Japan) was used to observe
the morphology, primary particle size and the level of agglomeration.
The Raman spectrum (Portable_Rama_Spectrometer Nanobase, Korea)
procedure were conducted for identifying the crystalline nature and the
availability of the ZnO NPs. The hydrodynamic size, the degree of
polymerization and the separate coefficient of ZnO NPs suspension in
MilliQ water were evaluated by dynamic light scattering (DLS-Wyatt
NanoStar, Wyatt, USA), and the zeta potential of ZnO NPs was deter-
mined by using Zeta Potential Analyzer (Nano-ZS, Malvern, UK). In
addition, the flame atomic absorption spectroscopy (FAAS) (Thermo-
fisher Scientific iCE3000, MA, USA) was used to confirm the trans-
formation and stability of ZnO NPs in cell culture medium at different
time, different pH and different additive amount, as well as the accu-
mulated Zn element content in cells and different tissues in rats, setting
the parameters of the system determine the zinc atomic-related oper-
ating instructions. In the above scenarios, prepare the ZnO NPs freshly
and sonicated the solution with an ultrasound equipment (Yamato,
Japan) until a homogeneous suspension was obtained.
2.3. Animals handling and exposure
Twenty-four 7–8-week-old Healthy Sprague-Dawley rats from Lab-
oratory Animal, Shanghai, China, weighing 200–220 g, were all
randomly assigned in 2 animals/cage maintained under a 12 h light/
dark cycle. The rats were bred under specific controlled conditions
(temperature of 23 ℃  26 ℃ and humidity of 50%  60%), free to ac-
quire feed and tap water. After a week of acclimatization, the rats were
randomly divided into four groups (n = 6) and received intraperitone-
ally injection with the ZnO NPs suspension (0.1%SCMC as solvent) at a
dose of 12.5, 25, 50 mg/kg BW for 7 consecutive days. Before each in-
jection, the suspension should be prepared on the spot and ultrasonized
for 20 min in advance. To avoid the possibility of ascites, rats were
intraperitoneally injected below 1.5 mL of suspension at a time, and the
body weight of the rats were also considered. The one remaining group
of rats were treated with 0.1% SCMC as a control. During the treatment
period, the survival parameters of the rats was observed daily, including
body size, mental status, and feed intake. After 12 h of fasting and water
deprivation, the rats were prepared for the blood and serum examina-
tion, followed by sacrifice and autopsy. Tissues such as the heart, liver,
spleen, kidneys, lung and brain were all collected immediately and
stored according to the purpose of the measurement. All procedures
were permitted by Laboratory Animal Management and Ethics Com-
mittee of China Agricultural University and made it our basic principle
to reduce the pain of the rats.
2.4. Histopathological examination
The liver tissues were separated from the rats and rinsed with 0.9%
saline that had been precooled at 4 ℃. The uncontaminated tissue blocks
were then immersed in 10% Formalin Fix Solution (Solarbio Science &
Technology, Beijing, China) for 2 days. Briefly, the liver samples were
put out and washed by MilliQ water, subsequently, the sample was
dehydrated by alcohol at a series certain concentration, followed by
paraffin wax embedding and preparation of 4 μm histological slides. The
collected sections were dried in laboratory oven and then subjected to a
concentration gradient of xylene for dewaxing, followed with hydration
and washing. Thereafter, the specimen was subjected to a process of
hematoxylin staining, ethanol differentiation, washing, eosin staining
and another washing. After the final soak with alcohol and xylene,
pathological observation was completed by using an optical microscope
(Leica Microsystems, German).
2.5. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
The liver tissue blocks and HepG2 cells were fixed by 2.5% glutar-
aldehyde solution (Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China) for 4
h, then were rinsed three times with PBS, followed by treatment with 1%
osmium tetroxide for 1 h. Then, 55%, 75%, and 95% alcohol were used
to dehydrate the tissue in sequence for 10 min each. Subsequently, the
specimens were embedded overnight in Epon812 agent. Next day, the
tissue was dried at 37 ℃. The ultra-thin sectioning (60 nm) was treated
with lead citrate and uranyl acetate (4%) for 20 min each. Observations
of ultrastructure was made under transmission electron microscope with
the magnification at the range of 8000–10,000 × (Hitachi, Japan).
2.6. Hematology and serum biochemistry
The partial blood samples were from all rats collected and trans-
ferred to the heparin lithium blood collection tubes (BD Vacutainer, NJ,
USA), respectively. These samples were transported to the animal hos-
pital of China Agricultural University within 30 min for hematology test.
While, the rest of the blood samples in each group were set aside in

2.7. Tissue immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence assay
The ZnO NPs- mediated hepatotoxicity mechanism was identified
through tissue immunohistochemical assay. The activation level of the
inflammasome in the liver sections was evaluated by examining the
expression of the NLRP3 and ASC proteins. The anti- IL-1β and anti- IL-
18 was also selected to label the inflammatory cytokines involved in
hepatocyte pyroptosis signaling pathway. Positive areas marked in
brown in the photographs were visualized under an inverted microscope
(Leica, German) and the intensity of the hyperchromatic positive signals
were compared by Image lab (National Institutes of Health, USA).
Immunofluorescence assay was used to assist in the further verifi-
cation of rat hepatocytes pyroptosis. Briefly, the pre-prepared liver
sections were labeled overnight with the antibodies against IL-1β and
GSDMD, respectively. For TUNEL examing, the processing time was
adjusted to 1 h. After incubation, the sections were soaked by fluores-
cent secondary antibodies of corresponding species, followed with DAPI
staining. Using a touch-screen fluorescent microscope (Leica, Germany),
the immunostaining fluorescence intensity was captured. The expres-
sion degree of the IL-1β, GSDMD or the TUNEL positive rate was count
and converted in Image lab software.
2.8. Cell lines and treatment
The HepG2 cell line was given by Center for Disease Control and
Prevention (Tianjin, China), and was further applied to reflect the
lethality of ZnO NPs in our study, and the high glucose-DMEM (Gibco,
USA) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin and streptomycin was
used as the cell culture medium. The HepG2 cells were kept in an
incubator which was set to 37 ◦C and 5% CO2. The DMEM was used as a
solvent for obtaining pretreated ZnO NPs: the ZnO NPs were prepared at
the gradient concentrations of 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 μg/mL for HepG2
cells treatment.
2.9. Cell viability and LDH detection
Cell survival rate and LDH release were detected based on previous
study (Pei et al., 2021). HepG2 cells were subcultured in 100 μL DMEM
and transferred into a 96-well plate to reach a density of 1 × 104
cells/well. After incubation overnight at 37 ◦C with 5% CO2, the me-
dium of each well was removed, then the wells were added with 100 μL
DMEM containing ZnO NPs suspension ranged from 5 to 25 μg/mL and
maintained for 24 h. The DMEM containing 0.1% DMSO (AMRESCO,
Solon, OH, USA) was used as a control reagent in our all study in vitro.
Thereafter, the supernatant was replaced by 10% CCK-8 solution
(AMRESCO, Solon, OH, USA) and the cells were subsequently incubated
at 37 ℃ for 1 h. Next, the absorbance was measured through a micro-
plate reader (Biotek, USA) at 450 nm. The secretion of Lactate dehy-
drogenase (LDH) was determined by LDH ELISA kit (Nanjing Jiancheng,
Nanjing, China) based on the concentration of extracellular LDH leaked
out from the damaged hepatocyte. After treatment with ZnO NPs, the
medium obtained by centrifugation (1500 rpm, 10 min) and the level of
LDH was subsequently detected according to the description of
instruction.
2.10. Western bolting
To extract intracellular proteins, HepG2 cells were scraped from 6-
well plate, and the liver tissue was isolated, frozen and pounded into a
homogenate. Both of the above products were lysed into delicate mucus
and sonicated in an ultrasonic processor (Sinap Tec, France). Then, the
products were collected through refrigerated centrifugation for 10 min.
Thereafter, the loading buffer (5 ×) was diluted 5 times by extracted
protein solution and the mixture was denatured during the 10-min
boiling process. After the above procedure, protein solution (40 μg of
each group) was separated in the lanes of 10% or 12% (SDS)-PAGE so-
lution system (Beyotime, Haimen, China), and then electro-blotted onto
nitrocellulose membrane (Millipore, USA) for 40 min. Next, Quick-
Block™ Blocking Buffer (Beyotime, Haimen, China) was used for
blocking the membrane which was subsequently immersed in TBST on
table concentrator for 3 times. Then, the target primary antibodies (Cell
Signaling Technology Beverly, MA, USA) against required proteins were
prepared for binding to the membrane. After overnight incubation, the
membrane was reacted with secondary antibody for 60 min. Then, the
ECL signals of protein expression were evaluated using Image lab (Na-
tional Institutes of Health, USA) after rinsing and being soaked by
Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) solution (Beyotime, Haimen,
China).
2.11. Measurement of intracellular oxidative stress
The cellular ROS generation in the HepG2 cells was measured via the
DCFH-DA assay. In brief, after incubation with 10, 15 and 20 μg/mL ZnO
NPs, the cells were treated with DCFH-DA probe (10 μM) for 20 min at
37 ◦C (avoid light). Afterwards, the ROS production was observed in
terms of the green fluorescence under a fluorescent microscope (Thermo
fisher, USA). For assessing the levels of the oxidative stress indicators in
HepG2 cells and liver tissue, the corresponding detection kits (Solarbio
Life Sciences, China) were used to base on the merchant manual. In
brief, the HepG2 cells and tissue were stimulated with ZnO NPs,
respectively. Thereafter, cells and tissue homogenate were processed
into supernatants, followed by evaluation of superoxide dismutase
(SOD), catalase (CAT), malondialdehyde (MDA) and glutathione (GSH).
2.12. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
The HepG2 cells were exposed to ZnO NPs on the wafers for 24 h,
followed by incubation with primary fixative, a mixture of glutaralde-
hyde and formaldehyde. After 1 h, the cell samples were rinsing by PBS
solution and the wafers were post-fixed in OsO4 that was diluted with
deionized water. After 1.5 h immersion, the wafers were subjected to a
sequential ethanol at an incremental concentration of 20%, 40%, 60%,
80% and 100%, followed with desiccation in a critical-point dryer. Af-
terwards, the samples were coated by a ion sputtered layer and mounted
on a SEM sample holder. The morphology the cell surface was photo-
graphed by SEM-Hitachi S-4100 (Tokyo, Japan).
2.13. Flow cytometry
Annexin V-FITC/PI staining unite with flow cytometer were used to
assist in proving ZnO NPs-triggered pyroptosis. After the work stage that
the HepG2 cells were stimulated by ZnO NPs and digested with trypsin
that contained no EDTA (Beyotime, Haimen, China), the cells subse-
quently reacted with the Annexin V-FITC/PI Double Stain Kit (Vazyme
Biotech, Nanjing, China) in the light of the manufacturer’s protocol.
Briefly, the HepG2 cells were stained with the probe solution (2 μg/mL).
The fragmentation of the plasma membrane in pyroptosis cells was
embodied through flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA).

2.16. Statistical analysis
Data analysis was accomplished through one-way anova in SPSS
software (SPSS 13.0, USA) and Prism 5.0 software (GraphPad, USA). The
evaluation in each group was conducted for more than three times. The
mean ± standard deviation (SD) was used for the data expression. The
values of p less than 0.05 was considered as the statistically significant
level.

3.2. Bioaccumulation of ZnO NPs in rat tissues
The bioaccumulation of zinc element in the heart, liver, spleen,
kidney, lung and brain of the rats in the high-dose group (50 mg/kg) and
the control group was quantitatively evaluated by using a FAAS assay
after 7-day administration. As a result, ZnO NPs disrupted zinc ho-
meostasis in rat tissues, in view of the higher concentration of zinc
compared with the control. Furthermore, it must be emphasized that the
liver was the primary target organ of Zn penetration induced by ZnO
NPs, as evidenced by the most distribution of zinc in the liver among all
the organs. The concentration sequence of zinc in different organ sam-
ples of high-dose group were shown below: liver > spleen > kidney
> lung > heart > brain, the corresponding values were 104.96, 67.21,
60.14, 45.73, 32.37, 18.52, respectively (Fig. 2). These findings verified
that the vast majority of ZnO NPs specifically targeted the liver via the
rat peritoneal capillary.
3.3. ZnO NPs induced inflammatory damage and oxidative stress in rat
liver
Over the course of 7 days，the side effects-related signs of the rats
were observed. The rats in the high-dose group presented with loose
stools, decreased food consumption, weight loss and a reduction in
organ coefficient. As showed in Table 1, the routine blood inspection
indexes of rats treated with ZnO NPs at medium-dose and high-dose
were obviously abnormal compared with the control, including the
increased level of white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil (NEU),
monocyte (MONO) and lymphocyte (LYMPH). Furthermore, the
abnormal hepatic architecture in pretreated group was determined, as
shown in the medium dose and high dose groups in Fig. 3A, the
arrangement of the hepatocytes was disordered, the cytoplasm pre-
sented vacuolate degeneration, the portal vein was congested and the
congestion and extravasated, as well as a number of infiltrating in-
flammatory cells and tissue calcification appeared. In addition,
compared with the control group, the microstructure of liver cells was
significantly abnormal, which was manifested as nuclear pyknosis,
mitochondrial swelling, mitochondrial crest fracture and disappearance
(Fig. 3B). Although the liver sections implied liver inflammatory injury,
more evidences in clinical markers were necessary to further support
this judgment. The serum biochemical parameters in serum of ZnO NPs
treated rats were observed, indicating that the content of ALT, AST and
γ-GT were significantly higher than that in the control (Fig. 3C-E). In
addition, the oxidative stress level was also confirmed, featured with
increased generation of MDA and the down-regulated SOD, GSH and
CAT (Fig. 3F-I). The above information suggested that the inflammatory
damage and oxidative stress was induced in rat liver exposed to ZnO
NPs.
3.4. ZnO NPs induced pyroptosis in rat liver
The pyroptosis pathway triggered by ZnO NPs was assessed by
western blot, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence. The re-
sults of western blotting demonstrated that the NLRP3, ASC, Cleaved
Caspase-1 and GSDMD-NT proteins were all up-regulated, as well as the
significantly high expression of the IL-1β and IL-18 cytokines, compared
to the control group (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the NLRP3 inflammasome
and inflammatory cytokines were activated and was involved in the liver
damage, as evidenced by the strengthened hyperchromatic region rep-
resenting the expression of NLRP3, ASC, IL-1β and IL-18 in the immu-
nohistochemistry assay (Fig. 4B). It was also explored that the
immunofluorescent proportion of IL-1β positive cells was elevated
obviously and the expression of GSDMD-NT was increased (Fig. 4D-E).
Similar to apoptosis, the DNA damage was also a property of pyroptosis
process. Therefore, we measured the nucleic acid fragmentation and
found that the TUNEL-DAPI positive fluorescent signal was remarkably
enhanced (Fig. 4C). All above results suggested that the representative
pyroptosis pathway played a pathogenic role in liver damage caused by
ZnO NPs.
3.5. ZnO NPs induced Zn intracellular accumulation and cytotoxicity
As shown in Fig. 5, the rapid accumulation of total Zn element in
HepG2 cells in a dose-dependent manner was found through the FAAS
data (Fig. 5A). Meanwhile, the undissolved nanoparticles appeared in
cytoplasm of HepG2 cells under exposure to ZnO NPs at a concentration
of 20 μg/mL (Fig. 5B). Although stability tests mentioned above showed
that the Zn ions released from ZnO NPs (12–30 μg/mL) were almost
constant over different time and certain pH ranges, the concentration of
intracellular Zn2+ was significantly increased characterized by the
enhancement of fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5C), indicating that the ZnO
NPs may gradually convert into ions in acidic lysosomes. Together, it
proved that the zinc accumulation was induced by ZnO NPs in different
forms, and the toxic effects of ZnO NPs on HepG2 cells were related to
the properties of nanoparticles besides the chemical action of ions. These
evidences predicted that both nanoparticles taken up into acidic lyso-
somes and Zn ions liberated from the nanoparticles in the cytoplasm
were probably essential for pyroptosis. For the investigation of the
cytotoxicity induced by ZnO NPs, the absorbance obtained by CCK-8 was
recorded and the microscopic state of culture dish was observed
(Fig. 5D-E). The viable cell count was reduced in a time and dose
dependent manner, and the working middle concentration was set to be
20 μg/mL by the rule of close to the 50% inhibitive concentration.
HepG2 cells treated with higher concentrations of ZnO NPs (15, 20 and
25 μg/mL) for 24 h exhibited significant morphological abnormalities,
which were characteristics of increase of dead cells, such as cell
swelling, detachment from neighboring cells and reduced cell density.

3.6. Effect of ZnO NPs on oxidative stress-related indexes in hepatocyte
The increased ROS generation in HepG2 cells stimulated by ZnO NPs
was demonstrated by DCFH-DA (Fig. 6A). According to the enhanced
green fluorescence, the ROS accumulation was promoted by ZnO NPs in
a dose dependent manner. As the antioxidant markers, the CAT and SOD
were both significantly down-regulated after treatment by 15 and
20 μg/mL ZnO NPs (Fig. 6B and C). The level of MDA was found to rise
significantly, as shown in Fig. 6 D. In addition, both the Nrf2 and HO-1

ZnO NPs ≥ 15 μg/mL (Fig. 6E). The results verified that the ZnO NPs
triggered oxidative stress through suppressing the antioxidant level.
3.7. Effect of ZnO NPs on inflammatory response and NLRP3
inflammasome activation in hepatocyte
NF-κB and HMGB1 mediators have been known as the important
mediators of cellular inflammatory injury. Here we found that the
expression of HMGB1 was significantly increased by 15 μg/mL ZnO NPs,
and the NF-κB was up-regulated by 20 μg/mL ZnO NPs (Fig. 7A-B).
Inflammasomes, especially the NLRP3 inflammatory complex, played a
critical role in cell pyroptosis and inflammation. Our western blotting
results demonstrated that NLRP3 and ASC were significantly upregu-
lated and the cleaved Caspase-1 expression was enhanced in a dose and
time-dependent manner (Fig. 7A-B). In addition, the fact of inflamma-
some activation was also confirmed by immunofluorescence assay. The
positive rate and fluorescence intensity of inflammasome co-localization
of NLRP3/Caspase-1 and ASC were strongly increased (Fig. 7C-D). In
brief, the ZnO NPs awakened the assembly of inflammasomes in the
process of triggering hepatocellular inflammation.
3.8. ZnO NPs induced GSDMD pore formation and hepatocyte pyroptosis
According to the fact that cleaved Caspase-1 was possible to drive
pyroptosis through GSDMD cleavage, we further detected the expression
level of GSDMD-NT, IL-1β and IL-18 and all the results showed sub-
stantial increases, with a time and dose -dependent trend (Fig. 8A-B).
Furthermore, as auxiliary markers of cytoplasmic membrane rupture
induced by pyroptosis, the LDH release and AV-PI double-uptake were
measured at 10,15, 20μg/mL dosage of ZnO NPs. The exposure to ZnO
NPs resulted in a rise of LDH outflow, and the AV-PI double-positive
proportion was obviously increased (similar to late apoptosis) (Fig. 8C-
D). In the microstructural photos of the HepG2 cells, the cell membrane
showed typical characteristics of pyroptosis due to the impacts of the
ZnO NPs. Notably, the cell became swollen, the plasma membrane
structure was punched. The number of plasma membrane protrusions
and external vesicles decreased, replaced by homogeneous small holes
on the membrane surface (Fig. 8E). To verify another auxiliary index,
pyroptosis-mediated DNA damage, the TUNEL-DAPI probe was used to
evaluate DNA strand breakage under ZnO NP stress. It showed that the
red fluorescent spot was obscure in the control, while the red fluorescent
intensity and proportion were overtly enhanced, as described in Fig. 8F.
Collectively, hepatocyte damage induced by ZnO NPs was associated
with the initiation of pyroptosis. (Although the above indexes were
partly similar to apoptosis, but they were all subordinate to pyroptosis
indexes).
3.9. Caspase-1 was involved in regulation of ZnO NPs-induced hepatocyte
pyroptosis
Apart from morphology, the most significant difference between
pyroptosis and apoptosis is the dependence of inflammatory caspase,
especially caspase-1. The VX765 was used as an inhibiter to define the
importance of Caspase-1 in pyroptosis triggered by ZnO NPs. The 10 μM
was selected as the inhibitory dose in the light of observing the grayscale
value of the protein bands. After VX-765 intervention, the expression
index of cleaved Caspase-1 of GSDMD-NT was down-regulated, whereas
the GSDMD-FL didn’t alter too much, as shown in Fig. 9A, Furthermore,
the secretion of mature IL-1β and IL-18 were also diminished obviously.
Additionally, the cell membrane was more intact with the decreased
round pores and the recovered plasma membrane protrusions and
external vesicles under scanning electron microscope (Fig. 9B), which
was further confirmed by the decreased proportion of LDH leakage and
reduced number of AV-PI double positive cells (Fig. 9C-D). Likewise,
TUNEL positive rate represented DNA fragments was also reduced due to
the suppression of Caspase-1 (Fig. 9E). In addition, our result provided
evidence that VX-765 prevented ZnO NPs-induced cell death (Fig. 9F).
Furthermore, the positive rate of inflammasome co-localization of
Caspase-1 and ASC were slightly reduced (Fig. 9G). On the whole,
Caspase-1 played a crucial role in ZnO NPs-mediated hepatocyte
pyroptosis, that is, the caspase-1-dependent canonical pyroptosis.

4. Discussion
ZnO NPs have been gradually used in daily necessities, clinical
diagnosis, drug delivery and agricultural production due to their effects
of biocompatibility, drug delivery, sterilization and nutritional
enhancement (Mohd Yusof et al., 2019). Although ZnO NPs were more
effective than traditional Zn sources in terms of application field, there
were still some defects behind their advantages. The teratogenic,
mutagenic and carcinogenic potential of ZnO NPs in animals has been
explored, and the toxic damage to animal organs and human cells has
also been confirmed (Kumar and Dhawan, 2013; Pan et al., 2010; Yan
et al., 2020). The most important reason of choosing to explore ZnO NPs
rather than other Zn salt compounds was more serious toxic effects of
ZnO NPs compared with traditional Zn sources due to their special
physicochemical properties of nanoparticles, including size, shape,

Although ZnO NPs have been used in skin care and medical in-
dustries for a long time, they were still listed as innovative technology in
pharmaceutical and agriculture. According to previous survey, despite
being regard as the premise of auxiliary sterilization, the oxidative stress
and excessive immune stimulation brought by ZnO NPs could cause
irreversible consequences such as pyroptosis. Fortunately, we found that
ZnO NPs-induced pyroptosis was restricted after boosting the antioxi-
dant function with NAC, this prepared us to deal with the side effects of
ZnO NPs-induced pyroptosis. According to previous reports, the mech-
anism ZnO NPs induced oxidative stress was probably related to the
photocatalysis of ZnO NPs, the O2- generated by ZnO NPs penetration
and NOX-dependent ROS generation induced by ZnO NPs (Liu et al.,
2016; Sruthi et al., 2018). Furthermore, MAPK and NF-κB pathways
followed with elevated oxidative stress was shown to be a key signal for
subsequent inflammasome activation (Li et al., 2021; Teng et al., 2020;
Xie et al., 2020). Not confined to ZnO NPs, our finding provided a basis
for the safety evaluation and preventive solution of other nanoparticle
pollutants. Nevertheless, the evaluation results provided by our labo-
ratory should be more complete. First, the future study should be
extended to the comparison of the toxic effects caused by ZnO NPs from
different sources to find the essence of ZnO NPs toxicity with different
physicochemical properties (size, shape and surface). Besides, in addi-
tion to understanding the rescue of oxidative stress on pyroptotic
damage, the further evaluation will be also necessary for screening of
natural antioxidants. Since the toxicity of ZnO NPs was related to the
mode and duration of administration, the future antioxidants test should
be used in a long-term experiment through different administration
including inhalation, oral and skin contact to acquire the comprehensive
protection mechanism, Thirdly, in addition to clarifying liver damage,
the oxidative stress and pyroptosis induced by ZnO NPs should also be
evaluated in other organs in the future animal models, as Zn may have
different toxic mechanism in different tissues and the conservation tar-
gets could be altered. At present, our new findings have revealed a new
form of hepatocyte death under exposure of ZnO NPs, and opened a door
for the future prevention of ZnO NPs-induced pyroptosis through anti-
oxidation, a potential protection strategy.
In conclusion, our study has found a potential association between
ZnO NPs and the hepatocyte pyroptosis. The inflammatory injury of rat
liver, the assembly of inflammasome and the activation of the typical
caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis pathway induced by ZnO NPs in vivo
and in vitro was also confirmed. Moreover, we revealed the ROS gen-
eration and the elimination of antioxidant capacity induced by ZnO NPs,
and further clarified the protection of antioxidation against pyroptosis
damage (Fig. 11). These clues refreshed the cognition related to the
toxicity mechanism of ZnO NPs. More importantly, our discovery in this
paper is expected to provide data support for future risk assessment of
ZnO NPs and their therapeutic target exploration for the clinical treat-
ment related to pyroptosis.
